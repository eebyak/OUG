# Pattern Pair 4: Resource Allocation and Incentive Design

**The Year-End Flush → The Need-Based Allocation**

*Derived from: HEI-2 — End-of-year budget mobilisation documented as a routine coordination task requiring a dedicated working group meeting cycle.*

## AP-4 · Antipattern: The Year-End Flush

**Context — where this appears**
Public institutions operating under annual budget cycles in which unspent funds are returned to central administration at period end rather than carried forward to the following year. Affects every level of the institution simultaneously, from individual programme budgets to faculty discretionary funds.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Spending accelerates in the final weeks of the budget period regardless of institutional need. Procurement decisions made in November and December are driven by the question "what can we spend this budget on?" rather than "what does the institution need?" Staff responsible for budget management develop strong end-of-year mobilisation skills. The phenomenon is known, named, managed — and never questioned as a structural problem.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Annual budget cycles in public institutions create an asymmetric incentive: careful budget management (spending less than allocated) is punished by reduced future allocation; full consumption is rewarded by allocation maintenance. The rational institutional response is to ensure full consumption, regardless of whether the spending serves strategic priorities. The incentive operates at every level and cannot be overcome by individual goodwill or managerial instruction — it is structural.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
A significant and unmeasured proportion of institutional discretionary spending is allocated by calendar logic rather than strategic priority. The institution learns over time that efficient resource use is not rewarded — a lesson that shapes behaviour at every level, not just at year-end. Financial planning becomes a game of predicting and managing the flush rather than aligning resources with institutional need. The cost is not only financial: the management attention consumed by the flush is management attention not available for strategic work.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Budget cycles must be decoupled from spending incentives. Unspent funds must be carryable to the following period, at least within defined limits and for defined purposes. Alternatively, unspent funds must be redeployable to other institutional priorities during the budget year rather than returned. The allocation model must reward need-based spending rather than punishing underspending. Where carry-forward is legally constrained, the institution must be explicit about this as a governance cost and advocate for legislative change.

## DP-4 · Design Pattern: The Need-Based Allocation

*Structural inverse of: The Year-End Flush*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that wishes to align resource allocation with institutional need rather than with budget cycle logic — particularly institutions where discretionary spending plays a significant role in programme quality and strategic development.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To design resource allocation processes so that spending decisions are driven by institutional need rather than by calendar incentives — and to make the cost of misaligned incentives visible when they cannot be fully removed.

**Structure — what must be built**
Resource allocation is structured in two layers: a Base Allocation (set at the beginning of the period based on defined need indicators and prior-year outcomes) and a Strategic Reserve (held centrally and allocated during the year in response to demonstrated need, opportunity, or priority). Unspent Base Allocation funds are carried forward to a defined maximum, after which they are transferred to the Strategic Reserve rather than returned to central administration. The Year-End Flush is not a coordination task — it is a governance indicator: if it occurs at significant scale, it signals a misalignment between allocation and need that requires redesign.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
Budget performance is measured not by consumption rate but by need-alignment rate: the proportion of spending that can be traced to a defined institutional priority. Strategic Reserve allocation decisions are documented with explicit need justification and reviewed retrospectively. Where carry-forward is legally constrained, the institution documents the constraint explicitly, quantifies the cost of the flush annually, and includes advocacy for legislative change in its statutory review process.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Spending decisions are made in response to need rather than calendar. The management attention currently consumed by year-end mobilisation is available for strategic work. The institution develops an accurate picture of its own resource needs because spending is aligned with them. Budget allocation becomes a strategic instrument rather than an annual consumption exercise.
