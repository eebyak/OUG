# Pattern Pair 36: Institutional Renewal: Entrenchment versus Circulating Leadership

## AP-36 · Antipattern: The Permanent Position

**Context — where this appears**
Any governance structure in which senior leadership roles are held indefinitely, or in which the only available exit from a leadership role is retirement, forced removal, or moving to a different institution. Common in systems where the leadership role and the employment contract are structurally inseparable, or where returning to a non-leadership position after a leadership role carries significant loss of status or income.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
People cannot recall the last time this leadership role changed hands other than through retirement or scandal. Colleagues describe the role-holder as "always having been there" rather than as someone serving a term. Proposals for term limits are met not with substantive objection but with the observation that "it's never really worked that way here." The prospect of the role-holder stepping down is discussed, when at all, as a disruption to be managed rather than a normal transition to plan for.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
The role-holder experiences the leadership position as a permanent status rather than a temporary function. Decisions are made with a time horizon of indefinite tenure rather than four years: the short-term political cost of unpopular decisions weighs more heavily than the long-term institutional benefit. The governance body responsible for renewal cannot easily *not* renew: the cost of non-renewal (forced departure, legal challenge, reputational damage) is high enough that it is reserved for genuine crises rather than ordinary underperformance. The role becomes self-perpetuating.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
Leadership roles accumulate power over time rather than circulating. The institution cannot adapt without the role-holder choosing to change, which means institutional development is bounded by individual willingness rather than structural design. Knowledge of alternative approaches, of what other institutions have learned, of how the role could be played differently, does not enter the institution because the role is never vacated to allow new occupants with different perspectives.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Constitutionally limit leadership terms to a length long enough to produce results and short enough to prevent entrenchment, and structurally protect a genuine return option so that stepping down does not mean losing status or income. Treat the end of a term as a normal, planned governance event — with a handover document and transition period built in — rather than as a crisis to be managed only when it becomes unavoidable.

## DP-36 · Design Pattern: The Returnable Role

*Structural inverse of: The Permanent Position*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution where leadership roles currently have no real exit short of retirement or forced removal, and that wants leadership to circulate among qualified people rather than accumulate around whoever currently holds it.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To make stepping down from a leadership role a normal, low-cost event rather than a loss of status — so that term limits are actually usable rather than merely written down.

**Structure — what must be built**
Leadership roles are constitutionally term-limited, with terms long enough to produce results (four years) and short enough to prevent entrenchment. The return option is structurally protected: a role-holder who completes a term can return to a substantive academic or professional role without status or income loss. The end of a term is a normal governance event rather than a failure, and the institution's planning assumes it.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
Requires the return pathway to be genuinely attractive rather than nominally available. If returning to a non-leadership role carries implicit demotion, term limits will be resisted even when formally accepted. The pattern works best in institutions where academic and professional roles carry genuine status and where leadership is understood as a service rather than a career peak. The Handover Document and transition period are operational requirements without which term limits produce chaos rather than renewal.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Leadership roles circulate among qualified people rather than being captured by individuals. New occupants bring different perspectives, different networks, and different questions. Institutional adaptation is not blocked by incumbent self-interest. The governance body responsible for renewal exercises genuine choice rather than performing a ritual endorsement. The institution develops institutional memory through handover documentation rather than losing it through leadership transitions.

> **Note:** the OUG's own [Leadership Development & Induction Framework](../leadership/leadership-development-induction-framework.md) § VII (Term Completion and Transition) — including the four-year Rector term specified in the [Federation & Membership Framework's Statute on Organs](../partnership/federation-membership-framework.md) §6.8 and the Governance Career Recognition Record added by the [SP-7 amendment](../leadership/amendment-capability-measurement.md) — is a direct, working implementation of the Returnable Role this pattern specifies, right down to the four-year term length and the emphasis on a genuine, status-protected return pathway.
