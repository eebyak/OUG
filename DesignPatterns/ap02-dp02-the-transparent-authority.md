# Pattern Pair 2: Decision Authority and Accountability

## AP-2 · Antipattern: The Paper Tiger with Claws

**Context — where this appears**
Multi-level governance structures in which the formal designation of a body — advisory, consultative, recommendatory — does not match its structural power. Most common in institutions where governance has evolved incrementally, with each layer adding a new body without revisiting the authority map as a whole.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
A governance body is described as advisory but its positive vote is required before certain decisions can be taken. Participants are uncertain whether they are being consulted or whether they are deciding. When the body's recommendation is not followed, it is unclear whether this is legitimate executive discretion or a governance violation. When something goes wrong, neither the advisory body nor the executive can be clearly held responsible.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Formal advisory designation and substantive structural power are assigned to the same body without acknowledgment of the contradiction. The formal label ("advisory") shapes the culture and self-understanding of the body, while the substantive power (Einvernehmen, veto, mandatory consent) shapes its actual function. The two operate in parallel without ever being reconciled. The institution maintains the fiction of executive authority while the structural reality distributes power more widely — and less accountably.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
Accountability diffuses across the formal/substantive authority gap. No one is clearly responsible when decisions made in the advisory body's domain produce poor outcomes, because the body was "only advising." No one can clearly challenge the body's decisions, because it holds structural veto power. The institution cannot learn from governance failures because the failure cannot be assigned to a clearly responsible actor. Conflict between the advisory body's substantive power and the executive's formal authority produces procedural paralysis.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Every governance body must have a single, consistent authority designation that matches both its formal description and its structural power. If a body holds veto or consent rights, it is not advisory — it is a decision-making body and must be designed, resourced, and held accountable as one. If a body is genuinely advisory, it holds no blocking power. The two categories must not coexist in the same body. Governance architecture reviews must map the full authority structure, not just the formal designations.

## DP-2 · Design Pattern: The Transparent Authority

*Structural inverse of: The Paper Tiger with Claws*

**Context — where this applies**
Any governance structure with multiple layers or bodies in which decision-making authority must be distributed without creating ambiguity about who decides what.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To ensure that every governance body's formal designation, structural power, and accountability framework are aligned — so that participants, executives, and external observers can always determine who made a given decision and on what authority.

**Structure — what must be built**
Governance bodies are classified into three types with no overlap: Decision Bodies (hold formal authority to make binding decisions within their scope), Consent Bodies (must give positive consent before decisions in their scope take effect — functionally decision bodies and designed as such), and Advisory Bodies (provide input that decision-makers must consider but are not bound by). Every body is assigned one classification. The classification determines its composition, its accountability framework, its meeting requirements, and its documentation standards.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
The governance architecture document maps every body, its classification, its scope, and the decisions it is authorised to make. When a new body is created, its classification is determined before its composition. When bodies interact — when an Advisory Body's recommendation goes to a Decision Body — the handoff protocol is explicit: the Decision Body records that it has received the recommendation and states what weight it gave it. Governance reviews check classification consistency annually.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Governance failures can be assigned to clearly responsible actors. Participants in every body understand what authority they hold. Accountability is proportionate to actual power. The institution can learn from governance failures because it knows who decided what. Advisory bodies can function as genuine intelligence inputs rather than as blocked decision-makers. Decision bodies can act with confidence because their authority is unambiguous.
