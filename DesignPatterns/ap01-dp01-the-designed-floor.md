# Pattern Pair 1: Law, Convention, and the Boundaries of the Possible

**The Permission Illusion → The Designed Floor**

*Derived from: HEI-1 — Legal architecture presentation; the audience needed to be told what the law permits before reform could be discussed.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-1 · Antipattern: The Permission Illusion

**Context — where this appears**
Any organisation whose governance practices have been stable for a significant period and which operates within a statutory framework. Particularly prevalent in public higher education, where the legal framework is complex, multi-layered, and rarely read in full by the practitioners it governs.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
When reform is proposed, the first response is: "the law requires us to do it this way." Practitioners describe legal constraints that do not exist in the statutes they cite. Reform conversations spend significant time on permission-seeking before reaching design questions. External legal counsel is invoked to confirm what practitioners believe rather than to explore what the law actually permits.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Legal frameworks are typically more permissive than institutional practice suggests. The gap between statutory requirement and operational convention is filled by accumulated tradition, informal precedent, and the assumption — rarely examined — that current practice and legal mandate are identical. Over time, conventions harden into perceived constraints. The assumption is not deliberately maintained; it is simply never tested. Nobody reads the statute with the question "what does this actually permit?" because the answer is assumed to be known.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
Institutions fail to attempt reforms they are legally permitted to pursue. The legal framework becomes a ceiling when it was designed as a floor. Energy that should go into governance design goes into compliance theatre. When reform conversations do occur, a significant portion of available time and political capital must be spent dismantling the Permission Illusion before substantive design work can begin. Institutions that believe they are constrained by law are frequently constrained only by their own history — but the cost of that belief is real.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Require that every claimed legal constraint be verified by direct citation of the governing statute, not by reference to established practice. Design a periodic statutory review process in which governance documents are checked against the current legal text rather than against previous governance documents. Distinguish explicitly between what the law requires, what it permits, and what the institution has chosen — and document all three. Make the designed floor visible by stating governance choices as choices, not as requirements.

## DP-1 · Design Pattern: The Designed Floor

*Structural inverse of: The Permission Illusion*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution operating within a statutory framework that wishes to make active use of the design space the law provides rather than treating the law as a fixed constraint.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To make the distinction between legal requirement and institutional choice explicit, permanent, and revisable — so that the institution knows at all times what it must do, what it has chosen to do, and what it could do differently.

**Structure — what must be built**
Every governance rule is classified as one of three types: Statutory Requirement (what the law mandates), Institutional Choice (what the institution has decided within the legal space), and Open Design Space (what the law permits that the institution has not yet designed). The three types are documented separately and reviewed on different cycles. Statutory Requirements are reviewed when the law changes. Institutional Choices are reviewed on a defined governance cycle. Open Design Space is reviewed as part of strategic development.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
The institution maintains a Governance Compliance Map that distinguishes statutory requirements from institutional choices. When reform is proposed, the first question asked is not "is this permitted?" but "what type of rule governs this?" Governance documents cite their statutory basis explicitly. New governance roles specify which of their features are legally required and which are institutional design decisions. The statutory review is a standing item in the governance calendar, not an exceptional event.

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution can pursue reforms at the speed of design rather than the speed of legal consultation. The Permission Illusion cannot take hold because the distinction between law and convention is institutionally visible. Reform conversations begin from a shared understanding of the actual design space. Governance innovation is treated as a normal institutional activity, not as a risk-laden departure from legal requirement.
