# Pattern Pair 3: Role Design and Scope

**Role Overflow → The Bounded Role**

*Derived from: HEI-2 — A single Studiendekan role description with more than thirty distinct responsibilities spanning strategic leadership, operational management, compliance, communication, and facilities.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

## AP-3 · Antipattern: Role Overflow

**Context — where this appears**
Any governance role that has existed for a significant period without explicit scope definition, particularly roles that combine academic leadership with administrative responsibility in institutions where the distinction between the two is not structurally enforced.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
A role description lists more tasks than any single person could do well simultaneously. The role-holder is simultaneously the person responsible for strategic curriculum development and the person who books rooms, updates the homepage, and coordinates school visits. When asked to prioritise, the role-holder cannot — because no priority order exists in the role definition. Time studies, if conducted, would show the majority of time going to the most urgent tasks rather than the most important ones.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
When a governance role is defined by title and tradition rather than by explicit scope, it expands indefinitely. Tasks that need doing and have no obvious home flow toward the role because it is associated with responsibility and availability. Each task that arrives makes it slightly easier for the next task to arrive. The accumulation is gradual and invisible until the role has grown beyond any reasonable human capacity. The role-holder adapts by becoming very efficient at operational work — and losing capacity for strategic work entirely.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution loses strategic leadership capacity not because role-holders lack capability but because the role design consumes it. Operational urgency always displaces strategic importance — this is not a character failing but a structural certainty when both types of work share the same role without priority ordering. The pattern reproduces: when the role-holder leaves, the successor inherits the accumulated scope. The institution never examines what the role should be because it is too busy managing what the role has become.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Every governance role must have an explicit, written scope that distinguishes strategic responsibilities from operational responsibilities and specifies the maximum task load in each category. Tasks that exceed the scope must have a designated alternative home before they are assigned — not after. Role scope is reviewed annually and compared against the actual time allocation of role-holders. When the gap between designed scope and actual scope exceeds a defined threshold, a governance review is triggered.

## DP-3 · Design Pattern: The Bounded Role

*Structural inverse of: Role Overflow*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that needs academic governance roles to function as strategic leadership rather than accumulating as operational residual categories.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To define every governance role by explicit scope boundaries so that strategic work is protected from operational accumulation and role-holders can exercise the leadership authority their role was designed to hold.

**Structure — what must be built**
Every governance role is defined by four explicit boundaries: Strategic Scope (the decisions and directions the role-holder is responsible for leading), Operational Scope (the coordination and execution tasks the role-holder is responsible for), Exclusion List (tasks explicitly outside this role that must be assigned elsewhere), and Time Allocation (the minimum proportion of role-holder time that must be spent on strategic rather than operational work). The role definition is a governance document, not a job description — it is owned by the governance structure, not by HR.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a task arrives that has no clear home, the Exclusion List is consulted first. If the task falls outside every existing role's scope, a governance decision is required before it is assigned — it does not default to the nearest available person. Role-holders conduct a quarterly time audit against their defined allocation. The results are reported to the governance layer above them. When the strategic/operational ratio falls below the defined threshold for two consecutive quarters, a scope review is triggered.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Strategic governance capacity is structurally protected rather than structurally consumed. Role-holders can develop the leadership competencies their role requires because those competencies are actually used. The institution knows what its governance roles cost — in time, in capacity, in strategic attention — because the scope is explicit. When roles need to change, the change is a designed governance decision rather than an accumulated historical accident.
