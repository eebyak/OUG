# Pattern Pair 27: Quality Assurance: Compliance Inputs versus Learning Outcomes

**The Input Audit → The Outcome Measure**

*Derived from: GP-3 — Learning Is Measured by What Endures — GP-4: Governance Is the Condition for Sustained Pedagogical Innovation.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-27 · Antipattern: The Input Audit

**Context — where this appears**
Any quality assurance system — whether internal or external — that assesses educational quality primarily through input metrics: contact hours, staff-student ratios, library holdings, examination pass rates, assessment completion rates. Present in most European accreditation frameworks and in most institutional quality reporting systems.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Quality reports describe what was delivered — how many hours of teaching, what percentage of students passed, what infrastructure is available — rather than what was learned. Institutions invest significant effort in satisfying the metrics while the pedagogical question of whether the learning produced is durable, transferable, and genuinely capable-making receives little systematic attention. An institution that delivers poor content at high frequency and an institution that delivers excellent content at lower frequency score similarly on input metrics. The system cannot distinguish between them.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Input metrics are measurable, comparable, and auditable. Outcome metrics — does the graduate understand, can they apply, do they retain and transfer over time — are harder to measure, more expensive to track, and more difficult to standardise across institutions. Quality assurance systems default to input metrics because they can be collected, aggregated, and compared without requiring insight into what is actually happening in the learning relationship. The metrics then become the target: institutions optimise for what is measured, which means they optimise for inputs rather than for learning.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The quality assurance system is blind to the most important dimension of educational quality. It certifies institutions that meet input norms and fail their learners alongside institutions that meet input norms and genuinely develop them. It creates incentives for institutions to invest in measurable inputs rather than in the harder-to-measure conditions for genuine learning. Over time, the gap between what the quality system measures and what education actually produces widens, and the quality system's credibility as a genuine indicator of educational value erodes.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Quality assurance adds a longitudinal outcome layer alongside the input compliance layer: what can graduates do, what do they retain, and what do employers report about their capability? The institution tracks at least one outcome indicator that reaches beyond the credential issuance moment — alumni capability survey, employer feedback, or learner self-assessment at six and eighteen months post-completion.

## DP-27 · Design Pattern: The Outcome Measure

*Structural inverse of: The Input Audit*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that wishes its quality assurance to reflect whether learning is actually occurring rather than whether content is being delivered — and any policy maker designing quality frameworks who wishes those frameworks to improve educational quality rather than merely certify institutional compliance.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To build a quality assurance architecture in which the primary signal is what learners can do after engagement with the institution — durable, transferable, genuine capability — rather than what the institution delivered during engagement.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution maintains two quality layers operating in parallel. The compliance layer tracks input metrics required by accreditation and regulatory frameworks — not because they are the primary quality signal but because they are the price of institutional legitimacy and they provide minimum floor guarantees. The outcome layer tracks longitudinal capability indicators: competency assessment at module completion, alumni capability survey at six and eighteen months, employer feedback on graduate performance, and learner self-report on what they can do that they could not do before. The outcome layer is reported publicly alongside the compliance layer.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a module is reviewed, the primary question is: what evidence exists that learners who completed this module developed the capability it claimed to develop? Contact hours, reading lists, and examination pass rates are secondary. When an institution conducts its Developmental Audit, the first domain addresses learner outcomes — not as a compliance statement but as a genuine inquiry: are learners developing what the institution said they would develop? The answer informs programme revision rather than being reported and filed.

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution accumulates genuine knowledge about what its educational interventions produce — which approaches develop durable capability, which produce compliant completion without transfer, and which the quality system would have rated identically without the outcome layer. This knowledge enables genuine pedagogical improvement rather than metric optimisation. The institution can answer the question "do your graduates know more and can they do more than when they arrived?" with evidence rather than with proxy data.
