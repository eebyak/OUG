# Pattern Pair 31: Organisational Structure: Territory versus Shared Mission

**The Disciplinary Island → The Cooperative Cluster**

## AP-31 · Antipattern: The Disciplinary Island

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution organised primarily around individual chair or departmental boundaries rather than shared tasks or thematic problems. Most common in research-intensive universities where the chair (Lehrstuhl) model has been the organisational default for decades.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Requests for cross-unit collaboration are met with polite deferral rather than refusal. Joint proposals stall at the point where budget or staffing must actually be shared, not before. Each chair can describe in detail what its own unit does; no one can describe what the wider grouping of related units is trying to achieve together. New hires are recruited to strengthen an existing chair's territory rather than to fill a gap the institution as a whole has identified.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
The organisational unit is defined by a single professor's disciplinary territory. Resources, students, and intellectual production are managed within the unit rather than across units. The unit's success is measured by its own output rather than by its contribution to shared institutional goals. Cooperation between units requires individual goodwill and produces no structural benefit to either party.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
Knowledge that belongs together stays separated. Interdisciplinary research requires extraordinary effort and is structurally unrewarded. Curriculum cannot span unit boundaries without committee negotiation that systematically dilutes quality. Resource allocation is competitive rather than strategic. The institution cannot produce coherence because its organisational structure actively prevents it.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Define the institution's primary organisational units around shared tasks or thematic missions rather than individual chairs, and route budget and staffing decisions through those units rather than through disciplinary territories. Make cross-unit contribution a named, resourced expectation rather than a matter of individual goodwill. Measure units by their contribution to goals they share with others, not only by their own output.

## DP-31 · Design Pattern: The Cooperative Cluster

*Structural inverse of: The Disciplinary Island*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution, at founding or undergoing significant governance redesign, that wants interdisciplinary cooperation to be a structural default rather than an individual achievement.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To make cooperation a consequence of how units are defined, not a discretionary act that depends on goodwill. The institution should not need appeals to collegiality to produce collaboration; the organisational structure should produce it on its own.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution organises its primary units around shared tasks, thematic problems, or programme missions rather than around individual chair territories. Units are defined by what they are trying to achieve together, not by whose disciplinary ground they occupy. Resources flow to shared goals rather than to unit maintenance.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
Requires a founding moment or a significant governance redesign: existing chair structures resist dissolution because they are embedded in employment contracts, physical spaces, and cultural identity. Most applicable to new institutions or to restructuring processes with genuine executive mandate. **The OUG's Mission Hub architecture is a direct instantiation of this pattern** — the book's own words, not an inferred connection.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Interdisciplinarity is a structural consequence, not a cultural aspiration. Curriculum can cross unit boundaries without political negotiation. Resources are allocated where the shared work is, not where the historical unit is. The institution can respond to new societal needs by forming new clusters rather than waiting for existing units to expand their territory.

> **Note:** the source text names the OUG's Mission Hub architecture directly (see Implementation above). The [Federation & Membership Framework's Statute on Organs](../partnership/federation-membership-framework.md) §4.3–4.4 is the OUG document that actually defines Mission Hubs and how they're formed and led.
