# Pattern Pair 16: Teaching Roles, Division of Labour, and Design Efficiency

**The Overloaded Academic → The Unbundled Role**

*Derived from: CP-4 — The Unbundled Teacher — "Most academics are not trained in learning science or instructional design."*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-16 · Antipattern: The Overloaded Academic

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution in which academic staff are expected simultaneously to design curriculum, create content, and deliver teaching — without acknowledgment that these three functions require different expertise and without structural support for any of them.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
A faculty member who is an excellent researcher in their field is given responsibility for a module. They design the learning outcomes, write the teaching materials, create the assessments, and deliver the sessions. The quality of the outcome depends entirely on whether this individual also happens to have good instructional design instincts — which most do not, because they were trained in research methods, not learning science. Across the institution, module quality varies widely not because of disciplinary differences but because of individual differences in an unchosen and unsupported skill.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
The academic profession was designed around a model in which teaching and research are the two primary functions of an academic. Curriculum design and content creation were absorbed into teaching as an implicit expectation rather than acknowledged as separate professional functions requiring separate expertise and support. As research expectations have grown, the time and attention available for teaching preparation has shrunk — while the implicit expectation of curriculum design capability has remained. The result is a profession in which the teaching function is chronically under-resourced and under-supported, and in which quality varies systematically with individual capacity rather than institutional design.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution and its learners**
Curriculum quality across the institution is a function of individual accident rather than institutional design. Learners whose module happens to be taught by someone with good instructional instincts receive a better education than those whose module does not — through no fault of their own and through no choice of the institution. The institution simultaneously over-demands and under-supports its academic staff. Every institution separately recreates content that already exists elsewhere, often worse, because the person creating it was not trained for the task and lacks the time to do it well.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
The institution explicitly separates the functions of curriculum design, content creation, and learning facilitation — and provides structural support for each. Academic staff are not expected to be expert instructional designers. Where curriculum design and content creation require expertise that teaching staff do not have, that expertise is provided through dedicated roles or shared support. The institution invests in designing once, at the highest quality, rather than in every academic separately reinventing content that already exists.

## DP-16 · Design Pattern: The Unbundled Role

*Structural inverse of: The Overloaded Academic*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that wishes to achieve consistent curriculum quality across its portfolio without requiring every academic to be an expert in instructional design — and that recognises that the human value of teaching lies in facilitation and engagement, not in content creation.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To separate the functions of curriculum design, content architecture, and learning facilitation — assigning each to the people best equipped to perform it — so that teaching quality is determined by institutional design rather than by individual accident.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution distinguishes three functional roles in its curriculum architecture. The Curriculum Designer defines the competency architecture, the learning sequence, and the pedagogical framework — this role requires learning science expertise. The Content Architect builds the specific learning materials and assessments that implement the design — this role requires both disciplinary knowledge and instructional design skill. The Learning Facilitator delivers the learning experience — this role requires disciplinary expertise and the human capacity to engage learners. These roles may overlap in some individuals, but the distinction is explicit in role design, time allocation, and professional development.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a new module is needed, the process begins with the Design Authority specifying the competency requirements and pedagogical framework. Content creation is then undertaken by someone with appropriate instructional design support — not assumed to emerge from a research-oriented academic working alone. Teaching staff are briefed on the pedagogical framework and supported to facilitate rather than to recreate. Professional development for teaching staff focuses on facilitation skills, not on curriculum design, because curriculum design is not their function in this model.

**Result — what the institution and its learners gain**
Curriculum quality is determined by institutional design rather than by individual accident. Teaching staff are freed from a function they were not trained for and can focus on the function where their value is genuinely irreplaceable: the human relationship with learners. Content that is well-designed once is available to multiple cohorts rather than recreated annually by whoever teaches the module. The institution stops paying for the duplication of content creation across departments and invests instead in doing it once, excellently.
