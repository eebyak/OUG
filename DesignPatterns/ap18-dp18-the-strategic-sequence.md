## AP-18 · Antipattern: The Accidental Programme

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution or programme that was built by assembling courses before defining a learning strategy — so that the programme is the sum of what was available and what faculty agreed to teach, rather than the designed expression of a coherent educational purpose.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
The programme consists of a set of modules each of which is individually defensible but which do not obviously cohere from the learner's perspective. Students moving through the programme cannot see how the modules build on each other, what competency profile the programme is developing, or where they are in a development trajectory. When asked what the programme is for, faculty give different answers. When asked what makes the programme distinctive, the institution points to individual strong modules rather than to a coherent learning design.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Building courses is concrete and immediately productive. Building a learning strategy requires abstraction, expertise, and time — and its value is invisible until the courses are built against it and compared to courses built without it. Institutions under pressure to launch programmes quickly default to course-first design because it produces visible output faster. Each course is designed by the faculty member responsible for it, according to that person's understanding of what the course should be. The coherence that should come from a shared learning strategy is absent because the strategy was never written. The result is a programme that is the accidental sum of its parts.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution and its learners**
Learners experience the programme as incoherent. They cannot see the developmental logic. They cannot explain what they will be able to do at the end that they could not do at the beginning — because the programme was not designed to produce a specific development. Employers who hire graduates receive people who have a credential but cannot describe the capability it represents. The institution cannot articulate what makes its programme distinctive, because distinctiveness requires deliberate design and the programme was not deliberately designed at the level of learning strategy.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
The institution defines its learning strategy, teaching strategy, and assessment strategy before designing any course. Courses are then designed explicitly to serve the strategies: each course specifies which strategic objectives it contributes to and how. The programme is expressible — it can be described in two minutes to a prospective partner, in five minutes to a sceptical faculty member, and in thirty seconds to a prospective student — because it was designed with a coherent purpose that makes the description possible.

## DP-18 · Design Pattern: The Strategic Sequence

*Structural inverse of: The Accidental Programme*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution building a new programme or significantly reforming an existing one — and that wishes the resulting programme to be a coherent designed learning experience rather than an accidental assembly of available courses.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To ensure that every curriculum element is the deliberate expression of a prior strategic decision — so that the programme is coherent from the learner's perspective, expressible to every audience it needs to reach, and capable of demonstrating its own value.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution follows a defined design sequence. Stage one: define purpose, target group, and institutional identity. Stage two: develop learning strategy, teaching strategy, and assessment strategy — three explicit documents that describe how the institution expects learners to develop, what role different forms of instruction play, and how capability will be demonstrated. Stage three: design curriculum and courses as the structural expression of the strategies. No course design begins until stages one and two are complete. The strategies are public documents that can be assessed by prospective partners, learners, and accreditation bodies.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
Each course in the programme includes a Strategy Alignment statement: which strategic objectives does this course serve, and how? When a proposed course cannot produce a coherent Strategy Alignment statement, the course is redesigned before it is admitted to the programme. Programme review includes a coherence audit: can a learner who completes the programme describe the development trajectory they went through? Can the institution articulate the programme's distinctive value in the language of the strategies? The expressibility test — can the programme be described compellingly at multiple levels of detail — is applied as a quality indicator alongside learning outcome measures.

**Result — what the institution and its learners gain**
The programme is a coherent designed learning experience. Learners can see the developmental logic. The institution can articulate what makes the programme distinctive, because distinctiveness was designed rather than accumulated. Early adopters — partners, faculty, students, funders — can understand what they are joining because the programme can be explained clearly at every level of detail. The institution's curriculum is not the accidental sum of what faculty agreed to teach. It is the deliberate expression of what the institution decided its learners should become.
