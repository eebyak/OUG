# Pattern Pair 6: Information Flow and Structural Barriers

**The Fault Line → The Crossing Point**

*Derived from: HEI-2 — A faculty manager excluded from professorial discussions due to structural separation between academic and administrative staff — described explicitly as "camp formation" — despite holding responsibility for operational areas that depend on that information.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-6 · Antipattern: The Fault Line

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution where different categories of staff operate under different employment frameworks, different career logics, and different formal governance rights, and where no designed mechanism exists to transfer information across these categorical boundaries. Most visible in the gap between professorial/academic staff and administrative/technical staff in higher education.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Operational staff are not present in the meetings where strategic decisions affecting their work are made. Academic staff make decisions about processes they do not fully understand because operational knowledge does not reach them. Information about what is actually happening in programmes, laboratories, and student interactions accumulates on the administrative side and does not reach governance level. Strategy documents are written without the operational intelligence needed to assess their feasibility. Implementation fails for reasons that were visible to operational staff but invisible to the people who designed the strategy.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
The Fault Line is produced by the intersection of three structural factors: different employment frameworks (TVL versus *Beamtenstatus* versus professorial appointment) that create different institutional identities and loyalties; different governance rights (voting rights, committee membership, formal standing) that exclude administrative staff from the spaces where information is exchanged; and different career logics (academic recognition versus administrative progression) that create different incentive structures for information sharing. None of these factors individually produces the Fault Line — their intersection does.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
Institutional intelligence about operational reality accumulates on one side of the Fault Line and does not reach the governance level where decisions are made. Decisions made without operational intelligence are systematically worse than they would otherwise be. Conversely, strategic direction set at governance level does not translate into operational understanding on the administrative side — implementation is less effective than it could be. The Fault Line also produces the Proximity Task ([AP-5](ap05-dp05-the-designed-task.md)): operational staff who cannot access governance-level information cannot challenge task accumulation that violates their role design, because they do not have the governance standing to raise it.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Designed crossing points must be built into governance structures wherever the Fault Line runs. This means formal mechanisms — not informal relationships — through which operational intelligence reaches governance level and strategic direction reaches operational level. Crossing points include: mixed-membership bodies where administrative and academic staff have equivalent standing for defined purposes; structured information transfer protocols at the boundary between strategic decision and operational implementation; and formal role definitions for operational staff that include governance participation as a designed component, not an optional extra.

## DP-6 · Design Pattern: The Crossing Point

*Structural inverse of: The Fault Line*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution with structurally distinct staff categories that need to share information effectively in both directions — from operational reality upward to governance, and from strategic direction downward to operational implementation.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To build designed mechanisms that transfer information across structural boundaries between staff categories, so that governance decisions are informed by operational intelligence and operational implementation is informed by strategic understanding.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution maps its Fault Lines explicitly: every boundary between staff categories where information asymmetry is structurally likely. For each identified Fault Line, at least one Crossing Point is designed. A Crossing Point is a formal mechanism — not a relationship, not a cultural aspiration — with defined participants, defined information flow, defined frequency, and defined documentation. Crossing Points take different forms depending on the information need: Upward Crossing Points bring operational intelligence to governance level; Downward Crossing Points translate strategic decisions into operational understanding; Lateral Crossing Points enable equivalent-level coordination across categorical boundaries.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
Each Crossing Point has a designated convener, a defined agenda structure, and a documentation standard. The convener is responsible for ensuring that information that arrived at the Crossing Point from one side is confirmed as received and understood by the other side — not merely transmitted. Crossing Point effectiveness is reviewed annually: has the information asymmetry it was designed to address actually reduced? When a governance decision subsequently fails for operational reasons that were knowable in advance, the post-mortem includes a Crossing Point audit: was there a Crossing Point that should have carried this information? If not, a new Crossing Point is designed.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Governance decisions are made with operational intelligence. Strategic direction is implemented with strategic understanding. The Fault Line does not disappear — the structural factors that produce it are real and not easily removed — but its information consequences are mitigated by designed transfer mechanisms. The institution can identify, before implementation, the operational constraints that will shape strategic execution. Operational staff have a formal role in institutional intelligence that is designed, not proximity-dependent.
