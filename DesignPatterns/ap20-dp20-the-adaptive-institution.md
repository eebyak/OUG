# Pattern Pair 20: Governance Flexibility and Structural Revision

**The Frozen Structure → The Adaptive Institution**

*Derived from: SP-2 — Adaptive Governance — "Rigid governance structures lose their ability to respond to change."*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-20 · Antipattern: The Frozen Structure

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution whose governance structures — committees, faculties, boards, procedural frameworks — were designed at a particular moment and have since acquired the permanence of constitutional fixtures rather than the revisability of design choices. Most prevalent in institutions with long histories, strong guild cultures, and governance documents that have not been substantively revised in more than a decade.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Reform proposals are assessed not on their merit but on their compatibility with existing structural arrangements. Committees that were created to solve problems which no longer exist continue to meet and produce outputs that nobody acts on. When a governance structure is proposed for dissolution or significant revision, its members mobilise to preserve it — not because its purpose is still served but because its existence defines their institutional role. The institution can describe its governance in great structural detail but cannot explain why each element exists.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Governance structures accumulate legitimacy through age and familiarity. A committee that has existed for twenty years is experienced as a natural feature of the institution rather than a design decision that can be revisited. The cost of changing any element is visible and immediate — disruption, political opposition, procedural complexity. The cost of not changing is invisible and deferred — reduced adaptability, accumulated irrelevance, governance energy spent on maintenance rather than purpose. The rational individual response is to leave existing structures undisturbed; the systemic consequence is institutional calcification.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution becomes progressively less capable of responding to changes in its environment, its learner population, and its social context — not because its leaders lack the will to adapt but because the governance architecture makes adaptation structurally costly. Transformation proposals that require significant governance change are quietly reshaped into proposals that can be accommodated within existing structures, which means they are reshaped into proposals that do not produce transformation.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Every governance element is subject to a periodic legitimacy review: does this structure still serve the purpose it was created for, and is that purpose still important? The review is not a threat to governance structures that are working — it is protection against the accumulation of structures that are not. The institution maintains a governance change protocol that makes structural revision a normal operation rather than a crisis.

## DP-20 · Design Pattern: The Adaptive Institution

*Structural inverse of: The Frozen Structure*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that wishes to remain capable of responding to societal transformation over time — and that recognises that this capacity requires governance structures that can themselves be changed rather than governance structures that resist change.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To build governance adaptability into the institution's design — so that structural revision is a normal, managed process rather than a crisis, and so that governance elements that no longer serve their purpose can be retired without existential institutional drama.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution maintains a Governance Review Calendar: every significant governance structure — committee, faculty, board, working group — has a scheduled review date at which its continued existence and form must be justified against its current purpose. The review is not an evaluation of performance but a legitimacy check: does this structure still serve the purpose it was created for? Structures that pass the review are confirmed. Structures that fail are either reformed or retired. New structures are created with explicit sunset clauses or review triggers.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a significant external change occurs — in law, in technology, in the institution's learner population, in its funding environment — the institution conducts an impact assessment on its governance architecture: which existing structures are now misaligned with the new context? The Governance Review Calendar is accelerated for those structures. Leadership has explicit authority to initiate governance reviews between scheduled dates when warranted by changed circumstances. The institution tracks the proportion of governance decisions that required structural change versus those accommodated within existing structures — a high ratio of accommodation relative to structural change is an early warning signal.

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution can adapt to changed circumstances without governance crisis. Governance energy is spent on purpose rather than on maintenance of structures whose purpose has lapsed. Leaders who identify necessary structural changes have a legitimate process for initiating them rather than having to work around existing structures. The institution's governance architecture at any given time reflects its current purpose rather than the accumulated residue of previous purposes.

> **Note:** the OUG's own [Governance Review Calendar](../governance/governance-review-calendar.md) is a direct, named implementation of this design pattern.
