# Pattern Pair 23: Leadership as Legitimate Vocation

*Derived from: SP-6 and SP-7 — The Heart and Mind of Leadership · Leadership as Legitimate Vocation.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

## AP-23 · Antipattern: The Accidental Leader

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution in which governance and leadership roles are assigned on the basis of seniority, availability, or political negotiation — rather than on the basis of demonstrated leadership capacity and genuine vocational commitment to the governance function.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Leadership roles are filled by rotation among willing or reluctant colleagues rather than through competitive selection against leadership criteria. Newly appointed leaders receive no significant development support and are expected to learn the role while performing it. Leaders who are visibly ineffective cannot be removed without damaging the collegial relationships that the institution depends on. Leaders who are effective leave the role after a short term because the role offers neither career development nor institutional recognition commensurate with its demands. The institution's leadership quality at any given time is a function of who happened to be willing and available.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
The academic profession was designed around the assumption that academic excellence and governance capacity are the same thing or close enough to be treated as the same. This assumption is structurally convenient — it means that the institution's most respected members can fill governance roles without the institution having to develop a separate leadership pipeline. The assumption is also structurally false: the skills required to produce excellent research or teaching are genuinely different from the skills required to design organisations, manage complexity, hold accountability, and lead through change. The conflation produces leaders who are neither fully committed to the role nor equipped for it.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution is governed by people who did not choose to govern and are not equipped to govern well. The organisational consequences — poor decisions, slow adaptation, missed opportunities, demoralised staff — are attributed to individual failures rather than to the structural absence of a leadership pipeline. Excellent academics who would be excellent leaders are never identified, developed, or retained in leadership roles because the institution has no framework for doing so.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
The institution treats leadership as a distinct professional function with its own criteria, development pathways, career recognition, and accountability mechanisms. Governance roles are filled by selection against explicit leadership criteria — which include both the scientist's heart (commitment to academic norms) and the manager's mind (organisational design capacity). Leadership development is a standing institutional investment, not an optional enrichment.

## DP-23 · Design Pattern: The Professionalised Vocation

*Structural inverse of: The Accidental Leader*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that wishes its governance to be led by people who chose to lead, are equipped to lead, and are held accountable for the quality of their leadership — rather than by people who happened to be available when a role needed filling.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To create a leadership architecture in which governance roles are filled through genuine vocational commitment and professional preparation — making leadership a legitimate career path with its own development, recognition, and accountability rather than a service obligation rotated among reluctant colleagues.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution maintains a Leadership Architecture document that specifies: the competency profile required for each significant governance role (including the scientist's heart and the manager's mind as distinct and both required); the selection process for each role; the development support available to role-holders; the performance expectations and accountability mechanisms; and the career pathways that governance experience enables. Term limits are specified for all significant roles. The institution actively identifies potential leaders early and provides development opportunities before they are needed in a role.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a governance role becomes vacant, the institution conducts a genuine selection process against the published competency profile — not a search for the senior person who is willing to serve. New leaders enter a structured induction and receive ongoing development support throughout their term. When a leader is performing poorly against the role's expectations, the institution has a legitimate process for addressing this that does not require the destruction of collegial relationships. When a leader's term ends, their governance experience is formally recognised in their professional record and opens subsequent career opportunities.

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution is governed by people who want to govern and are equipped to govern well. Leadership roles attract candidates rather than defaulting to the willing. The scientist's heart and the manager's mind coexist in the institution's leaders because both are required and both are developed. Governance quality improves over time because leadership is treated as a learnable, developable professional function rather than an innate characteristic of seniority.
