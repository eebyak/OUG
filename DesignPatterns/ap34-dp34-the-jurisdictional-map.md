# Pattern Pair 34: Role Clarity: Ambiguous Authority versus Written Jurisdiction

## AP-34 · Antipattern: The Blurred Boundary

**Context — where this appears**
Any governance structure in which the boundaries between roles are insufficiently specified, leaving significant overlap, gap, or ambiguity about who is authorised to decide, who must be consulted, and who is accountable for outcomes. Extremely common in academic governance, where role definitions are historically informal and accumulated over time.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
A decision sits unmade for weeks because two office-holders each assume the other is handling it. When something does go wrong, the first governance conversation is about who should have acted, not about what should happen next — and the conversation rarely reaches a clear answer. The same type of decision is handled differently depending on who happens to be in the room. People describe their own role by what they personally have taken on, not by what a written mandate says.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
A decision arises that multiple roles could claim authority over. Each role-holder either assumes the other will act (producing the gap: the decision is not made) or both act simultaneously in different directions (producing the conflict: incompatible decisions are implemented). Neither outcome triggers a governance correction, because the ambiguity makes it impossible to determine who was at fault. The ambiguity is then reproduced in subsequent decisions because the underlying role boundaries are never clarified.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution experiences repeated instances of decisions that are not made, made inconsistently, or made by whoever happens to have more political energy at the moment. These outcomes are attributed to the individuals involved rather than to the structural ambiguity that produced them. The institution learns nothing from the pattern because it cannot see the pattern. Role clarity is experienced as a bureaucratic concern rather than a governance precondition, so it is perpetually deprioritised in favour of more visible governance reforms.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Give every governance role a written jurisdictional specification distinguishing what it decides alone, what it must consult on, and what it refers upward — produced before disputes arise, not in response to them. Review and update the specification whenever a boundary incident reveals a gap, so that ambiguity becomes a visible, correctable design fault rather than a recurring, invisible one.

## DP-34 · Design Pattern: The Jurisdictional Map

*Structural inverse of: The Blurred Boundary*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution experiencing recurring decisions that are either unmade or made inconsistently because more than one role could plausibly claim authority over them.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To make jurisdiction over each type of decision explicit and documented, so that boundary failures become visible as map failures rather than as personal failures — and so they can be corrected rather than merely repeated.

**Structure — what must be built**
Every governance role has a written jurisdictional specification that distinguishes three categories: decisions this role makes unilaterally, decisions this role must consult on before making, and decisions this role refers to a higher or different authority. The specifications are public within the institution, reviewed when roles change hands, and updated when jurisdictional disputes reveal gaps.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
The jurisdictional map must be produced before governance disputes arise, not in response to them. Maps produced in response to disputes encode the political outcome of the dispute rather than the correct jurisdictional logic. The investment required is modest: a structured workshop with each role-holder, documented in a standard format. The main resistance is cultural: role-holders who benefit from jurisdictional ambiguity will resist specification. Requires executive will to produce and maintain the map.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Jurisdictional disputes become visible as map failures rather than as personal failures, which makes them correctable. Role-holders know what they are responsible for and can be held accountable to it. Decisions that fall in the gap between roles are visible as governance design problems rather than as individual failures of initiative. The institution can learn from boundary incidents and update the map accordingly.

> **Note:** the OUG's own [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md) — with its explicit decision-domain mapping (D-A through D-I) — and the [Leadership & Stewardship Profiles'](../leadership/leadership-stewardship-profiles.md) explicit-limits-of-authority sections for every role are both direct, working implementations of the Jurisdictional Map this pattern specifies.
