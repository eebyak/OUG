# Pattern Pair 29: External Funding and the Difference Between Reorganisation and Transformation

**The Untranslated Incentive → The Converted Incentive**

*Derived from: GP-6 — Exogenous Incentives Can Shift Governance Logic — But Only If Leadership Converts Them.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-29 · Antipattern: The Untranslated Incentive

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution that receives an exogenous incentive — a funding programme, a policy mandate, a competitive mechanism, a reputational opportunity — and responds by producing structural documents, reorganised org charts, and new committee names without developing the shared internal vision that would make the incentive a genuine driver of cultural and governance change.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
The institution applies for the funding, wins it, reorganises the structure it requires, and produces the outputs the programme demands. When the programme ends, the institution returns to its previous operating logic. The structural changes made to satisfy the programme persist as artefacts — new units that now do what old units did, new titles that now carry what old roles carried. The underlying culture, the distribution of real authority, and the governance logic that determines how decisions are actually made are unchanged.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Exogenous incentives create structural pressure but not internal direction. An institution that receives additional funding to develop cross-faculty strategies has a reason to create cross-faculty governance structures. It does not thereby have a shared understanding of what those structures are for, who they serve, and how they connect to the institution's actual educational purpose. Without leadership capable of converting the external incentive into an internal narrative — here is why this matters for who we are and what we are trying to become — the structural response is cosmetic. The incentive produces reorganisation rather than transformation.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution depletes resources and political capital on structural changes that do not change the institution. Repeated experiences of this kind produce scepticism among staff about the value of reform initiatives — a scepticism that is empirically justified and institutionally harmful. Over time, the institution becomes skilled at responding to external incentives without being changed by them, which means it becomes progressively less capable of the genuine transformation that would require it to be changed by something.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Leadership explicitly translates every significant external incentive into an internal narrative before structural changes are made. The narrative answers three questions: why does this matter for our educational purpose, what will be genuinely different when this is done, and how will we know? Structural changes follow from the narrative rather than preceding it.

## DP-29 · Design Pattern: The Converted Incentive

*Structural inverse of: The Untranslated Incentive*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that receives an exogenous incentive — funding, policy mandate, competitive mechanism — and wishes to use it as a genuine driver of structural and cultural change rather than as an occasion for cosmetic reorganisation.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To ensure that every significant exogenous incentive is converted into a shared internal vision before structural responses are designed — so that the institution uses external pressure to become something genuinely different rather than to produce structural artefacts that demonstrate responsiveness without producing change.

**Structure — what must be built**
Before any significant structural response to an exogenous incentive is designed, leadership produces an Incentive Conversion Statement: a short document that answers three questions. Why does this incentive matter for the institution's educational purpose — not for its budget or its standing? What will be genuinely different about how the institution operates when this is done — not in terms of org chart but in terms of what learners experience and what staff do? How will the institution know whether the change has occurred? The Incentive Conversion Statement is reviewed by the Hochschulrat before structural changes are approved.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When the Excellence Initiative, a BMBF programme, or an EU funding call arrives, the first institutional response is not a structural proposal but a narrative conversation: what could this make possible that matters for our learners? The structural proposal follows when the narrative has been developed and tested. When the programme ends, the Incentive Conversion Statement becomes the accountability document: did the institution become what it said it would become, or did it produce structural changes that the programme required without achieving the educational purpose the narrative identified?

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution develops genuine institutional memory of what external incentives have made possible and what they have failed to change. Leadership develops the capability to convert external pressure into internal direction rather than internal compliance performance. The institution becomes more capable of genuine transformation over time because each incentive builds on a shared understanding of why it mattered rather than on a structural response that disperses when the funding ends.

> **Note:** this pattern's name is not a paraphrase — the OUG's own [Incentive Conversion Statement](../governance/incentive-conversion-statement.md) is the literal document this design pattern specifies, down to the three-question structure (why does this matter, what will be different, how will we know) and the Hochschulrat review requirement.
