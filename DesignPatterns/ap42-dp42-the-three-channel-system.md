## AP-42 · Antipattern: The One-Way Institution

**Context — where this appears**
Any governance system in which information flows primarily from the top of the hierarchy downward to the people responsible for implementation, without designed mechanisms for operational knowledge to travel upward or for coordination knowledge to travel laterally across governance domains. Present in most traditionally structured institutions and in many that have introduced participatory rhetoric without changing the underlying information architecture.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Operational staff can describe problems with the current strategy in detail, in private conversation, but there is no route for that knowledge to reach the people setting strategy other than an annual survey or an informal word to someone who happens to know someone. Coordination between adjacent units depends entirely on whether the people involved happen to know each other. When two units make conflicting decisions, the first anyone in governance hears about it is when the conflict has already caused a problem.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Strategy and operational direction travel top-down. Operational knowledge — what is actually happening in teaching, research, partnerships, and student experience — travels upward only through formal reporting, which is slow, filtered, and structured to confirm rather than challenge the strategic narrative. Lateral coordination happens informally through personal relationships rather than through designed channels, which means coordination quality depends on whether the right relationships happen to exist. Information losses occur at every structural boundary.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
Strategic decisions are made with incomplete information about operational reality. Misalignments between strategy and practice are discovered late. Lateral coordination failures produce duplicated effort, missed dependencies, and conflicting decisions in adjacent domains. Leadership has a confident but incomplete picture of the institution. The people with the most direct knowledge of what is actually working have no reliable channel through which that knowledge reaches governance. The institution learns slowly because the information it needs is trapped at the level where it is generated.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Design a named bottom-up channel through which operational knowledge reaches governance on a structured basis, and a named lateral channel through deliberate structural overlap between adjacent bodies — so that coordination and course-correction depend on designed channels rather than on which personal relationships happen to exist.

## DP-42 · Design Pattern: The Three-Channel System

*Structural inverse of: The One-Way Institution*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution whose information architecture currently moves strategy and direction downward but has no designed channel for operational knowledge to travel upward or coordination knowledge to travel laterally.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To make governance decisions informed by what is actually happening operationally, and to make cross-domain coordination a property of designed structure rather than of who happens to know whom.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution designs three simultaneous information channels: a top-down channel (strategy, priorities, and direction from governance to implementation), a bottom-up channel (operational knowledge, misalignments, and ground-truth from implementation to governance), and a lateral channel (coordination knowledge across governance domains). Each channel has a named mechanism: the bottom-up channel has a structured reporting process and a named body that receives and processes operational knowledge; the lateral channel has designed structural overlap between adjacent governance bodies.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
The bottom-up channel is the most politically difficult because it requires leadership to receive information that contradicts the strategic narrative and respond with engagement rather than defensiveness. The lateral channel requires designing structural overlap deliberately — individuals in multiple bodies, committees with shared members — rather than treating this as an efficiency problem to be eliminated. Most applicable at founding. In established institutions, introducing bottom-up channels requires sustained cultural work alongside structural design.

**Result — what the institution gains**
Governance decisions are informed by operational knowledge rather than only by the filtered version that travels through formal reporting. Misalignments are detected closer to the point of origin. Lateral coordination happens through designed channels rather than personal relationships, which makes coordination quality less dependent on who happens to know whom. The institution's learning speed increases because knowledge generated at operational levels reaches governance within the same cycle rather than in the next annual review.

> **Note:** "Three-Channel System" is not a coincidental name-match. The OUG's own [Committee Information Architecture](../governance/committee-information-architecture.md) already implements a three-channel structure, and [Part 5's structural incompatibilities table](../part5-maturity-signature.md) *(placeholder — link once that document has a page)* names AP-42 explicitly: *"Practitioner intelligence from New Study has no designed channel to DHBW governance. The OUG's Three-Channel System routes it to governance as a design feature."* This is the same pattern this document specifies, cited by its own name in the OUG's own comparative case study.
