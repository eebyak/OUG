# Pattern Pair 28: Transformation Governance: Assigning What Nobody Owns

**The Orphaned Mandate → The Named Transformer**

*Derived from: GP-5 — Responsibility for Transformation Must Be Explicitly Assigned.*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-28 · Antipattern: The Orphaned Mandate

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution in which the need for structural transformation is widely recognised but the responsibility for driving that transformation is distributed across existing roles without being explicitly assigned to any of them. Extremely common in public universities, where transformation is discussed in strategic plans, leadership speeches, and committee meetings, but where no role carries transformation as a primary mandate.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
When asked who is responsible for driving the institutional change described in the strategic plan, no single person can be named. Different parts of the institution point to different roles — the rector, the vice-rector for teaching, the quality commission, the faculty deans — each of whom has partial responsibility and none of whom has primary accountability. Reform proposals travel between committees without accumulating the decision-making authority needed to be implemented. Those who care most about change spend their energy navigating the governance architecture rather than implementing anything.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Transformation responsibility is not assigned because assigning it requires giving someone authority that other role-holders currently share — which means that assigning it requires someone else to give something up. In collegial governance cultures, this redistribution is politically costly and therefore avoided. Instead, transformation is added to existing roles as an expectation without additional authority, which means it is the first thing to be deprioritised when those roles are under operational pressure — which is always.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution moves through reform cycles without producing structural change. Each cycle produces a new strategic plan, a new set of working groups, and a new set of recommendations that are partially implemented and then abandoned when the political energy that produced them dissipates. The people most capable of driving change learn that the institution will not give them the authority to do so and either disengage or leave. The institution's reform capacity erodes over time precisely because its reform efforts are structured in a way that guarantees their insufficiency.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Transformation responsibility is a primary function of a named governance role — not an additional duty. The role has explicit authority to propose structural changes, mandate cross-unit coordination, and report directly to the highest governance body on progress. It is filled through a competency-based appointment process that selects for change management capability. Its mandate is time-bounded and reviewed at the governance level.

## DP-28 · Design Pattern: The Named Transformer

*Structural inverse of: The Orphaned Mandate*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that has identified structural changes it needs to make and wishes to ensure that those changes are driven by a role with genuine mandate rather than distributed across existing roles as an additional expectation.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To assign transformation responsibility to a named role with explicit authority, defined mandate, adequate resources, and governance-level accountability — so that the energy available for institutional change accumulates in one place with sufficient force to produce structural movement rather than dispersing across the institution without effect.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution creates or designates a Transformation Role — this may be an existing role reconstituted with explicit transformation mandate or a new role created for a defined period. The Transformation Role carries: primary responsibility for driving the structural changes identified in the strategic plan; authority to convene cross-unit working groups and mandate their participation; direct reporting to the Hochschulrat (G1) on a quarterly basis; and a defined term with explicit renewal criteria. The role is distinct from operational management: the Transformation Role holder is not running existing operations, they are changing them.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a structural reform is identified as necessary, the Transformation Role holder is named as responsible within the same decision that identifies the reform. The reform is added to their mandate — not to a committee's agenda. They have authority to request resources, convene actors, and escalate when units obstruct necessary changes. They report publicly on progress at each governance cycle. When their term ends, the transformation mandate passes explicitly to a named successor or is concluded with a documented account of what was achieved and what remains.

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution accumulates transformation capacity rather than depleting it. People who are capable of driving change are given roles in which that capability is the primary requirement. Reform cycles produce structural changes rather than strategic documents. The institution can answer the question "who is responsible for this changing?" with a name rather than with a process.

> **Note:** the OUG's own [Amendment: Rector's Transformation Mandate (GP-5, GP-6)](../leadership/amendment-transformation-mandate.md) is a direct, named implementation of this pattern — same GP-5 gap identifier as this pattern's own derivation source, and the amendment explicitly makes the Rector's office the "Named Transformer" this design pattern describes.
