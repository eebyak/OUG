## AP-32 · Antipattern: The Placeless Institution

**Context — where this appears**
Any higher education institution that understands its mandate primarily in terms of academic production — research outputs, degree completions, accreditation compliance — without a specific, named relationship to the society, city, or region it physically and constitutionally inhabits.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
The institution's public communications — annual reports, mission statements, recruitment materials — read as though they could be issued by any comparable institution anywhere. Asked what the region would lose if the institution closed, staff and leadership struggle to answer with anything more specific than "jobs" and "prestige." Local partnerships exist but are opportunistic and occasional rather than structurally expected. The campus is discussed in planning documents in terms of square metres and capacity, never as a civic presence.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
The institution's strategic documents describe its purpose in terms of academic excellence, student development, and research impact — all of which are self-referential: they describe what the institution does for itself and its members rather than what it does for the world outside. The societal mandate is acknowledged in general terms in mission statements but has no operational expression: it does not govern which programmes are offered, which partnerships are pursued, which communities are reached.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution**
The institution is indistinguishable in purpose from any other institution at the same research level or teaching focus. It cannot answer with specificity what would be lost from its city, region, or constitutional community if it ceased to exist. It is a knowledge factory that happens to be located somewhere rather than an institution that belongs somewhere. This makes it structurally unable to claim the public resources its mandate entitles it to, because it cannot articulate what public function it performs.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Require the institution's strategic documents to name a specific societal mandate — what this institution does for this city, region, and society that no comparable institution does — and route programme design, partnership decisions, and access policy through that mandate rather than through generic academic ambition. Ground the mandate explicitly in constitutional language (Article 12 GG, *Daseinsvorsorge*, the state education goal) so the specificity is a legal obligation being fulfilled, not a marketing preference.

## DP-32 · Design Pattern: The Embedded University

*Structural inverse of: The Placeless Institution*

**Context — where this applies**
Any publicly funded institution that wants to claim public resources on constitutional grounds rather than on competitive grounds alone, and that operates within a specific city, region, or societal context it can name.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To make the institution's societal mandate specific and operational rather than general and rhetorical, so that what the institution owes its host society, and what it is entitled to in return, can both be stated precisely.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution articulates and operationalises its specific relationship to the society, region, and constitutional community it serves. Its strategic documents answer two questions with specificity: what does this institution do for this city, region, and society that no other institution does? And what constitutional obligation is the institution expressing through its work? These answers govern programme design, partnership selection, and access architecture.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
Requires a willingness to make the societal mandate specific rather than general — which means accepting that the institution does not serve everyone and every purpose. The constitutional grounding (Article 12 GG, *Daseinsvorsorge*, state education goal) is the legal infrastructure that makes the specificity legitimate rather than merely preferential. Particularly important for publicly funded institutions that must be able to account for their use of public resources in public terms.

**Result — what the institution gains**
The institution can make a constitutional claim for public resources rather than merely a competitive claim. Its access architecture, programme portfolio, and partnership decisions are governed by a stated societal mandate rather than by market positioning. External stakeholders — funders, ministries, communities — can evaluate whether the institution is fulfilling its specific mandate rather than only whether it compares favourably to peer institutions on generic metrics.

> **Note:** the exact constitutional grounding named here — Article 12 GG, *Daseinsvorsorge* — is the same argument made at length in the OUG's own [Allgemeinwohl Verfassung](../governance/allgemeinwohl-verfassung.md) and in the [Role Statement's](../strategy/role-statement.md) constitutional argument for why the state is obligated to fund an institution like the OUG.
