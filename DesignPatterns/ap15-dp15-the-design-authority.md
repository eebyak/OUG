# Pattern Pair 15: Curriculum Governance and Design Authority

**The Committee Curriculum → The Design Authority**

*Derived from: CP-3 — Expert Design Authority — "Design is non-negotiable."*

[← Back to the pattern catalogue](index.md)

---

## AP-15 · Antipattern: The Committee Curriculum

**Context — where this appears**
Any institution in which curriculum design decisions are made primarily through committee processes — where the committee's composition is based on institutional representation rather than learning design expertise, and where consensus is the operative decision-making standard.

**Symptom — what it looks like from inside**
Curriculum reform proposals are submitted to a committee of faculty representatives from across the institution. Each representative advocates for their disciplinary area's interests and resists changes that reduce their area's prominence or autonomy. The reform process takes significantly longer than anticipated. The curriculum that emerges from the process reflects the negotiated accommodation of competing interests rather than a coherent learning design. No single member of the committee is strongly opposed to the result, but none of them believes it is excellent.

**Mechanism — the structural logic that produces it**
Committees are designed for accountability and representation, not for design excellence. A committee whose members represent institutional constituencies will optimise for the satisfaction of those constituencies. When design decisions — which require expertise in learning science, instructional design, and disciplinary integration — are made by representatives whose mandate is institutional interests, the expertise that should drive the decisions is absent from the room. The resulting curriculum is politically acceptable rather than pedagogically sound. This is not the committee's failure: it is a structural mismatch between the committee's purpose and the task assigned to it.

**Consequence — what it costs the institution and its learners**
The curriculum is designed to the level of political acceptability, not to the level of learning effectiveness. Disciplinary silos are preserved because no committee member is willing to challenge them. Innovative pedagogical approaches are diluted because committee members who are not experts in learning science cannot evaluate them and default to what they recognise. The reform process consumes institutional energy and produces incremental change rather than the structural redesign that the learning evidence supports.

**Counter-design — what prevents this structurally**
Curriculum design decisions are made by a Design Authority whose members are selected for learning design expertise, not for institutional representation. The Design Authority is accountable to governance committees — which ratify, resource, and oversee the design — but does not have its design decisions revised by committee consensus. The distinction between design decisions (made by expertise) and governance decisions (made by representation) is explicit, documented, and enforced.

## DP-15 · Design Pattern: The Design Authority

*Structural inverse of: The Committee Curriculum*

**Context — where this applies**
Any institution that needs to make curriculum design decisions based on learning science and pedagogical expertise rather than on institutional politics — and that recognises that the two require different governance mechanisms.

**Intent — what this pattern achieves**
To separate curriculum design decisions from curriculum governance decisions — so that design is made by those with expertise in making it, and governance is made by those with accountability for the institution — and to make this separation explicit rather than informal.

**Structure — what must be built**
The institution designates a Curriculum Design Authority: a small body whose members are selected for expertise in learning science, instructional design, and the relevant disciplinary domains. The Design Authority holds design authority — the power to make binding curriculum design decisions — within boundaries set by the institution's learning strategy. Governance committees hold ratification authority — the power to approve or reject curriculum proposals — and oversight authority — the power to review whether the Design Authority is operating within its mandate. They do not hold the power to redesign by consensus.

**Implementation — what this looks like in practice**
When a curriculum decision is proposed, it is developed by the Design Authority and presented to the relevant governance committee with an explicit rationale grounded in learning evidence. The committee's role is to assess whether the proposal is within the institution's strategic mandate, adequately resourced, and operationally feasible — not to redesign it. When a committee believes a design decision is wrong, the process is to return it to the Design Authority with specific concerns, not to revise it by vote. The distinction between design and governance decisions is documented and applied consistently.

**Result — what the institution and its learners gain**
Curriculum design is made at the quality level that learning expertise makes possible. Institutional politics do not determine pedagogical choices. Governance committees are freed from design decisions they are not equipped to make and can focus on accountability decisions they are. The institution's curriculum reflects its learning strategy rather than its political equilibrium.
