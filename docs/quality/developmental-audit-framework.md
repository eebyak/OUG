## Peer-Based Self-Assessment for an Institution That Holds Itself to a Higher Standard Than Accreditation Requires

> **Why this document exists.** Standard accreditation checks whether the OUG meets minimum input norms — sufficient qualified staff, adequate infrastructure, compliant procedures. This is necessary and the OUG designs for it. It is not sufficient. An institution that meets only the minimum norm cannot demonstrate whether it is genuinely achieving its purpose, whether it is developing over time, or whether the learners it was founded to serve are actually being served. The OUG was designed to be accountable to a higher standard than compliance.
>
> The Developmental Audit is that higher standard. It is a periodic, peer-based process in which the OUG assesses itself against its own defined purpose — and submits that self-assessment to external peers for challenge and validation. The audit produces not a compliance certificate but a Development Report: an honest account of where the institution stands, where it is falling short, and what it commits to doing about it. The audit cycle is published. The Development Reports are public. The OUG's accountability is visible.

---

## I. What a Developmental Audit Is — and Is Not

*Distinction from standard accreditation*

Standard accreditation and the Developmental Audit address different questions. They are not competing — they are complementary. The OUG conducts both and they serve different functions in its quality architecture.

| | Standard Accreditation | Developmental Audit |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary question** | Are minimum input norms met? | Is the institution achieving what it said it would achieve? |
| **Criteria** | Externally defined, universal | Self-defined purpose, reviewed by peers |
| **Evidence focus** | Inputs — staff numbers, infrastructure, procedures | Outcomes — learning, development, learner experience |
| **Reviewer role** | Compliance assessor | Peer challenger and development partner |
| **Output** | Accreditation decision — pass or fail | Development Report — findings and commitments |
| **Publication** | Accreditation status | Full Development Report, publicly accessible |
| **Frequency** | Every five to seven years | Every three years |
| **Primary purpose** | External legitimacy for degree-awarding | Internal development and public accountability |

> Accreditation asks: are you allowed to operate? The Developmental Audit asks: are you doing what you said you would do, for the people you said you would serve? The first question protects the public from bad institutions. The second question is what good institutions ask of themselves.

## II. The Audit Cycle

*Three-year rhythm · Four phases · One published report*

The Developmental Audit runs on a three-year cycle — aligned with the OUG's strategic planning cycle but offset by eighteen months so that audit findings inform the next strategic cycle rather than coinciding with it. Each cycle has four phases. The entire cycle from Self-Assessment initiation to Development Plan publication takes approximately nine months.

### Phase 1 · Months 1–3 · Self-Assessment
*Led by: OUG — led by Rector, owned by institution*

The OUG produces its Self-Assessment Document: a structured account of what it said it would achieve (drawn from its Role Statement and strategic commitments), what evidence it has that it is achieving it, and an honest account of where it is falling short. The Self-Assessment addresses five domains (see Section III). It is not a marketing document. It is the institution's best honest account of itself, written for people who will challenge it.

### Phase 2 · Months 4–6 · Peer Panel Review
*Led by: External Peer Panel — convened by Hochschulrat (G1)*

A Peer Panel of five people reads the Self-Assessment, conducts a two-day site visit (which may be hybrid or fully digital), and produces a Panel Assessment against three questions: Are the OUG's goals legitimate and ambitious enough? Is the evidence for achievement credible? Where should development focus? The Panel does not produce a pass/fail judgment. It produces an honest external perspective on where the institution stands relative to where it said it would stand.

### Phase 3 · Month 7 · Institutional Response
*Led by: OUG — led by Rector with Senate and Hochschulrat*

The OUG reads the Panel Assessment and produces an Institutional Response: a document that accepts, challenges, or contextualises the Panel's findings — and commits to specific development actions with named owners and timelines for any finding the institution accepts. The Response is the institution's public commitment. It is not a rebuttal exercise. Where the Panel is right, the institution says so and acts. Where the institution believes the Panel is wrong, it explains why with evidence.

### Phase 4 · Months 8–9 · Development Report Publication
*Led by: Hochschulrat (G1) — owns publication*

The Self-Assessment, Panel Assessment, and Institutional Response are compiled into the Development Report and published on the OUG's website. The Report is fully public — not summarised, not redacted. Learners, partners, the ministry, and the public can read what the institution found, what the peers found, and what the institution committed to doing. In the next audit cycle, the first section of the new Self-Assessment accounts for progress against the previous cycle's commitments.

## III. The Five Self-Assessment Domains

*What the OUG assesses itself against*

The Self-Assessment is structured around five domains. Each domain has a set of standing questions that the institution addresses in every cycle. The questions do not change — they are the stable reference points against which development over time can be measured. The answers, evidence, and commitments change with each cycle.

### Domain 1 — Purpose and Learner Reach

1. Who are the learners we said we would serve? Are we reaching them — in what numbers, from what backgrounds, with what prior educational experience?
2. What is the evidence that €25/ECTS is reaching learners who would not have been able to access higher education at market prices?
3. Where are we not reaching the learners we said we would reach, and what structural barriers explain the gap?
4. Has our Role Statement been reviewed in this cycle? Does it still accurately describe the gap we fill in the European higher education ecosystem?

### Domain 2 — Learning Quality and Credential Integrity

1. What is the evidence that learners who complete our modules are developing the competencies the Europass credentials certify?
2. What did the Stichprobe (quality sampling) find this cycle? What patterns are visible across partners and modules?
3. Where is the gap between intended and achieved learning outcomes largest? What structural explanation do we have for that gap?
4. Are our Bloom-level progressions (Year 1 average 2.1 → Year 3 average 3.0+) being achieved in practice?

*(Amended by [GP-3 Amendment](amendment-longitudinal-outcome-tracking.md) to include a standing question on the LOI-1/LOI-2 longitudinal outcome data.)*

### Domain 3 — Governance Health and Adaptability

1. Which governance structures have been reviewed this cycle? What was the outcome — confirmed, reformed, or retired?
2. Has the counterbalancing between G1–G4 functioned as designed? Were there boundary disputes, and how were they resolved?
3. Are Proactive Capacity thresholds being met by governance role-holders? What does the quarterly audit data show?
4. Where has the governance architecture been tested by changed circumstances, and how did it respond?

### Domain 4 — Partnership Quality and Ecosystem Health

1. How many partners are in the federation? What is the distribution of quality signal data across partners?
2. Have any partners been suspended or exited this cycle? What did that process reveal about the partnership framework?
3. Are the Task Architecture and the Joint Governance mechanisms functioning as designed at the OUG/partner boundary?
4. Is the Mission Hub Permeability Architecture producing genuine cross-Hub collaboration? What is the evidence?

### Domain 5 — Institutional Development and Commitments

1. What were the development commitments from the previous audit cycle? Which have been fulfilled, which are in progress, which have not been addressed?
2. What is the honest account of where the institution is further from its design principles today than it was three years ago?
3. What is one thing the institution has learned about itself in this cycle that was not anticipated at founding?
4. What would we design differently if we were founding the institution today?

## IV. Peer Panel Composition

*Who reviews — and on what basis*

The Peer Panel is convened by the Hochschulrat (G1) independently of the Rector and the Senate. This independence is the structural guarantee of the panel's credibility. The panel is composed of five people selected for expertise directly relevant to the OUG's defined purpose — not for generic accreditation experience. The selection criteria exclude people with financial, professional, or personal conflicts of interest with the OUG.

| Panel Role | Required Expertise | Number | Appointed By |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Panel Chair** | Senior academic with experience in higher education governance or institutional design. Not affiliated with any OUG partner institution. | 1 | Hochschulrat nominates; Senate confirms |
| **Learner Experience Expert** | Researcher or practitioner with expertise in adult learning, non-traditional learner access, or lifelong learning policy. Ideally with personal experience of non-traditional higher education pathways. | 1 | Hochschulrat appoints on Senate recommendation |
| **Peer Institution Representative** | Senior academic or administrator from a European institution with a comparable mission — federated, open, or adult-learner focused. Brings direct practice knowledge of similar challenges. | 1 | Hochschulrat appoints |
| **Quality Assurance Expert** | Person with direct experience in European higher education quality assurance — not as a compliance inspector but as someone who understands what genuine quality development looks like. May be from an accreditation agency or from a university quality office. | 1 | Hochschulrat appoints |
| **Student or Learner Representative** | A current or recent OUG learner, or a representative of a learner advocacy organisation. Brings the perspective the institution was founded to serve. Has full panel standing — not an observer. | 1 | OUG Senate nominates; Hochschulrat confirms |

Panel members serve for one audit cycle. They may serve a second cycle if the Hochschulrat judges continuity valuable, but not a third. They receive an honorarium for their work, set at a level that reflects the genuine professional commitment required. They sign a conflict of interest declaration before appointment is confirmed. A panel member who develops a conflict of interest during the cycle declares it immediately and is replaced.

## V. The Development Report

*Structure, publication, and accountability*

### What the Development Report contains

The Development Report is a single published document containing three parts in full: the OUG's Self-Assessment, the Peer Panel's Panel Assessment, and the OUG's Institutional Response including development commitments. The three parts are published together, unredacted, so that readers can assess the quality of the institution's self-knowledge and the integrity of its response to external challenge.

**Part 1 — The Self-Assessment.** The OUG's structured account across the five domains. Maximum length: 60 pages. Appendices may contain supporting evidence. The Self-Assessment is approved by the Rector and noted by the Senate and Hochschulrat before submission to the Panel.

**Part 2 — The Panel Assessment.** The Peer Panel's findings, organised around three questions: Are the goals legitimate and ambitious? Is the evidence credible? Where should development focus? The Panel Assessment does not produce a score or a ranking. It produces qualitative findings with supporting reasoning. Maximum length: 20 pages. The Panel Chair signs the Assessment on behalf of the full panel.

**Part 3 — The Institutional Response and Development Commitments.** The OUG's response to the Panel Assessment, organised finding by finding. For each finding: accept, challenge with evidence, or contextualise. For each accepted finding: a named development commitment with a named owner and a defined timeline. Maximum length: 20 pages. The Institutional Response is approved by the Rector, the Senate, and the Hochschulrat before publication.

### Publication and access

The Development Report is published on the OUG's public website within thirty days of the Hochschulrat's approval of the Institutional Response. It remains publicly accessible permanently — previous cycles' reports are archived alongside current ones. The OUG's website maintains a Development Tracker: a live summary of outstanding development commitments, their owners, their timelines, and their current status. The Tracker is updated quarterly by the Rector's office and reviewed by the Hochschulrat at its annual strategic session.

### Accountability for commitments

Development commitments made in the Institutional Response are standing items in the OUG's governance calendar. The Hochschulrat reviews progress against all outstanding commitments at its annual strategic session. The Rector reports on any commitment that is behind schedule and proposes either a revised timeline or a revised approach. Commitments that are not met without explanation are escalated to the Hochschulrat for a formal response. In the next audit cycle, the first section of the new Self-Assessment provides an account of every commitment from the previous cycle — fulfilled, in progress, revised, or not completed with explanation.

> A commitment published and then ignored is worse than no commitment at all. The Development Tracker exists because transparency is only accountability when it is continuous, not only at audit time.

## VI. Relationship to Standard Accreditation and Quality Assurance

*How the Developmental Audit fits the wider quality architecture*

The Developmental Audit sits within a quality architecture that has three layers. Each layer serves a different function and addresses a different accountability question. The layers are complementary and non-duplicative.

| Layer | Instrument | Frequency | Question addressed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | Stichprobe + Partner Signal Reports + Platform Analytics | Ongoing / Quarterly | Is learning happening at module level across the partner network? |
| **Periodic internal** | OKR Review + Bug Protocol + Governance Review Calendar | Annual | Are we achieving our strategic objectives and is our governance working? |
| **Periodic external** | Developmental Audit | Every 3 years | Are we achieving our purpose and developing toward it? |
| **Formal compliance** | Programme Accreditation / System Accreditation | Every 5–7 years | Do we meet minimum standards for degree-awarding? |

The Developmental Audit does not replace the Stichprobe, the OKR cycle, or formal accreditation. It draws on the evidence produced by all three. The Self-Assessment uses Stichprobe findings, OKR outcomes, and governance review results as evidence in its five domain assessments. The Developmental Audit is the synthesis layer — the moment at which the institution steps back from the continuous and periodic data and asks the larger question: taken together, what does all of this tell us about whether we are becoming the institution we said we would be?

## VII. First Audit Schedule and Founding Commitment

*When the first audit occurs and what it must address*

The first Developmental Audit of the OUG will be conducted at the end of the first operational year — not at the end of the first three-year cycle. This is a founding commitment, not a standard schedule, and it reflects the OUG's recognition that a new institution needs external challenge sooner rather than later.

The first audit is necessarily different from subsequent cycles. The OUG will not have three years of outcomes data. It will have: its founding commitments, one year of operational evidence, and the accumulated Design Signature commitments from the founding period. The Peer Panel for the first audit is therefore briefed differently — it is assessing whether the institution's founding design is being implemented as committed, and whether the first year of operation has revealed design gaps that were not anticipated.

The first audit must specifically address the following questions, in addition to the standing domain questions:

- Has the Design Signature been updated to reflect the first year of operation, and are the commitments recorded in the founding version being actioned?
- Which of the governance gap closure actions specified before founding (Task Architecture, Bounded Role protections, Governance Review Calendar, Mission Hub Permeability Architecture) have been implemented, and what is the honest account of those that have not?
- What has the first year of partner relationships revealed about the Task Architecture and the Partnership Framework that was not visible in the founding design?
- Has the Developmental Audit process itself — this document — proved adequate for its purpose, or does it need amendment based on the experience of the first cycle?

That last question is self-referential by design. A quality process that cannot improve itself is a process that has already started to calcify. The first audit cycle is the Developmental Audit's own first stress test.

## VIII. The Commitment This Document Makes

This document is a governance commitment, not a quality assurance procedure. Procedures can be suspended, revised away, or quietly abandoned when they become inconvenient. A governance commitment — published, reviewed by the Hochschulrat, and referenced in the Design Signature — is harder to abandon without institutional acknowledgment.

The OUG commits to subjecting itself to a Developmental Audit on a three-year cycle beginning at the end of its first operational year. It commits to publishing the full Development Report. It commits to maintaining a public Development Tracker of outstanding commitments. It commits to making the first section of every future Self-Assessment an honest account of progress against the previous cycle's commitments.

These commitments are not made because they are required. Standard accreditation does not require them. The OUG makes them because it was founded to serve learners who the existing system has decided are not worth the cost of genuine accountability. The least the OUG can do — for those learners, and for the credibility of its founding claims — is hold itself to the standard it is asking the system to adopt.

> The institution that does not audit itself will eventually be audited by circumstances — and circumstances are neither peer-based nor developmental.
