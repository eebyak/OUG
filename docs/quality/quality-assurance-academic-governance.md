## How Academic Quality Is Defined, Protected, Monitored, and Improved at the OUG

## 0. Purpose and Scope

The Quality Assurance and Academic Governance Concept of the Open University of Germany defines how academic quality is ensured, monitored, and continuously improved within a modular, skill-based, and lifelong-learning-oriented educational model. It operationalises the institution's constitutional commitment to academic quality while enabling flexible learning pathways, stackable micro-credentials, and multiple routes toward recognised academic qualifications.

This document applies to all academic learning and assessment activities of the OUG, including modules, micro-credentials, skill profiles, and degree qualifications. It does not prescribe individual curricula or programme structures. Instead, it defines the rules, responsibilities, and safeguards through which academic coherence, integrity, and comparability are maintained across diverse and non-linear learning trajectories.

The concept is aligned with the Governance Architecture (G1–G4 layers) and supports accreditation by making academic responsibility, quality standards, and improvement mechanisms transparent and auditable.

## 1. Understanding of Academic Quality

### 1.1 Quality as Academic Integrity and Coherent Competence Development

At the OUG, academic quality is understood as the integrity, coherence, and validity of demonstrated competences, regardless of how or where learning has taken place. Quality is not defined by adherence to fixed curricula or cohort-based study paths, but by the reliable achievement and assessment of clearly defined learning outcomes aligned with recognised academic and professional standards.

This understanding enables modular and stackable learning, multiple learning trajectories toward comparable qualifications, inclusion of lifelong learners with heterogeneous backgrounds, and recognition of prior and non-formal learning.

### 1.2 Quality Across Multiple Levels

Given the modular and skill-based model, quality assurance operates simultaneously at three interconnected levels:

**Module and Micro-credential Level.** Each learning unit is governed by defined learning outcomes, assessment criteria, workload expectations, and academic level. Quality assurance ensures that micro-credentials are academically robust and independently meaningful.

**Skill Profile Level.** Skill profiles define coherent combinations of competences contributing to multiple degree pathways. Quality assurance ensures internal coherence, appropriate progression, alignment with qualification frameworks, and transparency of how skills combine into higher-level qualifications.

**Qualification Level.** Degrees are awarded based on the validated aggregation of skills and competences. Quality assurance ensures that degree-level standards are met regardless of the individual learning path, preserving equivalence, academic depth, and credibility.

This multi-level approach prevents fragmentation while preserving flexibility.

## 2. Academic Governance Structure for Quality Assurance

Academic quality at the OUG is governed through Governance Layer G3 (Academic and Quality Governance), which holds final authority over academic standards, assessment frameworks, curriculum architecture, and quality assurance mechanisms. G3 is constitutionally insulated from financial and executive authority — academic standards cannot be overridden by G2 on financial grounds.

### 2.1 Senate / Community Assembly

The Senate, operating in its constitutional identity as the Community Assembly, exercises overarching responsibility for academic standards and quality principles. It defines institution-wide academic standards, approves assessment frameworks and recognition rules, safeguards academic integrity against undue strategic or financial pressure, and ensures consistency across Mission Hubs and skill profiles. The Senate receives and acts upon the annual Academic Quality Report described in Section 5.

### 2.2 The Quality Commission

> **New in this version.** The Quality Commission is the named cross-programme quality body at the OUG. It holds the mandate that equivalent bodies hold at established German universities — coordinating quality development across programmes, comparing standards, and issuing action mandates. Its creation responds directly to the requirement that quality governance be assigned to a specific accountable body, not distributed implicitly across governance layers.

The Quality Commission is a standing committee of the Senate, operating within G3. It is the primary operational body for cross-programme quality assurance at the OUG. Its mandate covers:

- Cross-programme quality development — coordinating quality standards across Mission Hubs and skill profiles, with particular attention to portfolio coherence, equivalent standards across parallel programmes, and responses to acute quality concerns.
- Teaching and examination regulations — preparing learning-related rules (Studien- und Prüfungsordnungen) and forwarding recommendations to the Senate and the Presidium.
- Recommendations on examination practice, academic standards, and study operations for the Senate.
- Evaluation of consolidated quality and examination reports across Mission Hubs, alongside statistical data, graduate surveys, and results from accompanying studies and projects.
- Cross-programme quality comparison — benchmarking quality outcomes across programmes and partner delivery sites on the basis of quality report findings.
- Determining the annual examination sample (Stichprobe) — see Section 3.7.
- Issuing action mandates to Mission Hubs and Programme Councils where the evaluation process identifies quality improvements required.
- Issuing advisory notices to individual delivery partners where deviations from quality standards are identified at a specific site.
- Informing Senate governance bodies of quality assurance outcomes.
- Monitoring implementation of previous recommendations and mandated measures.
- Reflecting on the effectiveness of the QM system as a whole and developing proposals for its further development — see Section 10.

The Quality Commission meets at minimum each semester. It reports annually to the Senate in writing. Its membership comprises: the Dean (Academic) as chair, two Programme Directors nominated by Mission Hubs, the Research Director, two student representatives from the Senate, and two industry partner representatives from the Strategic Alignment Board. The inclusion of industry partner representatives is a structural expression of the OUG's *neue Dualität* model: industry members are full institutional participants with legitimate governance rights, not external advisors. Their perspective on graduate competence and professional relevance is integral to quality judgement, not supplementary to it.

### 2.3 Mission Hubs

Mission Hubs are responsible for academic quality within their disciplinary or mission-oriented scope. They develop and steward skill profiles, ensure coherence across modules and micro-credentials, oversee alignment with academic and professional standards, and initiate quality improvement measures where needed. Mission Hubs operate as the primary sites of academic sense-making and innovation, while remaining bound by institution-wide standards and subject to Quality Commission mandates.

### 2.4 Deans as Academic Stewards

Deans act as Academic Stewards within Mission Hubs. Their role is not managerial control but stewardship of academic coherence, assessment integrity, and quality development. They coordinate quality assurance activities, facilitate peer review and academic reflection, and ensure that modular innovation does not compromise academic depth. The Dean (Academic) chairs the Quality Commission and is personally accountable for the annual Academic Quality Report.

### 2.5 Programme and Subject Councils

Programme and Subject Councils apply academic standards at the level of specific modules, micro-credentials, and skill combinations. They ensure validity of learning outcomes, appropriateness of assessment methods, and consistency of academic level. These bodies operate under delegated authority and within clearly defined quality frameworks. They are the first level of response when individual module quality concerns arise.

## 3. Academic Standards and Assessment Framework

The academic standards and assessment framework defines how learning outcomes are specified, assessed, recognised, and combined across modular and non-linear learning pathways. It ensures academic integrity, comparability, and trustworthiness while enabling flexible participation, stackable micro-credentials, and multiple routes toward recognised qualifications. This framework applies to all learning and assessment activities, independent of delivery format, learning context, or learner status.

### 3.1 Learning Outcomes as the Primary Unit of Quality

Academic standards at the OUG are defined at the level of explicit learning outcomes, expressed as demonstrable competences rather than time-bound curricular exposure. Learning outcomes are discipline-grounded and academically validated, mapped to recognised qualification levels, expressed in a way that allows assessment independent of teaching context, and reusable across multiple skill profiles and degree pathways.

Learning outcomes are specified for individual modules and micro-credentials, aggregated skill profiles, and qualification-level competence requirements. This approach ensures that academic quality is attached to what a learner can demonstrably do, not to where or how learning occurred.

### 3.2 Standardisation and Cross-Field Alignment of Skills

To ensure transparency, comparability, and interoperability, learning outcomes and skill descriptions are aligned with the European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations (ESCO) framework. ESCO alignment provides a shared semantic reference layer that supports quality assurance across modular and interdisciplinary offerings. It does not replace academic judgement but enables standardisation of skill language, facilitation of interdisciplinary profiles, and compatibility with European qualification frameworks.

### 3.3 Prerequisite Skills and Progressive Competence Development

Academic quality in a modular and stackable system depends critically on the transparent definition and enforcement of prerequisite skills. For each module, micro-credential, or skill unit, the following are explicitly defined: required prerequisite competences, assumed academic level and prior knowledge, and dependencies on other skills or learning outcomes. Prerequisite definitions are subject to academic governance and reviewed regularly to ensure they remain appropriate, inclusive, and aligned with evolving disciplinary standards.

### 3.4 Metacognitive Skills as a Core Academic Standard

The OUG recognises metacognitive skills as a foundational component of academic quality: the ability to reflect on one's own learning, self-regulation of learning processes, critical evaluation of sources and methods, transfer of knowledge across contexts, and responsible use of digital and AI-based tools. These skills are explicitly articulated in learning outcome frameworks, progressively developed across skill profiles, and assessed through appropriate reflective and performance-based methods.

### 3.5 Separation of Teaching and Assessment

A core principle of the OUG's quality framework is the separation of teaching and assessment. Assessment criteria are defined independently of teaching formats, assessors are academically qualified and operate under defined standards, assessment decisions are evidence-based and auditable, and learning may occur through institutional offerings, external providers, self-directed study, or professional experience.

### 3.6 Assessment Validity, Reliability, and Transparency

Assessment methods are selected to validly and reliably measure defined learning outcomes. For each assessed learning outcome, assessment criteria and performance levels are explicitly defined, alignment with prerequisite skills is ensured, and transparency for learners is guaranteed. Assessment frameworks are reviewed regularly to ensure fairness, consistency, and alignment with evolving academic standards.

### 3.7 Annual Examination Sample (Stichprobe)

> **New in this version.** The Stichprobe is an annual independent review of a defined sample of assessed work. It is the operational mechanism through which assessment independence is verified — not assumed. Without it, the separation of teaching and assessment remains a principle without a check.

Each academic year, the Quality Commission determines the scope and composition of an annual examination sample. The sample includes a minimum of 5% of all assessed module submissions across all active programmes, selected to cover: at least two modules per Mission Hub, modules at different academic levels (introductory, intermediate, advanced), and at least one capstone or thesis per active programme.

Sampled assessments are reviewed by an independent examiner — an academic not involved in the delivery of the relevant module — appointed by the Quality Commission. The review assesses: alignment between stated learning outcomes and assessment tasks, consistency of grading against stated criteria, evidence of assessment independence, and appropriateness of the academic level achieved. Findings are reported to the Quality Commission and included in the annual Academic Quality Report. Where the Stichprobe reveals systematic concerns — inconsistent grading, misaligned outcomes, assessment tasks that do not test the stated outcomes — the Quality Commission issues a formal action mandate to the relevant Mission Hub or Programme Council.

> **Status.** The Stichprobe is binding. It is not a voluntary peer review exercise. The Quality Commission is responsible for its execution; the Dean (Academic) is accountable for ensuring it happens every year without exception.

## 4. Curriculum Architecture and Coherence

The OUG does not organise learning around fixed, cohort-based curricula. Instead, it operates with a curriculum architecture that defines rules of coherence, progression, and equivalence across modular learning units, skill profiles, and qualifications. Curriculum coherence is achieved by design, not by prescription.

### 4.1 Curriculum as Architecture, Not Sequence

Curricula at the OUG are understood as architectural frameworks rather than linear study plans. They define which competences belong together conceptually and academically, how learning outcomes relate across levels of complexity, how prerequisite skills structure progression, and how modular units can be meaningfully combined. This architectural approach allows multiple valid learning trajectories to lead to equivalent academic outcomes while preventing arbitrary or incoherent combinations.

### 4.2 Modular Structure and Academic Levels

All learning units are positioned within a clearly defined academic level structure aligned with recognised qualification frameworks. Each unit specifies its academic level, its contribution to skill profiles, its prerequisite competences, and its potential role in qualification pathways. This ensures that stackability does not collapse academic levels or blur distinctions between introductory, intermediate, and advanced learning.

### 4.3 Skill Profiles as Coherence Anchors

Skill profiles function as intermediate coherence layers between individual learning units and formal qualifications. They define academically meaningful combinations of competences that reflect disciplinary or interdisciplinary logic, support professional and societal relevance, and can contribute to multiple qualification pathways. Skill profiles are academically governed artefacts stewarded by Mission Hubs, preventing fragmentation while enabling reuse and flexibility.

### 4.4 Multiple Pathways to Equivalent Qualifications

Degrees are awarded based on the validated aggregation of competences, not on adherence to a single predefined curriculum. The curriculum architecture specifies required competence domains, minimum and maximum proportions of certain skill types, rules for progression and depth, and constraints preventing over-specialisation or excessive dispersion. This allows learners to enter at different points, combine learning across contexts, and pursue personalised trajectories while ensuring all graduates meet equivalent academic standards.

### 4.5 Interdisciplinarity and Boundary Management

The curriculum architecture explicitly supports interdisciplinarity while safeguarding disciplinary depth. Rules are defined for combining competences across Mission Hubs, recognising interdisciplinary skill profiles, and ensuring that core disciplinary foundations are not bypassed. Boundary rules ensure that interdisciplinarity enriches rather than dilutes academic quality.

### 4.6 Responsibility for Curriculum Coherence

Responsibility for curriculum architecture and coherence is distributed but clearly assigned. Mission Hubs steward coherence within and across skill profiles. Academic Stewards (Deans) ensure alignment with institutional standards and qualification frameworks. The Quality Commission validates architectural rules across programmes and intervenes where coherence is at risk. Curriculum coherence is continuously monitored and adjusted as part of academic governance, not treated as a static design decision.

## 5. Quality Monitoring and Evidence

Quality assurance at the OUG is grounded in systematic evidence collection, interpretation, and use. Evidence is used not for compliance reporting alone, but as a core input for academic governance, curriculum stewardship, and continuous improvement. The OUG explicitly distinguishes between evidence for academic quality and operational or strategic performance indicators. This section addresses the former.

### 5.1 Principles of Evidence-Based Quality Assurance

Quality monitoring follows four guiding principles. **Relevance:** evidence collected must be meaningful for academic decision-making and improvement. **Multi-dimensionality:** no single indicator is sufficient to capture academic quality. **Proportionality:** evidence requirements are appropriate to the level and risk of the academic unit. **Interpretation over automation:** data informs judgement; it does not replace it.

### 5.2 Evidence Sources

Evidence is collected from four sources, each providing a distinct and necessary perspective:

**Learner evidence.** Structured feedback on learning and assessment clarity, reflective artefacts, feedforward evaluation outputs, progression patterns across entry points. Treated as qualitative evidence interpreted alongside academic judgement.

**Academic staff evidence.** Peer review of assessment tasks and grading practices, module-level outcome distribution analysis, assessment alignment reviews.

**Industry partner evidence.** Graduate competence assessments from industry partners participating in governance bodies, employer surveys, feedback from Strategic Alignment Board members on professional relevance of programme outcomes. Industry partners are full members of quality governance at OUG — their perspective is structural, not supplementary.

**External academic evidence.** External peer review, participation in national or international benchmarking, Stichprobe findings from independent examiners, results from accreditation and audit processes.

### 5.3 The Annual Academic Quality Report

The Dean (Academic) produces an Annual Academic Quality Report covering all active programmes and Mission Hubs. The report is submitted to the Quality Commission each semester for review and to the Senate annually for governance action. It contains:

- Module and programme completion rates, grade distribution analyses, and progression data by entry pathway.
- Stichprobe findings from the annual examination sample.
- Learner feedback summaries and analysis of recurring concerns.
- Industry partner input on graduate competence and professional relevance.
- External peer review findings where available.
- Cross-programme and cross-Hub quality comparisons.
- Status of implementation of previous Quality Commission action mandates.
- Proposals for quality improvement where evidence identifies systemic concerns.

The report is a governance document. The Quality Commission does not merely receive it — it evaluates it, issues action mandates where improvement is required, and tracks implementation of previous mandates. The Senate reviews the annual report and satisfies itself that the quality system is functioning.

### 5.4 Transparency and Documentation

Decisions informed by quality evidence are documented and communicated within the governance system. The Quality Commission maintains a register of action mandates, responses, and implementation status. This register is available to the Senate and to accreditation bodies on request. Transparency ensures traceability for accreditation, institutional learning, and trust among academic staff, learners, and partners.

> **Status.** The Annual Academic Quality Report and the action mandate register together constitute the primary accreditation evidence for quality monitoring at OUG. They demonstrate that quality assurance is evidence-based, governance-anchored, and produces real consequences.

## 6. Continuous Improvement and Developmental Loops

Quality assurance at the OUG is not conceived as a static verification mechanism but as a continuous, developmental process. Improvement is understood to occur simultaneously at three interrelated levels: learner development, curriculum architecture, and teaching and assessment practice. These levels are governed through explicit improvement loops that are iterative, evidence-informed, and academically stewarded.

### 6.1 Continuous Improvement as a Developmental Process

Continuous improvement focuses on enabling learners to increasingly take responsibility for their learning, refining curriculum architecture to support diverse learning trajectories, and supporting teachers and assessors in reflective, adaptive practice. Improvement loops operate iteratively, allowing the system to learn from experience and adjust structures, sequencing, and support mechanisms accordingly.

### 6.2 Learner Agency and Metacognitive Development

A core dimension of academic quality is the progressive development of learner agency and metacognitive competence. Learners are expected to grow in their ability to plan and regulate their learning, understand and manage prerequisite competences, reflect on learning strategies and outcomes, transfer skills across domains and contexts, and make informed choices about learning pathways. Learner agency is treated as an academically governed developmental trajectory, not as a precondition or assumed capability.

### 6.3 Feedforward-Oriented Evaluation

Improvement processes are anchored in feedforward-oriented evaluation. Unlike traditional feedback models focused primarily on past performance, feedforward evaluation is oriented toward future learning and development. It makes expectations and next steps explicit, supports learners in identifying developmental priorities, informs decisions about prerequisite readiness and sequencing, and provides guidance for both learners and teachers on how learning paths can be optimised. Feedforward formats are integrated into assessment and reflection processes without compromising assessment independence.

### 6.4 Dual Improvement Loops

Continuous improvement operates through two tightly coupled loops.

**The Learner Development Loop.** Collects evidence on learning outcomes, prerequisites, and metacognitive engagement; learners engage in structured reflection; learning paths, sequencing, and support structures are adjusted; learners re-engage at a higher level of agency and competence.

**The Teaching and Assessment Practice Loop.** Reviews evidence on assessment validity, learner progression, and prerequisite alignment; teachers and assessors reflect on learning design and evaluation methods; practices are adapted to better support diverse learner trajectories; improved practices feed back into curriculum architecture and quality standards.

### 6.5 Curriculum Adaptation

Continuous improvement processes explicitly inform curriculum architecture and sequencing rules. Evidence from learner progression and feedforward evaluation is used to refine prerequisite definitions, adjust recommended learning sequences, identify common bottlenecks or overload points, and improve the alignment between skill profiles and qualification requirements. This enables the curriculum architecture to support learners from diverse backgrounds without compromising academic depth or coherence.

### 6.6 Governance of Continuous Improvement

Responsibility for continuous improvement is clearly assigned within G3. Mission Hubs analyse evidence and initiate improvements within their scope. Academic Stewards (Deans) ensure coherence across modules and skill profiles and facilitate reflective processes. The Quality Commission validates adjustments across programmes and intervenes where systemic quality risks are identified. Improvement processes are documented, reviewed, and subject to governance oversight to ensure transparency and institutional learning.

### 6.7 Safeguards Against Instrumentalisation

Developmental indicators are not reduced to summative metrics. Learner reflection is not used for ranking or exclusion. Improvement processes prioritise support and progression over selection. Academic judgement remains central in interpreting evidence. These safeguards ensure that continuous improvement strengthens academic quality and learner empowerment rather than introducing new forms of pressure or compliance.

## 7. Participation in Quality Assurance

Participation is an integral component of quality assurance at the OUG. Three groups participate in quality-related processes at defined stages and levels.

**Learners** contribute through structured feedback, reflective artefacts, and feedforward-oriented evaluation formats. Their input informs both immediate improvement actions and longer-term curriculum and sequencing adaptations. Student representatives hold seats on the Quality Commission with full participation rights.

**Academic staff** participate through peer review, reflective practice, and governance roles within Mission Hubs, Programme Councils, and the Quality Commission.

**Industry partners** participate as full members of the Quality Commission and the Strategic Alignment Board. Their role in quality assurance reflects the OUG's *neue Dualität* model: industry does not advise on quality from the outside — it participates in quality governance from within, with the same rights and responsibilities as other governance members. Their contribution focuses on professional relevance, graduate competence assessment, and the alignment of skill profiles with evolving labour market and societal needs.

Participation in quality assurance is governed, purposeful, and linked to defined improvement processes. It complements, but does not replace, academic decision authority.

## 8. Safeguards for Academic Integrity

Academic integrity is a foundational requirement of the OUG's quality system and is protected through explicit structural safeguards:

- Clear separation of teaching and assessment — no module is assessed exclusively by its own teacher without peer oversight.
- Academically governed standards and assessment frameworks — defined in this document and protected by G3 constitutional independence.
- Protection of academic decisions from strategic or financial pressure — G3 authority over academic standards cannot be overridden by G2 on financial grounds.
- Annual Stichprobe — independent verification that assessment independence is actual, not merely declared.
- Transparent documentation of quality-related decisions — Quality Commission mandate register and Annual Academic Quality Report.
- Escalation mechanisms — where integrity risks are identified by the Stichprobe or the quality reporting cycle, the Quality Commission issues formal mandates with defined response timelines.

These safeguards ensure that flexibility, modularity, and innovation do not compromise academic rigour, trust, or equivalence of qualifications.

## 9. Relationship to Accreditation and External Review

The Quality Assurance and Academic Governance Concept is designed to support both programme-level and system-level accreditation. It provides a transparent and auditable framework demonstrating clear academic responsibility, coherent standards across diverse learning paths, continuous quality monitoring and improvement, and alignment with national and international qualification frameworks.

External reviews, accreditation processes, and audits are treated as opportunities for institutional learning and calibration rather than isolated compliance exercises. The Quality Commission prepares the institutional self-evaluation for accreditation processes and is the primary governance body accountable to external reviewers for quality assurance outcomes.

## 10. Review and Evolution of the Quality Assurance System

> **New in this version.** The QM system explicitly reviews itself. This is not automatic in most quality frameworks — institutions routinely review programmes but not the review processes themselves. At OUG, the Quality Commission holds an explicit mandate to assess whether the QM system is working as a system, and to propose revisions when it is not.

The quality assurance system itself is subject to periodic review and development on a two-year cycle, coordinated by the Quality Commission and reported to the Senate. The review assesses:

- The effectiveness of the Stichprobe — is the sample design producing useful findings? Are action mandates following from it leading to real improvement?
- The effectiveness of the Annual Academic Quality Report cycle — is the evidence collected the right evidence? Is it being used in governance decisions?
- The functioning of the dual improvement loops — are learner development and teaching practice actually improving as a result of the evidence cycle?
- The appropriateness of standards and sequencing rules — do prerequisite structures remain educationally sound?
- The quality of learner agency development support — are learners actually developing the metacognitive competences the framework aims to produce?
- The participation quality of industry partners in quality governance — are their contributions substantive and integrated into quality decisions, or nominal?
- The alignment of the system with institutional mission and the OUG's constitutional principles.

Revisions to the quality assurance framework proposed by the Quality Commission follow established governance processes and require Senate approval. Constitutional precedence of academic quality is preserved in all revisions.

> The purpose of every element of this framework is the same: to protect the learner from receiving a credential that does not represent what it claims. Everything else — the Quality Commission, the Stichprobe, the improvement loops, the industry participation — is in service of that single obligation.
