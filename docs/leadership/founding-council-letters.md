## Letter 1 — Governance & Organizational Culture

*[Name TBD] · March 2026*

Dear [Name],

I am writing to you because I believe you are one of a very small number of people who would immediately understand what I am trying to build — and why it matters now.

The Open University of Germany is a proposal for a new kind of public university: one that does not replace existing institutions, but makes their teaching accessible to the people who currently fall through the gaps — working professionals, caregivers, career changers, people in rural regions, immigrants building new lives. In Germany alone, we estimate over 100,000 people want to study but cannot, not from lack of ability, but from lack of a system designed for their lives.

The architecture I have designed addresses this through three principles that are unusual in their combination: degree authority held centrally by OUG, teaching delivered through partner universities who retain full academic sovereignty, and quality assured through competency-based module standards and the separation of teaching from examination. The financial model reaches break-even in Year 8 through ECTS-based partner revenue sharing and phased state co-funding — without permanent dependency on project grants.

I am not writing to ask for your endorsement of a finished idea. I am writing to invite you to help shape it.

**Why I am writing to you specifically.** Work on organizational theory in higher education — in particular the argument that institutions are living systems of people with histories and loyalties, not machines to be optimized — is the most honest description I have encountered of why so many new university projects fail. They fail not because the architecture is wrong, but because the relational work was never done.

I have tried to build that relational work into OUG from the beginning. The governance structure gives faculty genuine decision-making power, not consultative voice. The Mission Hubs are designed as communities first, administrative units second. The quality standards are co-developed with partner universities, not imposed on them.

But designed participation is not the same as lived participation. The distance between an org chart and a functioning collegium is measured in trust, not in policies.

This is exactly where I need someone who understands both. Not to validate what I have built, but to challenge what I have assumed. I believe the founding council of OUG needs a voice that will ask, at every decision point: "Are we building a machine, or a community?" You are that voice.

The founding council will meet quarterly in its first two years, with monthly virtual check-ins during the setup phase. Your specific role would be to chair the Governance and Culture Committee — the body that oversees how OUG's founding principles are embodied in practice, not just described in documents.

I would welcome the opportunity to speak with you for thirty minutes — a conversation, not a presentation. If you are curious, I will send you a short document in advance: a twenty-page narrative called "Ein gewöhnlicher Dienstag" (A Perfectly Ordinary Tuesday), which shows what OUG looks like from the inside in the year 2029. It is, I believe, the fastest way to understand what I am trying to build.

This is a founding council, not an advisory board. The difference is simple: advisory boards are consulted. Founding councils decide. I am looking for people who want to build something, not observe it.

If you are willing to talk, please reply to this letter. I will arrange a time within the week.

With respect and genuine hope,
`[Your Name]`
Professor of Computer Science, DHBW / Founding Director OUG (proposed)

**Why I am writing to you specifically.** You built a world-class research institution at a moment when your region needed one — and had to create it within, not despite, a complex public system. That kind of institution succeeds when its founder understands that institutional economics is not just about incentive design, but about political feasibility within existing structures.

OUG faces the same challenge in a different key. The German higher education system is federated, legally conservative, and deeply sceptical of anything that looks like privatization. The financial model I have designed — 60% revenue sharing with partner universities, ECTS-based pricing at €20 per credit unit, break-even in Year 8 — is deliberately modest precisely because political viability requires it to be.

But I know that modest models have blind spots — institutions like the one you built have not always caught them in time either, and revenue assumptions that are never stress-tested against a real downturn are a common way new institutions falter. I have modelled optimistic, realistic, and conservative scenarios. I have not yet modelled the scenario where Germany enters a fiscal contraction in Year 3 and Länder budgets freeze.

I need someone who has built an institution of this kind, who understands both the economics and the politics, and who will not let me present a clean line to a ministry without first asking what happens when it breaks.

Your specific role in the founding council would be to chair the Finance and Sustainability Committee — with a mandate to stress-test every assumption before it becomes policy.

**Why I am writing to you specifically.** Your research on university financial sustainability and the structural challenges facing European University Alliances describes, with precision, exactly the problem OUG is designed to solve. Most alliances fail the transition from project funding to institutional permanence because they were built as projects — with project logic, project timelines, and project accountability structures — rather than as institutions.

OUG is built differently. The degree-awarding authority sits with a single institution. The financial model is designed for self-sufficiency by Year 8, not for perpetual grant dependency. The Mission Hub structure gives regional communities genuine participation without creating the governance fragmentation that destroys alliances.

But I am aware that I may be solving the German version of a European problem in ways that inadvertently make the solution less portable. The "interoperable by design" architecture — competency-based modules, external examining, quality gates — was conceived in the context of German accreditation law. You will know immediately whether it maps onto the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System in ways that would allow, for example, a Dutch or Polish university to participate as a teaching partner without requiring OUG to navigate 27 different national frameworks.

The European Education Area needs an existence proof. A model that shows how flexible, open, high-quality higher education can be institutionalized — not just piloted. I believe OUG can be that proof. But it needs to be designed with that ambition from the beginning, not retrofitted later.

Your specific role in the founding council would be to chair the European Affairs and Interoperability Committee — with a mandate to ensure that what we build in Germany is legible, and eventually accessible, to European partners.

The legal framework, the federal structure, the academic culture, the starting assumption about what a university is for — all of these are different in ways that matter. What I am building is not an OU for Germany. It is a German answer to the same question the OU answered for Britain half a century ago: who is higher education for, and who decides?

What I need from you is not a template but a mirror. Someone who has run an open university at scale, who knows what breaks in Year 3 that you could not see in Year 1, who understands that the hardest governance problems are not the ones in the constitution but the ones that arise between the lines when real people in real situations discover that the rules do not quite cover their case.

I also need your credibility. Not as a figurehead — I am not building a letterhead institution — but as a voice that can say, to the German ministry and to European partners: "I have seen this done. This proposal is serious. The people behind it understand what they are getting into."
