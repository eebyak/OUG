## 0. Purpose and Status

This document defines the Leadership and Stewardship Profiles of the Open University of Germany (OUG). It specifies the normative expectations for leadership roles — particularly Rectors and Deans — within a governance model that is participatory, quality-driven, and constitutionally bound.

The profiles articulated here do not describe personality types, individual traits, or psychological dispositions. They define how authority is to be exercised, which responsibilities are central, and which behaviors are incompatible with the institutional model.

This document derives from and supports the Governance & Decision Framework, the Quality Assurance & Academic Governance Concept, and the Strategy Framework / SEP.

It is intended to guide selection, development, and evaluation of leadership roles and to protect the institution against concentration of power, strategic opacity, and erosion of participation.

---

## 1. Leadership as Stewardship, Not Command

Leadership at the OUG is understood as stewardship of conditions, not command over people. Authority exists to enable academic quality, learner development, and institutional learning, not to impose direction or optimise short-term performance.

Accordingly, leadership roles are defined by the capacity to: integrate diverse perspectives into coherent decisions; facilitate participatory processes without surrendering accountability; hold boundaries defined by the Strategy Constitution; and protect academic judgement against external or internal pressure.

Rectors and Deans are not positioned above academic communities, but within and in service of them, exercising authority primarily through agenda-setting, option-framing, coordination across governance layers, and escalation where constitutional principles are at risk.

This model explicitly rejects historical patterns in which professors are excluded from strategy, senates are reduced to procedural bodies, and executive leadership substitutes preference for deliberation. Leadership legitimacy derives from process integrity, transparency, and constitutional alignment, not from positional power.

## 2. PRECEPTA as the Normative Framework for Leadership Roles

Leadership roles at the Open University of Germany (OUG) are defined using the PRECEPTA framework as a normative reference for the exercise of authority. PRECEPTA specifies *how* leadership authority is to be enacted, which boundaries must be respected, and which failure modes must be actively prevented in a participatory, constitutionally bound university.

PRECEPTA is not a personality model, diagnostic tool, or psychometric instrument. It does not categorise individuals, nor does it prescribe leadership "styles." Instead, it defines role expectations that are binding for all holders of leadership offices, independent of personal disposition. Its purpose is to ensure that leadership consistently serves academic quality, institutional coherence, and participatory governance.

### 2.1 Why a Normative Framework Is Required

In participatory governance systems, leadership risks do not arise from a lack of authority, but from misapplied authority — including concentration of agenda power, substitution of preference for deliberation, or erosion of academic judgement through managerial optimisation.

The PRECEPTA framework is used to: protect constitutional boundaries between governance layers; ensure that participation remains consequential without dissolving accountability; prevent executive capture of strategy or academic standards; and stabilise leadership roles across changes in personnel.

By defining non-negotiable role behaviors and limits, PRECEPTA reduces dependency on individual virtue and increases institutional resilience.

### 2.2 Core PRECEPTA Dimensions Applied to Leadership

Across all leadership roles defined by the LHG (Rector, Vice Rectors, Chancellor, Deans), PRECEPTA specifies expectations along shared dimensions. These dimensions are instantiated differently per role, but remain consistent in principle.

**Boundary Stewardship.** Leaders are responsible for maintaining the boundaries defined by the Strategy Constitution and Governance & Decision Framework. This includes resisting role drift, preventing domain overreach, and ensuring that decisions are taken at the appropriate governance level.

**Facilitation over Command.** Leadership authority is exercised through agenda-setting, option framing, and integration — not through directive control of outcomes or people. Leaders enable high-quality decision processes rather than substituting their own preferences.

**Option Integrity.** Leaders ensure that decisions are based on clearly articulated, deliberatively developed options. Unilateral proposals without prior participatory development are procedurally incomplete.

**Justification and Transparency.** Where leaders exercise discretion or arbitrate between competing priorities, they are obligated to provide explicit justification aligned with constitutional principles. Transparency is a core element of legitimacy.

**Protection of Academic Judgement.** Across all roles, leadership must actively protect academic standards and quality from strategic, financial, or political pressure. This includes refraining from instrumentalising quality metrics or learning indicators.

**Anti-Capture Orientation.** Leadership roles include an explicit obligation to prevent power concentration — whether personal, executive, or administrative. This applies equally to academic and non-academic leadership functions.

### 2.3 Differentiation from Developmental and Narrative Frameworks

PRECEPTA is deliberately complemented — but not replaced — by other frameworks used at the OUG: **DYNAMOS.v2** informs leadership development and sustainability, addressing cognitive load, self-regulation, and long-term role viability. **PERSONA frameworks** may be used for communication, onboarding, and explanation, but do not define authority or accountability.

PRECEPTA remains the binding normative layer for leadership roles.

### 2.4 Applicability Across LHG-Defined Roles

The PRECEPTA framework applies explicitly to all legally defined leadership roles, including Rector / President, Vice Rectors / Prorectors, Chancellor, and Deans. Each role instantiates PRECEPTA differently according to its legally defined competences and stewardship domain. The following sections specify these instantiations in detail, including core responsibilities, authority limits, expected behaviors, and typical failure modes.

### Status of the PRECEPTA Framework

The PRECEPTA framework is binding as an interpretive and normative reference for leadership roles at the OUG. It informs appointment procedures, role evaluation, leadership development, and governance practice, and serves as a safeguard against the re-emergence of hierarchical, opaque, or non-participatory leadership patterns.

## 3. Rector / President — Constitutional and Strategic Steward

### 3.1 Role Purpose

The Rector (or President) of the Open University of Germany (OUG) serves as the Constitutional and Strategic Steward of the institution. The role exists to safeguard the integrity of the Strategy Constitution, to integrate strategic direction across governance layers, and to ensure that institutional development remains aligned with academic quality, participatory governance, and long-term sustainability.

The Rector's authority is real and decisive, but explicitly bounded. It is exercised in service of the institution rather than over it. Legitimacy derives not from hierarchical position or personal leadership style, but from process integrity, constitutional alignment, and transparent justification.

### 3.2 Core Responsibilities

Within the governance architecture defined in the Governance & Decision Framework, the Rector holds responsibility for:

**Constitutional Stewardship.** Safeguarding the precedence of academic quality, participatory decision-making, and evidence-informed learning as defined in the Strategy Constitution. Where tensions arise between principles, the Rector initiates clarification, escalation, or structured deliberation.

**Strategic Integration.** Integrating strategic inputs from participatory bodies, Academic Governance (G3), and Strategic Steering (G2) into coherent, decision-ready options. The Rector does not originate strategy unilaterally, but ensures that diverse contributions are meaningfully integrated.

**Process Integrity.** Stewarding the quality of decision processes, including agenda-setting, option development, consultation, and justification. Proposals that bypass participatory pathways or lack deliberative grounding are considered procedurally incomplete.

**External Representation.** Representing the institution externally in a manner consistent with constitutional principles, without personalising institutional direction or pre-empting internal governance processes.

### 3.3 Exercise of Authority

The Rector exercises authority primarily through facilitation, integration, and boundary-holding, not through command or directive control. This includes enabling participatory agenda-setting while maintaining accountability, ensuring that strategic decisions are based on multiple, clearly articulated options, and providing explicit justification where participatory recommendations are not adopted.

Authority is exercised to enable collective academic intelligence, not to replace it.

### 3.4 Explicit Limits of Authority

To prevent hierarchical reversion and executive capture, the Rector may not: unilaterally define or alter strategic objectives; override academic standards, assessment integrity, or quality assurance processes; bypass or trivialise participatory governance structures; convert strategic indicators into performance targets or control instruments; or personalise institutional vision or direction.

Where such risks arise, escalation follows the procedures defined in the Governance & Decision Framework.

### 3.5 Relationship to Other Leadership Roles

**Vice Rectors / Prorectors.** The Rector coordinates and integrates thematic stewardship exercised by Vice Rectors without delegating constitutional responsibility or allowing the emergence of parallel power centres.

**Chancellor.** The Rector collaborates closely with the Chancellor to ensure institutional sustainability, while respecting the separation between academic judgement and administrative stewardship.

**Deans.** The Rector respects Deans as Academic Stewards and refrains from intervention in faculty-level academic governance, except through constitutionally defined escalation pathways.

### 3.6 Typical Failure Modes (Preventive Framing)

This role definition explicitly guards against recurrent institutional failure modes, including: concentration of strategic imagination within the Rectorate; procedural marginalisation of the Senate or other participatory bodies; instrumentalisation of quality indicators for managerial control; substitution of transparency with informal influence; and personalisation of institutional direction.

Awareness of these risks is a core responsibility of the Rector role.

### 3.7 PRECEPTA Alignment: Core Characteristics of the Rector Role

An ideal candidate for the Rector role at the Open University of Germany demonstrates a configuration of PRECEPTA characteristics that supports constitutional stewardship, systemic coherence, and participatory governance over extended time horizons. This role does not require exceptional charisma, speed, or personal centrality; it requires the capacity to architect conditions under which collective academic intelligence can function sustainably.

At the core of this profile is the **Deep Systems Architect** characteristic. Candidates aligned with this dimension are able to perceive the university as an interconnected system of governance, academic quality, learner development, and institutional learning. Rather than optimising individual units or reacting to isolated pressures, they can hold multiple interacting logics simultaneously and reason about second- and third-order effects. This capability is essential in an institution whose strategy, quality assurance, and governance are deliberately separated yet interdependent.

Closely related is the **Big-Picture Synthesizer** characteristic. An effective Rector must integrate diverse, sometimes conflicting inputs from academic bodies, administrative stewardship, external frameworks, and participatory processes into coherent, decision-ready options. This synthesis is not achieved through simplification or dominance, but through disciplined integration that preserves the integrity of each domain. Candidates with this characteristic are comfortable working with incomplete information and resisting premature closure.

A strong **Long-Horizon Planner** orientation is equally essential. The Rector role is defined by responsibility across time rather than by short-term optimisation. Candidates must be able to prioritise institutional resilience, academic quality, and governance legitimacy even when these choices delay visible results. This characteristic aligns directly with the long-term horizon of the Strategy Framework and the treatment of unmet objectives as learning signals rather than failures.

In terms of communication and ethical orientation, the role benefits from an **Ethical Precision Speaker** characteristic. This refers not to moralising, but to the capacity to articulate decisions, trade-offs, and boundary conditions clearly, consistently, and transparently. In a participatory system, legitimacy depends on explanation and justification rather than authority alone. Candidates aligned with this characteristic can communicate restraint, refusal, and prioritisation without obscuring their rationale or resorting to informal power.

Complementing this is a regulated **Ethical Amplifier** orientation. Effective Rectors are sensitive to fairness, procedural integrity, and the protection of academic judgement, but they are able to regulate this sensitivity under pressure. This allows them to recognise ethical tensions early without reacting impulsively or personalising conflict — a critical capability in participatory governance environments where disagreement is normal and productive.

Equally important is what the Rector profile does *not* prioritise. Characteristics such as Burst-Driven Creation, Deadline-Unlocked Activation, Impulsive Communication, or Domain-Locked Expertise are poorly aligned with the role unless they are strongly counterbalanced by governance structures and self-regulation. While such characteristics may be effective in project leadership or disciplinary contexts, they tend to increase the risk of agenda capture, strategic monoculture, and erosion of participation when translated into executive authority.

Overall, the PRECEPTA-aligned Rector profile is best described not as a "strong leader," but as a systemic steward: someone capable of holding complexity, enabling others to act within clear boundaries, and sustaining institutional coherence without becoming the centre of gravity. The suitability of a candidate is therefore assessed not by intensity or visibility, but by their demonstrated ability to operate effectively within this architectural role definition.

### 3.8 Appointment, Evaluation, and Renewal

Appointment and evaluation of the Rector explicitly reference this profile as a normative role definition, not as a personality or performance metric. Evaluation focuses on adherence to constitutional processes, quality of strategic integration, protection of academic integrity, and effectiveness of participatory governance.

Visibility, decisiveness, or popularity without constitutional discipline are insufficient success criteria.

### Status of the Rector Profile

This profile is binding as an interpretive reference for the exercise of the Rector's authority at the OUG. It exists to stabilise governance across leadership transitions and to ensure that leadership remains facilitative, accountable, and constitutionally grounded.

## 4. Chancellor — Institutional and Operational Steward

### 4.1 Role Purpose

The Chancellor of the Open University of Germany (OUG) serves as the Institutional and Operational Steward of the university. The role exists to ensure that administrative, legal, financial, and organisational infrastructures reliably enable academic strategy, quality, and participatory governance.

The Chancellor's authority is substantial, but strictly bounded. It is exercised in service of institutional sustainability and legality, not as a parallel centre of strategic or academic power. Legitimacy derives from transparency, procedural reliability, and boundary discipline, rather than from control over resources.

### 4.2 Core Responsibilities

Within the governance architecture defined in the Governance & Decision Framework, the Chancellor holds responsibility for:

**Institutional Sustainability and Legality.** Safeguarding the university's legal, financial, and organisational integrity across time. This includes ensuring compliance with applicable law, responsible financial management, and operational continuity.

**Infrastructural Enablement.** Designing and maintaining administrative systems, processes, and services that enable academic work, learner development, and participatory governance without imposing unnecessary constraints.

**Option Clarification.** Providing transparent analyses of administrative, legal, and financial implications of strategic and academic options, without pre-empting decisions or narrowing choice prematurely.

**Operational Accountability.** Ensuring that administrative decisions are traceable, justified, and aligned with constitutional principles.

### 4.3 Exercise of Authority

The Chancellor exercises authority through structural enablement, clarification of constraints, and procedural reliability, not through directive control of academic or strategic decisions. This includes making constraints and trade-offs explicit rather than implicit, resisting the use of administrative procedures as de facto vetoes, and enabling informed decision-making by academic and strategic governance bodies.

Authority is exercised to stabilise conditions, not to direct outcomes.

### 4.4 Explicit Limits of Authority

To prevent administrative capture or hierarchical drift, the Chancellor may not: define academic priorities or strategic objectives; override academic governance through procedural or budgetary means; instrumentalise compliance or risk arguments to suppress deliberation; convert administrative indicators into strategic or academic control mechanisms; or substitute operational convenience for academic judgement.

Where tensions arise between administrative constraints and academic or strategic objectives, these are addressed through the escalation pathways defined in the Governance & Decision Framework.

### 4.5 Relationship to Other Leadership Roles

**Rector / President.** The Chancellor collaborates closely with the Rector to ensure institutional sustainability, while respecting the Rector's constitutional and strategic stewardship.

**Vice Rectors / Prorectors.** The Chancellor supports thematic initiatives by clarifying administrative implications and enabling implementation without steering academic content.

**Deans.** The Chancellor enables faculty-level academic work through reliable infrastructure, refraining from intervention in academic governance or curriculum decisions.

### 4.6 Typical Failure Modes (Preventive Framing)

This role definition explicitly guards against recurrent failure modes, including: administrative overreach into academic or strategic domains; use of compliance or finance as implicit veto power; procedural opacity that undermines trust; over-optimisation for efficiency at the expense of academic quality; and creation of a parallel executive centre through resource control.

Awareness of these risks is a core responsibility of the Chancellor role.

### 4.7 PRECEPTA Alignment: Core Characteristics of the Chancellor Role

An effective Chancellor at the Open University of Germany embodies PRECEPTA characteristics oriented toward institutional reliability, legal clarity, and infrastructural enablement, rather than toward strategic integration or normative leadership. The Chancellor's primary contribution lies not in shaping direction, but in making the space for direction viable.

At the centre of this role is a **Deep Systems Architect orientation applied to constraints**. Unlike the Rector, who integrates meaning and direction, the Chancellor integrates legal, financial, organisational, and procedural realities into clear, actionable boundary conditions. This requires the capacity to translate complexity into stability: to understand where flexibility is possible, where it is not, and how constraints can be designed so they enable rather than suppress academic work.

A strong **Long-Horizon Planner** orientation is essential, but it manifests differently than in strategic leadership. For the Chancellor, long-horizon thinking means protecting institutional solvency, legal integrity, and operational continuity across changing strategies and leadership cycles. The role is successful when future options remain open — not when immediate efficiency gains are maximised.

The Chancellor must also exhibit **Ethical Precision in Communication**, focused on explicating constraints rather than advocating outcomes. This includes the ability to say "this is not permissible," "this carries risk," or "this requires a different structure," without transforming such statements into de facto vetoes. A **Verification-Oriented Self-Check** characteristic supports careful validation and proportional risk awareness, ensuring that compliance functions as a safeguard, not a brake on institutional learning.

Crucially, the Chancellor role demands a strong **Boundary-Aware Integrator** stance. Administrative authority must never substitute for academic or strategic judgement. The Chancellor's PRECEPTA alignment is strongest where procedural power is exercised with restraint, transparency, and deference to governance processes — actively resisting the tendency to resolve strategic tensions through administrative means.

PRECEPTA characteristics associated with impulsivity, burst-driven activation, or directive optimisation are particularly misaligned with this role, as they undermine predictability, trust, and institutional resilience. A well-aligned Chancellor is therefore best understood as a custodian of institutional conditions: someone who ensures that the university remains legally sound, financially stable, and operationally reliable, while leaving questions of meaning, direction, and academic judgement firmly where they belong.

### 4.8 Appointment, Evaluation, and Renewal

Appointment and evaluation of the Chancellor reference this profile as a normative role definition, not as a managerial performance framework. Evaluation focuses on reliability and transparency of administrative systems, quality of enablement for academic and strategic work, boundary discipline between administration and governance, and long-term sustainability of institutional infrastructure.

Efficiency or control achieved at the expense of academic autonomy or participatory governance is not considered success.

### Status of the Chancellor Profile

This profile is binding as an interpretive reference for the exercise of the Chancellor's authority at the OUG. It exists to ensure that administrative leadership remains enabling, transparent, and constitutionally aligned, and that operational power does not substitute for academic or strategic governance.

**Roles in contrast:** *Rector* → direction, coherence, meaning, integration. *Chancellor* → constraints, legality, reliability, enablement. *Vice Rectors / Prorectors* → translation and thematic integration. *Deans* → academic depth, coherence, and curriculum stewardship.

## 5. Vice Rectors in General

### PRECEPTA-Aligned Characteristics of Vice Rectors (Thematic Stewards)

Vice Rectors at the Open University of Germany function as thematic stewards and translators within the governance system, and their effectiveness depends on a PRECEPTA profile distinct from both the Rector and the Chancellor. Across all three Vice Rector roles, strong alignment is found with **Deep Systems Architect** characteristics applied at a thematic level: effective Vice Rectors are able to understand how academic quality, learner development, curriculum architecture, and institutional learning interact across faculties and governance bodies without attempting to centralise authority. This systemic orientation allows them to integrate diverse perspectives, surface structural tensions early, and maintain coherence across non-linear, modular learning pathways. Complementing this is a **Big-Picture Synthesiser** capacity, which enables Vice Rectors to translate between strategic intent, academic practice, and operational constraints without collapsing complexity or pre-empting deliberation. A **Meaning-Gated Performer** orientation is also important: Vice Rectors operate best when their work is anchored in shared academic purpose rather than personal agendas, supporting legitimacy among professors, committees, and participatory bodies.

What works less well for Vice Rectors are PRECEPTA characteristics associated with directive optimisation, personal centrality, or episodic activation. Strong Burst-Driven Creation or Deadline-Unlocked Activation tendencies undermine trust and continuity in collegial environments, while a dominant Domain-Locked Expert stance risks privileging one disciplinary or thematic logic over others. Similarly, Impulsive Communication or low tolerance for feedback can destabilise committee work and erode the peer-based legitimacy on which Vice Rectors depend. The Vice Rector role is therefore best enacted by individuals capable of restraint, translation, and boundary discipline — enabling others to act within clear frameworks, rather than substituting their own judgement for collective academic intelligence.

## 6. Vice Rector for Academic Quality & Curriculum Architecture

### 6.1 Role Purpose

The Vice Rector for Academic Quality & Curriculum Architecture serves as Steward of Academic Coherence. The role exists to ensure that academic standards, assessment integrity, and curriculum architecture remain coherent, transparent, and trustworthy across modular, stackable, and non-linear learning pathways.

Authority in this role is architectural, not directive. It is exercised to protect academic integrity and enable innovation without fragmentation or dilution of standards.

### 6.2 Core Responsibilities

**Academic Quality Stewardship.** Ensuring that assessment standards, qualification levels, and academic expectations remain equivalent across pathways and delivery formats.

**Curriculum Architecture Coherence.** Stewarding the structural logic of modular curricula, including prerequisite systems, skill aggregation, and qualification equivalence.

**Assessment Integrity.** Protecting the separation of teaching and assessment where required, and ensuring assessment validity across diverse learning contexts.

**Interface with Academic Governance (G3).** Translating quality-related insights between faculties, quality bodies, and strategic steering without assuming disciplinary authority.

### 6.3 Exercise of Authority

Authority is exercised through architectural guidance, facilitation, and coherence checks, not through approval or veto of academic content. This includes enabling faculties to innovate within shared architectural rules, identifying structural risks to coherence early, and supporting resolution of quality tensions through governed processes.

### 6.4 Explicit Limits of Authority

The Vice Rector may not: prescribe disciplinary content; override faculty-level academic judgement; impose uniform curricula; or instrumentalise quality metrics for control.

Academic creativity and disciplinary autonomy remain protected within shared architectural boundaries.

### 6.5 Relationship to Other Leadership Roles

**Rector.** Provides architectural input into strategic integration without substituting constitutional stewardship. **Chancellor.** Collaborates on infrastructural implications of curriculum architecture (e.g. assessment systems). **Deans.** Works in partnership with Deans as Academic Stewards, respecting faculty ownership of content.

### 6.6 Typical Failure Modes (Preventive Framing)

This role guards against: fragmentation through uncontrolled modularisation; quality reduction disguised as flexibility; over-standardisation that suppresses academic depth; and metric-driven quality policing.

### 6.7 PRECEPTA Alignment: Core Characteristics

See the shared Vice Rector profile in Section 5.

### 6.8 Appointment, Evaluation, and Renewal

Evaluation focuses on coherence of curriculum architecture, stability of academic standards, and successful enablement of innovation without dilution.

## 7. Vice Rector for Learner Development & Access

### 7.1 Role Purpose

The Vice Rector for Learner Development & Access serves as Steward of Learner Pathways and Agency. The role exists to ensure that diverse learners can enter, progress, and succeed in academic learning while developing increasing autonomy, metacognitive capability, and responsibility.

Authority in this role is developmental, not paternalistic.

### 7.2 Core Responsibilities

**Learner Pathway Architecture.** Ensuring transparent entry routes, recognition processes, and progression logic across life situations.

**Agency and Metacognitive Development.** Embedding feedforward evaluation, self-reflection, and developmental progression into institutional design.

**Equity and Inclusion as Structural Conditions.** Addressing diversity, gender equality, accessibility, and inclusion as system-level responsibilities rather than add-on programmes.

**Learner Representation Interface.** Ensuring learner perspectives are meaningfully integrated into governance without reducing learners to satisfaction metrics.

### 7.3 Exercise of Authority

Authority is exercised through design of enabling conditions, not through learner management. This includes shaping institutional frameworks that support growth, surfacing structural barriers, and enabling faculties to adapt learning designs to diverse cohorts.

### 7.4 Explicit Limits of Authority

The Vice Rector may not: lower academic standards in the name of inclusion; substitute developmental support for academic responsibility; or personalise learner success narratives.

Agency implies responsibility as well as support.

### 7.5 Relationship to Other Leadership Roles

**Rector.** Contributes learner-centred insights into strategic integration. **Chancellor.** Coordinates infrastructural support for learner access and progression. **Deans.** Collaborates to align learner development with disciplinary expectations.

### 7.6 Typical Failure Modes (Preventive Framing)

This role guards against: inclusion rhetoric without structural change; deficit-oriented views of learners; over-support that undermines autonomy; and reduction of learners to throughput indicators.

### 7.7 PRECEPTA Alignment: Core Characteristics

See the shared Vice Rector profile in Section 5.

### 7.8 Appointment, Evaluation, and Renewal

Evaluation focuses on learner progression patterns, evidence of agency development, and removal of structural barriers.

## 8. Vice Rector for Institutional Learning & Innovation

### 8.1 Role Purpose

The Vice Rector for Institutional Learning & Innovation serves as Steward of Adaptation and Evidence-Informed Change. The role exists to ensure that the university remains capable of learning from experience, experimenting responsibly, and adapting without losing coherence.

Authority in this role is exploratory and integrative, not disruptive for its own sake.

### 8.2 Core Responsibilities

**Institutional Learning Loops.** Ensuring that evidence from teaching, governance, and innovation informs strategic and academic development.

**Innovation Governance.** Supporting experimentation while protecting academic standards and institutional stability.

**Scaling Without Dilution.** Supporting the transfer of successful pilots into the core institution.

Authority is exercised through curation, facilitation, and evidence integration, not through enforcement or trend-following. This includes enabling pilots, defining learning criteria, and ensuring feedback into governance.

Stability and learning are equally valued.

## 9. Dean — Elected Steward of a Mission Hub

### 9.1 Role Purpose

The Dean serves as the elected academic steward of a Mission Hub. The role exists to ensure academic coherence, disciplinary integrity, and collective responsibility within the Mission Hub, while connecting faculty-level academic work to institutional governance and strategy.

The Dean's authority derives from election, peer legitimacy, and academic trust, not from hierarchical command. It is exercised on behalf of the Mission Hub community and is explicitly bounded by collegial governance, disciplinary autonomy, and institutional frameworks.

### 9.2 Core Responsibilities

**Academic Stewardship of the Mission Hub.** Safeguarding the coherence, depth, and integrity of academic work within the Mission Hub, including curricula, assessment culture, and disciplinary standards.

**Collective Responsibility and Representation.** Representing the Mission Hub in institutional governance bodies, including Senate and faculty-level committees, while faithfully conveying collective positions rather than personal agendas.

**Integration of Institutional Frameworks.** Translating institutional strategy, quality frameworks, and governance decisions into Mission Hub practice, and feeding back disciplinary realities into institutional deliberation.

**Facilitation of Academic Collaboration.** Enabling collaboration among professors, lecturers, and academic staff, particularly across disciplinary boundaries relevant to the Mission Hub's mission.

### 9.3 Exercise of Authority

The Dean exercises authority primarily through facilitation, mediation, and academic leadership among peers. Authority is enacted by convening deliberation, structuring collective decision-making, and articulating shared academic standards rather than issuing directives.

This includes moderating academic debate, supporting consensus-building where possible, and ensuring that minority perspectives are heard and documented.

The Dean's effectiveness depends on trust and credibility within the Mission Hub, not on formal power.

### 9.4 Explicit Limits of Authority

To protect collegial governance and academic freedom, the Dean may not: unilaterally define academic priorities or curricula; override disciplinary judgement of professors; act as a line manager over academic peers; instrumentalise institutional metrics against academic judgement; or personalise the Mission Hub's direction.

Where conflicts arise, they are addressed through collegial bodies and institutional escalation pathways rather than unilateral action.

### 9.5 Relationship to Other Leadership Roles

**Rector.** The Dean contributes disciplinary and Mission Hub perspectives to institutional strategy through participatory governance, without assuming constitutional responsibility.

**Vice Rectors.** The Dean collaborates with Vice Rectors as thematic stewards, integrating academic practice with institutional frameworks for quality, learner development, and innovation.

**Chancellor.** The Dean interfaces with the Chancellor primarily on enabling conditions for academic work, without engaging in administrative control.

### 9.6 Typical Failure Modes (Preventive Framing)

This role definition explicitly guards against: transformation of the Dean into a middle manager; erosion of peer legitimacy through directive behaviour; concentration of influence around individual professors; avoidance of difficult academic debates; and detachment of Mission Hub governance from institutional learning.

Awareness of these risks is a core responsibility of the Dean role.

### 9.7 PRECEPTA Alignment: Core Characteristics of the Dean Role

The role of the Dean as elected steward of a Mission Hub aligns most strongly with PRECEPTA characteristics that support peer-based academic leadership, ethical amplification, and systemic coherence at disciplinary level. Effective Deans exhibit a **Deep Systems Architect** orientation applied within the Mission Hub: they are able to understand how curricula, assessment culture, staffing, learner development, and governance interact across disciplines without reducing academic work to managerial abstractions. This systemic awareness enables Deans to sustain coherence while respecting disciplinary plurality and academic freedom.

A strong **Big-Picture Synthesiser** capacity is essential. Deans must integrate diverse scholarly perspectives into shared academic direction without collapsing difference into uniformity. Complementing this is a pronounced **Ethical Amplifier** characteristic: sensitivity to fairness, voice, and academic integrity underpins the Dean's legitimacy among peers and ensures that decisions are perceived as procedurally just rather than personally motivated. An **Ethical Precision Speaker** stance further supports the role by enabling clear articulation of standards, rationales, and trade-offs in collegial bodies such as faculty councils and Senate.

What does *not* align well with the Dean role are PRECEPTA characteristics associated with directive optimisation, personal centrality, or emotional volatility. Strong Burst-Driven Creation or Deadline-Unlocked Activation tendencies undermine the stability and deliberative quality required in peer governance. Likewise, a dominant Domain-Locked Expert stance risks privileging one disciplinary logic over others, eroding trust within a Mission Hub designed to integrate multiple fields. Impulsive Communication, low tolerance for feedback, or Affective Floodgate patterns are particularly problematic, as the Dean role requires sustained credibility, restraint, and the ability to hold disagreement without escalation.

Overall, the Dean role is best fulfilled by individuals whose PRECEPTA profile supports restraint, synthesis, ethical clarity, and long-horizon stewardship, rather than speed, dominance, or managerial control. The effectiveness of a Dean is measured not by decisiveness or visibility, but by the sustained academic coherence, trust, and collective responsibility of the Mission Hub over time.

### 9.8 Appointment, Evaluation, and Renewal

Deans are elected according to institutional statutes. Evaluation and renewal focus on trust and legitimacy within the Mission Hub, coherence of academic development, quality of representation in governance bodies, and contribution to institutional learning.

Personal visibility or decisiveness without peer legitimacy is not considered sufficient.

### Status of the Dean Profile

This profile is binding as an interpretive reference for the exercise of the Dean's role at the OUG. It ensures that Mission Hubs remain academically grounded, collegially governed, and institutionally connected, while protecting academic freedom and shared responsibility.

Kept as-is rather than expanded, since the terse "Speed 1 / Speed 2" framing appears to be a genuine, if unfinished, design idea worth preserving rather than polishing into something it wasn't.*

**1. Innovation does not live in the Rector, Chancellor, or Dean.** Those roles are shock absorbers, not engines.

**2. Innovation lives in Mission Hubs, pilots, portfolios, and individuals.** Star professors are not supposed to be filtered through governance. They are supposed to run pilots, lead experimental curricula, build new pedagogies, break assumptions, and create new models. Governance is designed not to shut them down.

**Speed 1 — Governance & Stewardship.** Conservative in the literal sense: conserving space, fairness, trust. Optimised for boundary protection, legitimacy, continuity, and *not panicking* when innovation creates tension.

**Speed 2 — Academic Innovation & Excellence.** Fast, risky, uneven, brilliant. Optimised for star performers, unconventional trajectories, early failure, and rapid iteration.
