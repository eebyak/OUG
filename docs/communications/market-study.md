## Executive Summary

This market study assesses OUG's competitive position relative to three institutions that operate in adjacent or overlapping segments of the European distance and digital higher education market: **FernUniversität in Hagen** (Germany's only state distance university, 75,000 students, €122.5M annual revenue); **EuroTeQ Engineering University** (a transnational alliance of nine leading technical universities, EU-funded, focused on engineering education and professional micro-credentials); and **IU International University of Applied Sciences** (Germany's largest private university by enrolment at 130,000+ students, owned by UK private equity firm Oakley Capital).

> **Key Findings**
>
> **OUG occupies a distinct strategic position.** No direct competitor currently combines OUG's specific mix: platform federation model, €25/ECTS pricing, European digital sovereignty architecture, formal accreditation pathway as a Fernhochschule in BW, and non-profit mission. OUG is not a clone of any existing institution.
>
> **The strongest overlap is with FernUni Hagen on student profile.** Both serve working adults, non-traditional learners, and geographically distributed students. The difference is structural: FernUni is NRW state-funded (€87.5M state subsidy per year) and has zero tuition for domestic students under most circumstances. OUG must win on programme relevance, credential interoperability, and the European digital architecture — not on price alone.
>
> **IU demonstrates the demand is real and very large.** IU grew from 15,000 students in 2017 to 130,000+ in 2024 — an 8x increase in seven years. The market for flexible, accessible, private distance higher education in Germany is proven and large. IU's recent difficulties (Spiegel reports, discontinued programmes, €37M loss in 2023, Projekt Minerva restructuring) demonstrate the risk of the pure for-profit hypergrowth model — and open space for a mission-driven alternative.
>
> **EuroTeQ is not a competitor — it is a potential alliance partner.** EuroTeQ targets elite engineering students at partner institutions and professionals in advanced technical education. OUG targets working adults across all disciplines seeking accessible, affordable, credited learning. The populations are largely non-overlapping. OUG should seek EuroTeQ partnership for micro-credential co-delivery rather than compete.
>
> **OUG's structural advantage is €25/ECTS with European credential quality.** At €25/ECTS, a full Bachelor's degree costs €4,500 total — substantially below IU (€2,300–5,200/year), comparable to the cheapest German private Fernhochschulen, and more interoperable than any competitor through ELM v3 credentials and EWP participation. This pricing model is a structural moat, not a discount strategy.

## Part I: The Market — Distance and Digital Higher Education in Germany and Europe

### 1. Market Context

#### 1.1 The Structural Case for Distance and Digital Higher Education

The German higher education market is the largest in the European Union by student numbers, with approximately 2.9 million enrolled students as of 2024. The market for distance and digital learning — which was a niche before 2015 — has become a mainstream segment driven by four structural forces that are unlikely to reverse.

**Workforce transformation:** The BMBF's 2024 *Berufsbildungsbericht* identifies a persistent skills gap in digital competences across the German workforce, with an estimated 1.7 million unfilled roles in digital and technology-adjacent fields. These are overwhelmingly held by people already in employment who cannot attend a traditional campus university.

**Non-traditional learner growth:** The proportion of German students entering higher education without a traditional Abitur has grown from under 5% in 2010 to approximately 17% in 2024 (IU internal data). Students from non-academic households, vocational entrants, and career changers require flexible, affordable, accessible formats.

**European credential portability:** The European Education Area's 2025 targets, the EU Council Recommendation on micro-credentials (2022), and the rollout of the European Digital Identity Wallet create structural demand for portable, machine-readable, verifiable credentials — exactly what OUG's ELM v3 infrastructure provides and existing institutions are still retrofitting.

**Post-COVID normalisation of online learning:** The COVID-19 period proved that high-quality learning can be delivered entirely digitally. Student willingness to pay for online degrees at accredited institutions has increased substantially. The stigma associated with distance learning — historically significant in Germany — has largely dissipated.

#### 1.2 The Moral and Civic Argument

The rapid growth of IU International University from 15,000 students in 2017 to 130,000+ in 2024 is, at its core, a market signal about a failure of the public system — not evidence of the superiority of the for-profit model. The students who chose IU did not primarily choose it because it is a better university than FernUni Hagen. They chose it because it offered the programme they needed, in the format they could manage, at the moment when they were ready to study.

The public system has not kept pace. FernUniversität in Hagen serves 75,000 students with an exceptional academic tradition — but it operates in one jurisdiction (NRW), under one legal framework, with state funding that constrains its ability to expand programme offerings rapidly. The Hochschulrektorenkonferenz's 2024 data shows that of Germany's approximately 420 higher education institutions, fewer than 30 offer significant distance learning programmes, and fewer than 10 offer fully digital degree pathways.

OUG's position is not that IU is wrong to have grown rapidly, or that its students made poor choices. OUG's position is that the function IU has been performing — making higher education accessible to people who cannot attend a traditional campus — is a public good that should be provided by institutions with a non-profit mission, European digital infrastructure, and academic governance structures accountable to their students and communities rather than to a UK private equity firm.

> **The Neider Question — A Direct Response.** Critics of IU's quality are often dismissed in Germany as *Neider* — envious rivals who resent commercial success in a domain that was previously the preserve of the state. This framing is worth examining directly.
>
> The quality concerns about IU that emerged in 2025–2026 are not primarily about academic quality in the traditional sense. The Spiegel investigations, the architecture programme court cases, and the Physician Assistant programme discontinuation are not about whether IU's lecturers are knowledgeable. They are about information integrity — whether students are told accurately what a qualification does and does not entitle them to do.
>
> The architecture case is instructive: IU offered a programme, enrolled students and charged them fees, without adequately disclosing that the resulting qualification would not be recognised by the *Architektenkammer*, making it impossible for graduates to practise as registered architects in Germany. This is not an academic quality question. It is a question of whether an institution's marketing and sales processes serve the student's interests or the institution's growth targets. IU's response — removing the offending language from the website after the court cases — is not the behaviour of an institution that puts students first. An institution that puts students first would not have needed a court case to clarify what its qualification actually qualified graduates to do.
>
> OUG's model is not the opposite of IU's model. It is the corrected version: the same accessibility, the same flexibility, the same ambition to reach students who cannot attend a traditional campus — but with transparent credential quality, European interoperability standards built in from day one, and a governance structure that is accountable to the mission rather than to a private equity return.

## Part II: Competitor Profiles

### 2. Competitor Profiles

#### 2.1 FernUniversität in Hagen

| At a Glance | |
|---|---|
| Students | ~75,000 (2024) |
| Annual revenue | €122.5M (2024) |
| Legal form | Public university under NRW Hochschulgesetz |
| Funding mix | 71% state subsidy, 21% fees/other, 7% third-party |
| Tuition | Semesterbeitrag ~€200; no degree-programme tuition for most students |
| Accreditation | Full state university, NRW; own degree-awarding powers |
| Focus | Law, economics, humanities, social sciences, mathematics, CS — broad |
| Key strength | CATALPA research centre, LEAD:FUH digital teaching, adult learner expertise |

FernUniversität in Hagen is the most structurally comparable institution to OUG in terms of target student population and pedagogical philosophy. Both serve working adults, non-traditional learners, and geographically distributed students who cannot attend a traditional campus. Both are committed to distance and digital learning as the primary mode of instruction. Both see accessible higher education as a public good rather than a premium service.

The fundamental difference is funding and governance. FernUni receives €87.5M per year in regular state subsidy from NRW — more than OUG's entire projected five-year revenue. This allows FernUni to charge minimal fees while offering broad programme coverage and significant research capacity. OUG cannot compete with FernUni on price (though €25/ECTS is competitive) and does not need to: OUG's advantage is programme agility, European credential interoperability, and the federated model that allows it to offer courses from multiple partner institutions rather than being constrained by a single institution's academic staffing.

FernUni's recent strategic initiatives — CATALPA (digital teaching research, 60+ researchers, NRW-funded) and LEAD:FUH (up to €7M from the Foundation for Innovation in Higher Education, focusing on AI-enabled learning) — show a significant investment in the digital pedagogy space that OUG is also entering. FernUni should be approached as a potential alliance partner and co-developer of digital learning research rather than as a direct competitor.

> **Strategic Relationship: Alliance Partner, Not Competitor.** FernUni Hagen's programme portfolio (law, economics, humanities, social sciences, mathematics, CS) is complementary to OUG's likely initial focus on technology, business, health, and education programmes. A partnership in which FernUni courses are offered through the OUG platform — with FernUni receiving the 40% partner share under OUG's revenue model — would give FernUni a new channel to reach students outside NRW and give OUG immediately accredited programme content without requiring OUG to develop it from scratch. This is exactly the kind of federated model OUG's architecture was designed for.

#### 2.2 EuroTeQ Engineering University

| At a Glance | |
|---|---|
| Structure | Alliance of 9 technical universities (TUM, DTU, École Polytechnique, Eindhoven, KTH, EPFL, CTU Prague, Aalto, Politecnico Milano) |
| Funding | EU Erasmus+: €17.8M to date; EU Horizon 2020: ~€2M; national co-funding undisclosed |
| Legal form | Consortium; no separate legal personality; operated through TUM as coordinator |
| Students | Primarily enrolled at partner institutions; specific EuroTeQ-only student count not published |
| Tuition | Varies by partner institution; micro-credentials may carry costs through offering institution |
| Focus | Engineering, innovation, challenge-based learning, executive education, European mobility |

EuroTeQ is the most prestigious name in this competitive analysis and the least directly competitive with OUG. It is, at its core, an alliance of elite European technical universities working together on mobility, challenge-based learning, and lifelong professional education. Its students are primarily engineering students already enrolled at TUM, DTU, EPFL, or one of the other partner institutions — not working adults in Germany seeking a flexible route to a Bachelor's degree.

EuroTeQ's Achilles heel is its funding model. €19.8M in EU grants across its initial phases is significant programme money, but it is project funding — time-limited, competitive, and dependent on continued EU programme priorities. EuroTeQ has no disclosed tuition revenue model for its core offer, and its consolidated operating budget (including partner institutions' in-kind contributions) is not publicly available. When EU programme funding for European universities changes — as it has done and will do — EuroTeQ must either have developed a self-sustaining revenue model or face a significant restructuring. This is not a criticism; it is a structural observation relevant to OUG's competitive positioning.

EuroTeQ's micro-credential offer under EuroTeQ Professional and its ELPIS executive programme are the areas of closest overlap with OUG's continuing education and micro-credential ambitions. However, EuroTeQ targets senior engineers and technology professionals at premium price points, while OUG's €25/ECTS model targets working adults across disciplines at accessible price points. These are non-overlapping segments.

> **Strategic Relationship: Complementary Alliance and Standard Co-Development Partner.** OUG should position itself for participation in EuroTeQ's next EU project application phase — specifically as a partner that can provide the scalable, GDPR-compliant, sovereignty-certified digital infrastructure that elite technical universities need but rarely have the institutional capacity to build themselves. Additionally, EuroTeQ's work on European micro-credential standards and ESCO competence mapping directly overlaps with OUG's credentialing architecture. A co-development relationship on ELM v3 and ESCO mapping methodology would be mutually beneficial.

#### 2.3 IU International University

| At a Glance | |
|---|---|
| Students | 130,000+ enrolled (2024/25) — largest in Germany by enrolment |
| Legal form | Private GmbH; staatlich anerkannte Fachhochschule; profit-oriented |
| Ownership | IU Group N.V. — owned by Oakley Capital (UK private equity) since 2017; previously Apollo Group (US) |
| Financial result | Loss of ~€37M in 2023 despite growing revenue; ~€790M debt reported (Spiegel, Feb 2026) |
| Tuition | Bachelor: €2,309–5,247/year; Master: €1,500–13,920/year; varies by format and programme |
| Accreditation | System-accredited by Akkreditierungsrat (reaccredited 2021, valid to 2033); state recognised since 1999 |
| Formats | Fernstudium (83%), Duales Studium, myStudium, on-campus (Berlin, Bad Honnef) |
| Growth | 15,000 (2017) to 130,000+ (2024); 63% CAGR 2019–2021; hypergrowth reversed 2024 |

IU is the most important competitor in this analysis precisely because it has demonstrated, at scale, that the market OUG intends to serve is very large and real. The journey from 15,000 to 130,000 students in seven years is not primarily a story about IU's excellence — it is a story about suppressed demand. Hundreds of thousands of people in Germany and across Europe want a flexible, accessible, affordable degree, and the public system has not been able to provide it.

IU's current difficulties deserve careful analysis, because they reveal both the structural weaknesses of the for-profit hypergrowth model and the specific quality risks that OUG must avoid. The Spiegel investigation (November 2025, February 2026) documented: aggressive sales tactics that prioritised contract signatures over informed student decisions; programmes launched without adequate verification of professional recognition (the architecture case, the Physician Assistant case); a management culture described as authoritarian and focused on growth metrics over educational outcomes; a lecturer hiring process that, in at least one documented case, involved an AI interview with no human discussion of professional qualifications; and a financial structure of approximately €790M in debt and multiple loss years, raising sustainability questions.

The Spiegel reports are not the whole story. IU's system accreditation — the highest form of quality assurance in the German *Hochschulrecht* — was renewed by the Akkreditierungsrat in 2021 and is valid until 2033. This means an independent external body continues to certify that IU's internal quality management systems meet the required standards. The architecture and Physician Assistant cases are serious failures, but they are failures of specific programme launch decisions, not of the entire institution's academic standards.

The more fundamental point — and one on which IU's critics and defenders can agree — is that IU grew so fast that its institutional governance and academic quality assurance could not keep pace. The hypergrowth strategy that Oakley Capital's private equity model incentivised was always in tension with the slow, deliberate, evidence-based processes that sound academic quality requires. OUG's non-profit, mission-driven model removes the incentive that created this tension.

> **The IU Lesson for OUG: Six Things to Do Differently**
>
> 1. **Transparent credential scope.** Before any programme is offered, OUG must verify and publicly document exactly what the qualification does and does not entitle the holder to do in their intended profession. Every programme page must include this information prominently, not buried in small print.
> 2. **ELM credentials from day one.** IU credentials are issued as PDFs. OUG credentials are ELM v3 — cryptographically sealed, machine-readable, employer-verifiable without contacting OUG. This is not just a quality signal; it is a structural protection for the student.
> 3. **Non-profit governance.** OUG's gGmbH structure and eventual Hochschule governance mean that academic quality decisions are made by academic governance bodies, not by a management team optimising for private equity returns.
> 4. **Partner HEI quality filter.** OUG's federated model means that courses are offered by accredited partner HEIs, not developed internally under growth pressure.
> 5. **Growth at the pace of quality.** OUG's five-year plan targets 5,000 students — less than 4% of IU's current enrolment. This is a deliberate choice to grow at the pace at which quality can be maintained.
> 6. **The student is a learner, not a lead.** IU's described sales process treats the prospective student as a sales lead. OUG's process treats the prospective student as a person making a significant investment in their own development who deserves complete, accurate information and no pressure.

## Part IV: OUG Strategic Positioning

### 4. OUG's Strategic Position

#### 4.1 The Position OUG Occupies

OUG's strategic position in this market can be defined with precision. It occupies a space that none of the three competitors currently fills: a non-profit, European-sovereign, ELM v3-credentialing, federated-platform Fernhochschule, charging €25/ECTS, designed from the ground up around the European Education Area's interoperability standards.

| Dimension | OUG Position | Why No Competitor Occupies This Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | €25/ECTS — among lowest in private HEI market | FernUni is cheaper (€0 for German students) but not a scalable platform model. IU charges 10–30× more. |
| **Mission** | Non-profit, public-good, European-sovereign | FernUni is non-profit but state-constrained and NRW-only. IU is explicitly for-profit. EuroTeQ is EU-grant-dependent. |
| **European credentials** | ELM v3 from day one — every grade, module and qualification | FernUni issues traditional German credentials. IU issues PDFs. EuroTeQ is developing micro-credentials but not full degree ELM. |
| **Digital sovereignty** | All 23 tools European-origin or sovereign-deployable open source | No competitor makes European digital sovereignty a founding principle. |
| **Federated platform model** | Partner HEIs contribute courses and share 40% ECTS revenue | FernUni courses cannot be shared commercially. IU develops its own content. EuroTeQ collaborates but has no revenue-sharing platform model. |
| **Scale pathway** | 5,000 → 30,000+ students; at 25K students: €10.5M+ annual surplus | FernUni cannot easily scale. IU has proven scale but at the cost of quality governance. EuroTeQ is constrained by partner institution capacity. |

## Part V: Strategic Recommendations

### 5. Strategic Recommendations

#### 5.1 The Positioning Statement

> OUG is what higher education should always have been: accessible to everyone who wants to learn, financially sustainable without private equity returns, digitally sovereign by design, and built on European standards that put credentials in the hands of learners — not institutions.
>
> It is not the cheapest option. It is the best value option.

#### 5.2 Immediate Strategic Actions (Year 1)

**Approach FernUni Hagen as a founding partner.** Write to the President of FernUniversität in Hagen before OUG's public launch. Frame the relationship as collaborative: OUG brings BW presence, European credential infrastructure, and a platform model; FernUni brings established programme content and academic credibility.

**Contact the EuroTeQ coordinator at TUM.** Approach TUM's EuroTeQ coordinator about OUG's participation in EuroTeQ 2030's digital infrastructure workstream.

**Be specific about what OUG is not.** OUG's marketing must be clear that it is not IU — not as a negative campaign, but as a positive statement of difference: European credentials, non-profit mission, transparent programme scope, no sales pressure.

**Engage DGB and ver.di networks.** The German trade unions have a direct interest in accessible, affordable, portable continuing education for their members. DGB's Bildungswerk is a natural early partner.

**Publish the framework.** Make the OUG's founding documents publicly available as open documents under a Creative Commons licence — establishing intellectual leadership and attracting partner HEIs who adopt the framework and thereby become natural network members.

#### 5.3 The Long-Term Vision

The three institutions compared in this study each represent a different answer to the same question: how should higher education work in a digitally networked Europe?

FernUniversität's answer is: it should be publicly funded, broadly accessible, and research-backed. This is a good answer, constrained by state funding dependency and NRW jurisdiction.

EuroTeQ's answer is: it should be a transnational alliance of elite institutions, funded by the EU, focused on innovation and European mobility. This is also a good answer, for the students who can access elite technical universities and benefit from premium professional education.

IU's answer is: it should be profitable, scalable, and responsive to market demand. This answer has proven that the demand is enormous, and it has also proven that the profit motive alone is not a sufficient governance framework for an institution whose purpose is to serve its students' long-term development rather than its investors' short-term returns.

OUG's answer is different from all three: higher education should be a public good, delivered on sovereign European infrastructure, with credentials that belong to the learner, at a price that reflects cost rather than market power, governed by academic communities that are accountable to their mission.

This is not a utopian vision. It is an achievable institution, planned in detail across the OUG document stack, with a specific accreditation pathway, a specific toolset, a specific financial model, and a specific timeline. OUG exists to prove that the answer to IU's success is not more IU — it is something better.

> **Final Note on the Neider Question.** The people who study at IU are not making a mistake. They are making the best choice available to them from the options the system has provided. IU deserves credit for providing an option that the public system failed to provide.
>
> But "better than nothing" is not the same as "good enough." The students who chose IU deserve an institution that is as accessible and flexible as IU, and that also puts their credential's long-term value, their data protection, and their accurate understanding of what their qualification entitles them to do ahead of its own growth targets.
>
> OUG is that institution. The goal is not to criticise IU. The goal is to make IU's approach to accessibility the floor of what every European higher education institution offers — and to make the European digital sovereignty, credential integrity, and non-profit mission that OUG provides the new standard.
