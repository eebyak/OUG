## Executive Summary

This report provides an update at the beginning of 2026 on the strategic trajectory toward establishing the Open University of Germany (OUG). It consolidates last year's activities towards pedagogical innovation, institutional learning, and system-level reflection into a coherent picture, showing how a set of complementary initiatives jointly advance the conditions under which an open, modular, and publicly anchored university model can emerge.

The motivation for OUG is structural rather than experimental. Germany's higher education system faces growing pressure from demographic change, rapid skills transformation, and increasing demand for lifelong, part-time, and non-linear study pathways. While pedagogical concepts for openness, digital learning, and internationalisation are well developed, existing institutional architectures — particularly in accreditation, recognition, governance, and workload management — are poorly aligned with these needs. As a result, openness remains fragile, often dependent on individual commitment rather than institutional capability.

The work described in this report reflects a deliberate shift from programme-level innovation to institution-building. Implementation through initiatives such as New Study demonstrated that these ideas are pedagogically viable under real conditions, while also revealing clear structural limits to scalability within existing frameworks. These insights directly informed the development of the Open University of Germany as a federated, public model designed to complement rather than replace existing universities.

OUG is pursued as a single project, but includes milestones that make up the portfolio of differentiated initiatives, each addressing a distinct class of structural challenge:

**OPEN STUDY (STIL)** provides the conceptual and pedagogical orientation, defining the values and educational logic that guide OUG, while providing the technical interoperability at skill level.

**OPEN PATHWAY (Erasmus KA2)** develops the institutional architecture required for openness to function reliably, focusing on governance, modularity, recognition, accreditation exchange, and interoperability.

**VIRTUAL STUDY (Erasmus KA1)** addresses the social and democratic reality of openness, establishing community, participation, and intercultural engagement as institutional functions rather than informal by-products.

**DIGITAL STUDY (Erasmus KA2)** builds the digital and AI-enabled capabilities necessary to support personalisation, quality assurance, and staff sustainability at scale, with a strong emphasis on ethics and trust.

These initiatives are intentionally interdependent. Conceptual orientation without institutional architecture remains aspirational; architecture without community risks becoming technocratic; community without digital capability does not scale; and digital capability without governance undermines legitimacy. Together, they move OUG from vision toward operability.

The accompanying appendix clarifies the financial logic underpinning this trajectory. OUG is conceived as a steady-state, publicly funded institution with predictable and scalable operating costs. Project funding does not finance the university itself; instead, it temporarily accelerates the development and validation of specific capabilities. This clear separation between baseline financing and project-based acceleration ensures resilience, transparency, and strategic independence.

If the proposed initiatives are funded, the expected outcome is not the immediate establishment of a standalone Open University of Germany, but a decisive increase in institutional readiness. Recognition processes become more predictable, modular pathways more robust, international participation more inclusive, and personalised learning more scalable without disproportionate staff burden. OUG emerges as a credible, operable framework embedded within public higher education.

For the advisory board, this report marks a transition from evaluating isolated innovations to accompanying an institutional emergence. While key questions — particularly regarding long-term financing and legal consolidation — remain open, the direction is now explicit and the pathway coherent. Taken together, the initiatives outlined here shift the Open University of Germany from aspiration to institutional possibility.

## Welcome 2026 — Where We Stand on the Path toward the Open University of Germany (OUG)

At the beginning of 2026, the discussion around the Open University of Germany (OUG) is continuing to move along. What began several years ago as a set of pedagogical experiments, strategic reflections, and pilot programmes (New Study) has gained a milestone plan with partial funding support (should they be granted). This report provides an update on where we stand today, how the individual initiatives now underway fit together, and how they collectively advance the development of OUG as a federated, public, and future-oriented university model.

The motivation for OUG is a societal necessity. Germany's higher education system faces structural pressures that can no longer be addressed through incremental programme-level innovation alone. Demographic change, an ageing workforce, increasing demand for lifelong and part-time study, and accelerating skills transformation across industry all point toward a fundamental mismatch between existing institutional structures and societal needs. At the same time, universities are expected to widen participation, support non-linear educational biographies, integrate digital and AI-supported learning, and maintain academic standards under growing resource constraints. These challenges are systemic in nature. They require responses at the level of institutional architecture, governance, and interoperability, not only at the level of individual courses or programmes (like, for example, New Study and many other innovations across Germany).

Over the past years, a recurring pattern has become visible across many innovation efforts in higher education: pedagogical concepts are available, digital tools are abundant, and willingness to change exists. What limits progress is not imagination, but structure. Credit recognition remains slow and unpredictable, accreditation frameworks are ill-suited to modular and cross-institutional learning, and personalised learning relies heavily on individual commitment rather than institutional capability. As a result, promising initiatives often remain isolated pilots, unable to scale sustainably within existing organisational and legal frameworks.

New Study, as a fully operating hybrid and English-taught programme, demonstrated that many elements associated with openness — digital delivery, international cohorts, strong community orientation, and explicit support for self-efficacy — can function under real institutional conditions. Importantly, New Study was not designed as a protected laboratory, but as a regular programme embedded in public higher education. This experience provided crucial evidence of operability. At the same time, it also made visible a central limitation: while such programmes can thrive pedagogically, they do not scale structurally within current frameworks. The demands placed on recognition processes, administrative coordination, digital infrastructure, and staff workload exceed what programme-based solutions can absorb. New Study, in this sense, has outgrown the institutional space available to it and requires a different structural context in order to develop further.

Between 2024 and 2025, this insight led to a decisive shift in focus: from developing innovative programmes to designing the institutional conditions under which openness can function reliably and at scale. The Open University of Germany emerged from this shift not as a single project, but as a federated model that builds on existing public universities while providing shared governance, recognition mechanisms, and digital capabilities at system level. OUG is conceived as an enabling structure: it does not replace universities, but connects them; it does not centralise teaching, but coordinates pathways; it does not lower standards, but makes standards operable across institutional boundaries.

By early 2026, the work toward OUG is organised as a coherent portfolio of initiatives, each addressing a distinct dimension of this institutional challenge. OPEN STUDY, whether funded or not, provides a conceptual orientation for Computer Science with interested partner HEIs. If funded, it also provides the technical interoperability required for any cross-HEI innovation — a major bottleneck currently faced by every single EU-Alliance. A forward-looking Erasmus OPEN PATHWAY cooperation initiative focuses on governance, accreditation exchange, micro-credentials, and interoperability, laying the structural backbone of OUG. A Virtual Exchange initiative addresses the social and democratic reality of openness, ensuring that community, participation, and intercultural learning are not treated as optional add-ons. A digital and AI-focused cooperation initiative develops the institutional capabilities required to personalise learning, redesign assessment, and use data ethically at scale. Each of these initiatives is viable in its own right; together, they form a staged pathway toward an operable Open University.

Key architectural questions — governance, recognition, digital capability, and sustainability — are being addressed explicitly and systematically. Even without funding, these issues are being addressed conceptually.

## Open Study (STIL Antrag für "Die Welt ist mein Campus")

OPEN STUDY describes the conceptual starting point of an Open University by designing sustainability microcredentials and lectures that students can choose across the five participating HEIs (Ostfalia, HAW Hamburg, Hochschule Reutlingen, Hochschule Heilbronn, DHBW). While OPEN STUDY was not designed as an institutional model, it articulates a pedagogical and normative reorientation to experiment with new institutional structures and motivates these with demographic developments in Germany. Should the proposal get funded, it shows EU-Alliance-like cooperation between HEIs across Germany and Baden-Württemberg in particular that are open to include more HEI on demand. OPEN STUDY was designed to support the larger conceptual idea of an OUG as a federated, public response to structural challenges in higher education.

At the core of OPEN STUDY is a reframing of the campus. Rather than treating the campus as a fixed physical location, OPEN STUDY conceptualised it as a distributed learning ecosystem composed of physical, digital, and social spaces. Learning is understood as taking place across institutions, borders, and life phases. This shift reflected broader societal realities: digitalisation, global connectivity, and increasingly non-linear educational biographies. From an OUG perspective, this idea directly informs a federated model at a later stage. If learning is no longer bound to a single place or institution, then governance, recognition, and responsibility must also be distributed rather than centralised.

A second central contribution of OPEN STUDY would be its emphasis on community over cohort. Learning was framed not as progression through a closed group with a fixed timeline, but as participation in purpose-driven communities of inquiry. These communities were characterised by shared responsibility for learning, dialogue across difference, and mutual support. This orientation anticipates more cooperative developments in virtual exchange, international collaboration, and lifelong learning across HEIs in Germany and abroad. For OUG, the community logic is not an optional pedagogical feature but a structural necessity: openness requires forms of belonging that function independently of physical presence and linear enrolment.

OPEN STUDY further positions learning as participation rather than consumption. Students are not treated as recipients of predefined content, but as active contributors to shared learning processes. Dialogue, co-creation, and visible engagement with uncertainty were central elements. This understanding aligns closely with democratic participation models and the recognition of diverse learning biographies. Within OUG, participation becomes a governance-relevant principle: systems designed for openness must enable learners to enter, contribute, pause, and re-enter without losing legitimacy or recognition.

A defining element of OPEN STUDY is its framing of education in terms of world stewardship and responsibility. Learning is explicitly linked to global challenges, long-term societal impact, and ethical engagement beyond immediate employability. This orientation resonates strongly with OUG's public mission and its alignment with sustainability and SDG-related frameworks. It clarifies that openness is not an end in itself, but a means to enable broader access to responsible participation in society.

Equally important is what OPEN STUDY deliberately did not attempt to solve. It did not address accreditation, recognition procedures, funding models, or governance machinery. It assumed that existing institutional structures would be able to absorb pedagogical innovation once its value became evident. Experience has shown that this assumption does not hold at scale. As OPEN STUDY-inspired practices may be implemented, recurring structural frictions will become visible: slow and unpredictable recognition processes, accreditation frameworks ill-suited to modular and cross-institutional learning, administrative overload, and reliance on individual commitment rather than institutional capability. These limitations will not negate the pedagogical validity of OPEN STUDY, but they reveal predictable structural gaps that OUG intends to explicitly address.

In this sense, OPEN STUDY would function as a diagnostic instrument. It will demonstrate that openness, community-based learning, and digital participation are viable and desirable, while simultaneously exposing the inability of current institutional architectures to support them sustainably. However, these insights through OPEN STUDY will directly inform the subsequent shift from programme-level innovation to system-level design, which underpins the OUG strategy, jointly with the other portfolio of initiatives described in this report.

OPEN STUDY as a conceptual idea, unless funded, will in any case serve as the conceptual reference point for OUG. It provides the language, values, and pedagogical orientation that can guide decisions across governance, community design, and digital capability building. OPEN STUDY is like a conceptual spine that ensures OUG develops not merely as a technical solution, but as a public university model grounded in openness, responsibility, and participation.

## Open Pathway: Inter-HEI Governance — Erasmus KA2

While the pedagogical rationale for openness has been articulated and tested through earlier initiatives, the development of the Open University of Germany (OUG) ultimately depends on the availability of an institutional architecture that can support openness reliably and at scale. OPEN PATHWAY addresses this requirement directly. Its purpose is not to introduce new teaching formats, but to design and validate the governance, recognition, and interoperability structures without which an open university model cannot function within the framework of public higher education.

The structural challenge addressed by OPEN PATHWAY is well documented. Germany's higher education system remains largely organised around linear degree programmes, institution-specific accreditation, and case-by-case recognition procedures. These structures are increasingly misaligned with societal needs shaped by demographic change, rapid skills transformation in industry, and European expectations regarding mobility and interoperability. While modular learning, micro-credentials, and flexible study pathways are widely discussed, they continue to rely heavily on individual negotiation and local goodwill. As a result, openness remains fragile, administratively costly, and difficult to scale.

OPEN PATHWAY responds to this mismatch by treating modularity, recognition, and accreditation as system-level functions rather than exceptional arrangements. A central contribution is the institutionalisation of modular learning pathways through stackable micro-credentials that are designed to connect coherently to bachelor-level qualifications. Modularity is no longer treated as a pedagogical experiment or add-on, but as an architectural principle embedded in programme design, quality assurance, and recognition processes. This approach directly supports lifelong learning, flexible entry and exit, and permeability between institutions — core objectives of OUG.

A second key contribution lies in the reframing of recognition and accreditation. OPEN PATHWAY moves away from bilateral, ad hoc recognition decisions toward rule-based, pre-defined recognition frameworks and accreditation exchange mechanisms. By establishing shared criteria, reference processes, and transparent decision paths, recognition becomes predictable, faster, and less dependent on individual interpretation. For OUG, this shift is decisive: recognition is transformed from a bottleneck into an enabling infrastructure that supports trust and scalability across participating institutions.

Governance is addressed through a federated rather than centralised model. OPEN PATHWAY deliberately avoids creating a new monolithic institution or concentrating authority in a single body. Instead, it develops shared governance structures that respect the autonomy of participating universities while enabling collective responsibility for quality, recognition, and openness. This federated approach aligns with the logic of public higher education, enhances political feasibility, and allows the model to scale beyond an initial consortium without undermining institutional diversity.

Interoperability is embedded as a foundational design principle throughout OPEN PATHWAY. Alignment with European frameworks such as ESCO, Europass, and the European Learning Model ensures that credentials, learning outcomes, and recognition processes are not only compatible across German institutions but also legible at the European level. Interoperability is understood in a comprehensive sense: semantic alignment of learning outcomes, procedural compatibility of recognition processes, and organisational coordination between institutions. This creates the conditions for future digital integration and European connectivity, while remaining grounded in existing legal frameworks and the work done by the European Digital Education Hub (EDEH).

Equally important is what OPEN PATHWAY does not attempt to do. It does not run study programmes, replace existing universities, or bypass accreditation law. Nor does it assume regulatory change as a precondition for progress. Instead, it works within current frameworks by designing interfaces, procedures, and shared standards that make openness operable under real conditions. In this sense, OPEN PATHWAY builds the operating system of OUG rather than its individual applications, without touching the technical IT infrastructure issue.

Within the overall OUG portfolio, OPEN PATHWAY provides the structural backbone. Initiatives such as OPEN STUDY articulate the pedagogical and normative rationale for openness, while programmes like New Study demonstrated feasibility under everyday institutional conditions. OPEN PATHWAY translates these insights into institutional design. Without this architectural layer, community-oriented initiatives risk remaining socially powerful but structurally fragile, and digital innovation risks becoming technologically sophisticated yet institutionally isolated. With OPEN PATHWAY, openness shifts from individual effort to institutional capability.

Taken together, OPEN PATHWAY marks the transition from innovation to institution-building. It establishes the governance, recognition, and interoperability structures that allow the Open University of Germany to function as a credible public institution rather than a collection of well-intentioned projects.

## Virtual Study (Erasmus KA1)

The Open University of Germany (OUG) is conceived not only as an institutional architecture, but as a public academic space that must be socially inhabited, experienced, and legitimised by its learners. VIRTUAL STUDY addresses this dimension directly. Its focus lies on the social, democratic, and participatory conditions under which openness can function sustainably. While OPEN PATHWAY establishes the structural rules of the system, VIRTUAL STUDY ensures that these structures are translated into lived academic experience.

The structural challenge addressed by VIRTUAL STUDY arises from the very openness OUG seeks to enable. Open, modular, and flexible study pathways increase heterogeneity among learners with respect to age, cultural background, prior education, work and care responsibilities, and modes of participation. Traditional universities have historically relied on physical co-presence, stable cohorts, and informal campus socialisation to create belonging and academic identity. These mechanisms no longer function reliably in distributed, hybrid, and non-linear learning environments. Without intentional community design, openness risks producing access without belonging, participation without voice, and flexibility without support.

VIRTUAL STUDY responds to this challenge by treating community-building as an institutional function rather than an emergent side effect. Communities are not left to develop informally, but are designed, facilitated, and resourced as part of the educational infrastructure. This approach recognises that peer interaction, mutual support, and shared responsibility for learning are essential for retention, trust, and academic success in open systems. Within OUG, community thus becomes infrastructure: a necessary condition for social sustainability rather than an optional enhancement.

Virtual Exchange plays a central role in this context. In VIRTUAL STUDY, it is not understood as a digital substitute for physical mobility, but as a distinct and complementary participation format. Virtual Exchange enables structured dialogue, collaborative inquiry, and intercultural learning without requiring travel. This is particularly relevant for non-mobile learners, adult students, and those with work or care obligations — groups that are central to OUG's mission but often underrepresented in traditional internationalisation models. Through Virtual Exchange, internationalisation becomes a default feature of participation rather than an exceptional opportunity.

A further core contribution of VIRTUAL STUDY lies in embedding democratic participation and learner voice into everyday academic practice. Learners are positioned as active participants in knowledge creation, dialogue, and reflection rather than passive recipients of content. Participation is supported through moderated formats, peer facilitation, and co-creative learning designs that make engagement visible and meaningful. This approach aligns with OUG's commitment to recognising diverse learning biographies and fostering trust in open systems. Democratic participation is not treated as an abstract value, but as a practised capability that strengthens legitimacy and accountability.

Inclusion and equity are operationalised through concrete design choices. VIRTUAL STUDY places explicit emphasis on accessible formats, varied modes of participation, and the creation of psychologically safe learning environments. Neurodiversity and heterogeneity are not framed as deficits to be compensated for, but as realities that require flexible and reflective participation structures. Inclusion is understood as an ongoing practice that must be actively designed, evaluated, and improved. This ensures that openness does not inadvertently reproduce existing patterns of exclusion or privilege.

VIRTUAL STUDY deliberately does not address accreditation, credentialing, or governance frameworks — these functions are provided by OPEN PATHWAY. Nor does it assume that community will emerge spontaneously from open access alone. Instead, it operates alongside the structural architecture of OUG, animating it through social practice.

Within the broader OUG portfolio, VIRTUAL STUDY fulfils a critical role. Initiatives such as OPEN STUDY articulated participation and responsibility as core educational values, while programmes like New Study demonstrated that strong learning communities can be sustained in hybrid environments. VIRTUAL STUDY builds on these insights by scaling community and participation beyond individual programmes. Without this social layer, OUG risks becoming administratively open but socially thin. With it, openness is experienced as meaningful participation, shared responsibility, and democratic engagement.

VIRTUAL STUDY thus provides the social and democratic fabric that allows the Open University of Germany to function as a community rather than merely a platform.

## Digital Study (KA2): Digital Capabilities for Scale

The Open University of Germany (OUG) is conceived as an open, modular, and inclusive institution operating across institutional boundaries and learner biographies. Such openness, however, increases heterogeneity, non-linearity, and asynchronous participation at a scale that cannot be managed sustainably through traditional organisational processes alone. DIGITAL STUDY addresses this challenge by developing the digital and AI-enabled capabilities required for OUG to function responsibly, human-centred, and at scale. Its purpose is not to drive change through technology, but to ensure that openness remains operable under real institutional conditions.

The structural digital challenge faced by OUG lies in the tension between flexibility and capacity. Open systems invite learners with diverse prior knowledge, learning speeds, life situations, and participation patterns. Traditional university models rely heavily on standardised cohorts, synchronous teaching, and high levels of manual staff intervention. As openness increases, these assumptions break down. Without appropriate digital support, personalised learning becomes dependent on individual teaching effort, staff workload rises unsustainably, and quality assurance becomes fragile. DIGITAL STUDY responds to this by reframing digitalisation as an institutional capability rather than a collection of tools.

A central contribution of DIGITAL STUDY is the institutionalisation of personalisation. Personalisation is treated not as a heroic act of individual teaching, but as a system property embedded in learning design and support structures. By enabling flexible pacing, adaptive pathways, and learner agency, DIGITAL STUDY supports diverse learner profiles, including adult learners and those with non-linear educational biographies. This approach is explicitly aligned with principles of Universal Design for Learning and neurodiversity-aware education, ensuring that flexibility serves equity rather than privilege. Within OUG, personalisation thus becomes a reliable public capability rather than an exception dependent on individual goodwill.

DIGITAL STUDY also addresses the responsible integration of artificial intelligence in teaching and assessment. AI is positioned as a support instrument that can enhance feedback, formative assessment, and learning support, while preserving academic judgement and responsibility. Clear boundaries are established regarding transparency of AI use, the distinction between collaboration and plagiarism, and the requirement for human-in-the-loop decision making. By embedding these principles institutionally, DIGITAL STUDY protects academic integrity and trust, which are essential for the legitimacy of credentials issued within an open system.

Learning analytics constitute a further key element. DIGITAL STUDY develops analytics approaches that are non-surveillant, student-facing, and supportive rather than punitive. The focus lies on feedforward, self-efficacy, and metacognitive awareness, enabling learners to understand their progress and make informed decisions about their learning paths. At the institutional level, analytics are used to inform teaching, identify support needs early, and enable reflective quality assurance. This evidence-informed approach strengthens OUG's ability to learn from its own operation without compromising learner autonomy.

Beyond learner-facing functions, DIGITAL STUDY explicitly addresses institutional and staff capability building. Open and digital education places new demands on academic and administrative staff, particularly with respect to assessment design, AI use, and data governance. DIGITAL STUDY therefore includes staff development and shared digital practices as core components. Rather than promoting isolated tool adoption, it establishes frameworks, guidelines, and governance structures that enable institutions to use digital and AI-supported approaches responsibly and consistently.

Ethics, data protection, and trust form the foundation of all digital development within DIGITAL STUDY. Compliance with data protection regulations, data minimisation, transparency, and contestability are embedded by design. Learners retain agency over their data and are informed about how digital systems support their learning. This ethical orientation is not an add-on, but a prerequisite for scale: without trust, digitalisation undermines rather than enables openness.

DIGITAL STUDY deliberately avoids centralising platforms, imposing specific technologies, or automating academic decisions. Its focus lies on principles, transferable practices, and institutional readiness. In this sense, it enables digital capability without creating new technical dependencies or undermining institutional autonomy.

Within the overall OUG portfolio, DIGITAL STUDY provides the operating capacity that allows governance structures and community-based participation to function under conditions of scale and diversity. While OPEN PATHWAY establishes the architectural framework and VIRTUAL STUDY builds the social and democratic fabric, DIGITAL STUDY ensures that openness remains manageable, evidence-informed, and future-proof. It provides the digital and AI-enabled capabilities that turn openness from an aspiration into a durable public capacity of the Open University of Germany.

## Synthesis and Trajectory

Taken together, the initiatives described in this report form a coherent and intentional pathway toward the Open University of Germany (OUG). They do not represent a collection of loosely related projects, nor do they attempt to realise an open university through a single intervention. Instead, they address different classes of structural problems that must be solved in parallel if openness is to function reliably within public higher education.

The underlying logic of this portfolio is differentiation by function. OPEN STUDY provides the conceptual and pedagogical orientation: a shared understanding of learning as distributed, participatory, and responsibility-oriented, with the underlying IT platform necessary for true interoperability at the level of exchanging module information digitally using EU standards. OPEN PATHWAY translates this orientation into institutional architecture by addressing governance, recognition, modularity, and interoperability. VIRTUAL STUDY ensures that openness is socially inhabited through community, participation, and democratic practice. DIGITAL STUDY develops the digital and AI-enabled capabilities required to sustain these structures under conditions of scale, heterogeneity, and non-linearity. Each initiative is necessary and forms a potential basis of evidence towards the OUG.

By the end of the current funding horizon, openness would no longer rely primarily on individual commitment or exceptional arrangements. Recognition processes would become more predictable and transparent, modular pathways more institutionally supported, international participation more accessible beyond physical mobility, and personalised learning more scalable without disproportionate staff burden. The Open University of Germany would not yet exist as a standalone institution, but it would exist as a credible, operable framework embedded within public higher education.

Beyond the immediate context, this trajectory has broader relevance. The challenges addressed — demographic change, skills transformation, digitalisation, and the need to maintain public trust in higher education — are not unique to any single institution. OUG is conceived as a public response to public challenges that demonstrates that openness can be governed, participation can be institutionalised, and digitalisation can be ethical and human-centred. In this sense, the work described here is not only preparatory for OUG, but also contributes to a wider discourse on the future of higher education in Germany and Europe.

For the advisory board, the key questions remain open, particularly with regard to long-term public financing, legal consolidation, and national coordination. These questions are acknowledged explicitly and are not prematurely resolved within project logic.

Taken together, the initiatives outlined in this report do not yet constitute the Open University of Germany. They do, however, decisively shift it from aspiration to institutional possibility.

## Appendix: Operational and Financial Foundations of the Open University of Germany (OUG)

### A. Purpose and Framing of This Appendix

This appendix clarifies how the Open University of Germany (OUG) is conceived as a financially and operationally viable public institution independent of project funding, while also showing how selected externally funded initiatives can accelerate maturity, quality, and readiness.

The financial model presented here is not a project budget. It describes the steady-state cost structure required to operate OUG responsibly as it grows. Project-based funding is treated as temporary capability acceleration, not as a substitute for baseline financing.

### B. Steady-State OUG Cost Model (Baseline — Unchanged)

The following cost categories constitute the non-negotiable baseline for operating OUG as a public university model. These elements must be financed independently of project success.

| Cost category | Description | Baseline status |
|---|---|---|
| Academic core | Teaching, assessment, supervision, curriculum maintenance, academic QA | Core |
| Governance & accreditation | Recognition processes, accreditation coordination, governance bodies, legal compliance | Core |
| Student & community services | Advising, inclusion support, baseline community facilitation | Core |
| Digital infrastructure (baseline) | Learning platforms, core IT operations, security & compliance | Core |
| Administration & coordination | Programme coordination, finance, reporting, evaluation | Core |

These categories define what it costs to run OUG responsibly once it exists. They scale predictably with learner numbers and institutional scope.

### C. Role of Project Funding: Controlled Augmentation, Not Substitution

Externally funded initiatives contribute selectively and temporarily to the development and validation of specific capabilities. They do not replace baseline staff, operations, or governance.

The logic applied throughout this appendix is: **Baseline** (required regardless of projects), **Augmented** (temporarily supported or accelerated by projects), **Nice-to-have** (quality or speed enhancements if funding is available).

### D. OPEN PATHWAY (KA2) — Architecture & Governance

OPEN PATHWAY contributes to the design and validation of institutional architecture but does not assume responsibility for permanent governance functions.

| Capability area | Baseline financing | Augmented by OPEN PATHWAY | Nice-to-have effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governance staff & bodies | Required | — | — |
| Recognition & accreditation processes | Required | Temporary design & modelling capacity | Faster maturity, fewer iterations |
| Legal & policy expertise | Required | External expertise during design phase | Higher initial robustness |
| QA procedures | Required | Pilot validation & documentation | Reduced long-term admin cost |

*Boundary: OPEN PATHWAY accelerates the quality and readiness of governance; it does not finance governance itself.*

### E. VIRTUAL STUDY (KA1) — Community & Virtual Exchange

VIRTUAL STUDY strengthens the social and participatory layer of OUG, without replacing core student services.

| Capability area | Baseline financing | Augmented by VIRTUAL STUDY | Nice-to-have effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student advising & support | Required | — | — |
| Baseline community facilitation | Required | — | — |
| Community formats & moderation | Required (minimal) | Additional facilitation capacity | Richer participation formats |
| International participation | Optional | Coordination & facilitation costs | Earlier international reach |
| Facilitator training | Optional | Development & delivery | Higher participation quality |

*Boundary: VIRTUAL STUDY enhances depth and reach of community; it does not carry responsibility for student support.*

### F. DIGITAL STUDY (KA2) — Digital & AI Capabilities

DIGITAL STUDY strengthens OUG's ability to operate at scale by reducing long-term operational risk and workload, not by paying for core IT.

| Capability area | Baseline financing | Augmented by DIGITAL STUDY | Nice-to-have effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core IT staff & platforms | Required | — | — |
| Security & compliance | Required | — | — |
| AI-supported feedback & assessment | Optional | Design, pilots, validation | Lower staff workload per learner |
| Learning analytics frameworks | Optional | Development & governance design | Better quality assurance |
| Staff digital capability | Optional | Training & shared practices | Faster institutional adoption |

*Boundary: DIGITAL STUDY does not finance IT operations; it reduces future cost and risk through better design.*

### G. OPEN STUDY — Conceptual Capital (Explicitly Non-Financial)

OPEN STUDY contributes indirectly to OUG operations by providing the digital backbone for interoperability. A €3 million project designed to provide interoperability with EU standards for five HEIs will also scale to an open-source digital backbone that Germany needs so desperately.

### H. Cross-Cutting Optional Enhancements (Only If Funded)

Across proposals, additional benefits may arise if funding is secured: earlier proof of scalable recognition; richer participation formats; reduced staff overload through AI-supported processes; stronger proof of concept for ministries and partners. These effects accelerate institutional learning and improve early quality — they do not determine viability.

The Open University of Germany is designed as a publicly funded institution with predictable and scalable operating costs. Project-based funding contributes selectively to the development, validation, and acceleration of specific capabilities, but does not substitute baseline financing. This separation ensures institutional resilience, financial transparency, and strategic independence as OUG grows.

## Accreditation To-Do List (Germany, HE Context)

### 1. Institutional Preconditions (Before Any Programme Is Reviewed)

**1.1 Legal hosting institution.**

**1.2 Grundordnung (Statutes).** Must explicitly allow: the degree type (B.Sc. Informatik); modular structure; external teaching contributions (if applicable); examination authority; digital / hybrid teaching formats; recognition of external credits (important!).

**1.3 Examination regulations (Prüfungsordnung / SPO).** Must include: admission requirements; credit structure (ECTS); assessment formats; grading rules; repetition rules; recognition rules (this is key for OUG); digital assessment provisions. Align SPO with modularity, recognition at scale, and AI-aware assessment rules (DIGITAL STUDY logic).

### 2. Programme-Level Accreditation (Informatik B.Sc.)

**2.1 Programme profile.** A clear academic identity is needed: Informatik (not "digital studies", not "applied tech"), aligned with the Fachqualifikationsrahmen Informatik, Bologna learning outcomes, and professional field relevance. To-do: explicitly position Informatik across core CS foundations, applied competence, and academic depth.

**2.2 Curriculum & Module Handbook (Modulhandbuch).** Each module must define: learning outcomes (knowledge, skills, competence); ECTS & workload; content; teaching format; assessment method; prerequisites; contribution to programme outcomes. To-do: make modules stand-alone recognisable, stackable, interoperable, and avoid institution-specific wording.

**2.3 Qualification objectives (Qualifikationsziele).** Must show academic qualification, professional qualification, personal development, and societal responsibility. To-do: explicitly link Informatik competence, lifelong learning, and digital responsibility — this aligns perfectly with the OPEN STUDY + OUG narrative.

**2.4 Study structure & progression.** Must show logical progression, foundational → advanced modules, thesis integration, and credit balance per semester. To-do: show that non-linear entry is possible while coherence is preserved.

### 3. Teaching Capacity & Resources

**3.1 Teaching staff.** Must show sufficient qualified professors, subject coverage, continuity, and examination authority. To-do: map who teaches what, who examines, who is responsible overall. For OUG: clarify delegated teaching vs. examination responsibility.

**3.2 Infrastructure.** IT systems, libraries, learning platforms, student support, examination security. To-do: explicitly describe digital infrastructure, online assessment safeguards, and student access.

### 4. Quality Assurance (QA)

**4.1 Internal QA system.** Evaluation of courses, student feedback, improvement loops, documentation. To-do: describe QA as institutional, systematic, and continuous.

**4.2 Programme responsibility & governance.** To-do: define programme director, committees, decision paths. For OUG: show federated governance clearly.

### 5. Recognition & Mobility

**5.1 Recognition rules.** Prior learning, external modules, international credits. To-do: move recognition from discretionary to rule-based (OPEN PATHWAY).

**5.2 Mobility & flexibility.** Mobility windows, flexibility, feasibility. To-do: show how modules can be taken elsewhere and credits flow back.

### 6. Accreditation Procedure Itself

**6.1 Choose accreditation type.** Programme accreditation.

**6.2 Choose agency.** E.g. AQAS, ASIIN (Informatik-friendly), ACQUIN.

**6.3 Self-evaluation report.** Narrative + evidence, referencing statutes, SPO, modules, and QA.

**6.4 Site visit (yes, still happens).** Interviews, clarification questions, trust-building.
