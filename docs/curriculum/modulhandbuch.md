## 1. Programme Overview and Academic Rationale

The Bachelor of Computer Science at the Open University of Germany (OUG) is designed as a modern, competence-oriented programme that prepares students for a rapidly evolving computational landscape.

The programme responds to three structural shifts in computer science education: the increasing role of intelligent and data-driven systems; the growing importance of systems thinking, security, and responsibility; and the need for lifelong, modular, and interoperable learning pathways.

Rather than organising computer science as a linear sequence of isolated subjects, the programme is structured around six coherent competency streams that reflect how computer science operates in practice: mathematical reasoning, software construction, systems and platforms, data and AI, human-centred design, and professional responsibility.

The programme is explicitly designed to support traditional full-time study, part-time and lifelong learning, and stackable microcredentials that can be accumulated toward the Bachelor degree.

## 2. Qualification Goals and Graduate Profile

Graduates of the Bachelor of Computer Science possess a scientifically grounded and practice-oriented understanding of computational systems.

They are able to: reason formally and algorithmically about computational problems; design, implement, and evaluate software systems; understand and operate within modern execution environments; apply data-driven and AI-based methods critically; design systems that account for human, ethical, and societal dimensions; and work responsibly in regulated and interdisciplinary contexts.

The programme explicitly moves beyond a narrow focus on programming syntax and tools. Instead, it emphasises computational thinking as a foundational medium for problem solving, expression, and system design.

Graduates are prepared both for direct entry into professional practice and continued academic study at Master level.

## 3. Curriculum Architecture

### 3.1 Stream-Based Structure

The curriculum is organised into six competency streams:

- **Stream A**: Mathematical & Algorithmic Foundations
- **Stream B**: Programming & Software Construction
- **Stream C**: Systems, Platforms & Security
- **Stream D**: Data, AI & Intelligent Systems
- **Stream E**: Human-Centred & Societal Computing
- **Stream F**: Research, Professional & Societal Practice

Each stream develops a coherent set of competencies and contributes explicitly to the overall qualification goals.

### 3.2 Semester-Based Progression

While streams define conceptual structure, the programme is delivered through a six-semester progression. Each semester integrates modules from multiple streams to ensure balanced development across theory, practice, systems understanding, and reflection.

Prerequisites are defined to ensure academic coherence while preserving flexibility for open pathways.

## 4. Teaching and Learning Concept

The programme follows a competence-oriented teaching and learning model. Key principles include:

**Active learning.** Students engage with problems, projects, and case studies rather than passively consuming content.

**Project-based learning.** Multiple modules involve applied projects that integrate knowledge across streams.

**Research-based learning.** Students are gradually introduced to scientific methods, culminating in independent research work.

**Reflective learning.** Learning diaries, portfolios, and feedback loops support metacognitive development.

**Responsible use of AI.** AI-based tools may support learning, but students are trained to evaluate, validate, and critically reflect on AI-generated outputs.

## 5. Assessment and Credit System

### 5.1 Assessment Philosophy

Assessment is aligned with learning outcomes and emphasises demonstrated competence rather than rote reproduction of knowledge. Assessment formats include written examinations, project work, programming assignments, seminar papers and presentations, and portfolios and reflective work. Each module specifies its assessment format explicitly.

### 5.2 Grading System

All modules are assessed using a graded system in accordance with German higher education standards. Graded assessment is a prerequisite for accreditation, credit transfer, and stackability toward the Bachelor degree.

### 5.3 ECTS and Workload

Each module is assigned ECTS credits reflecting the total student workload, including contact hours, self-study, project work, and assessment preparation. A full-time semester corresponds to 30 ECTS.

## 6. Open Pathways & Lifelong Learning Logic

The programme is explicitly designed for lifelong learning. Students may enter individual streams, complete stream-based certificates, pause and resume learning without loss of recognition, and stack certified learning outcomes toward the Bachelor degree.

Entry and exit points are formally defined. Learning outcomes achieved remain valid and recognised over time.

The Bachelor degree is awarded upon completion of all required streams and modules. Stream certificates and microcredentials are awarded independently and remain valid regardless of degree completion.

## 7. Curriculum Navigation: Streams, Semesters, and Learning Pathways

The Bachelor of Computer Science at the Open University of Germany is structured along two complementary dimensions: competency streams and temporal progression. Together, these dimensions provide both conceptual coherence and practical orientation while preserving flexibility for diverse learning biographies.

### 7.1 Streams as Conceptual Structure

The curriculum is organised into six competency streams. Each stream represents a coherent dimension of computer science education and contributes specific competencies to the overall graduate profile.

Streams are conceptual in nature. They define which types of competencies are developed, how learning outcomes relate to one another, and how individual modules contribute to the overall qualification goals.

Streams are not semesters, tracks, or specialisations. Instead, they function as organising principles that ensure intellectual coherence across modules and learning pathways.

Every module in the curriculum is assigned to exactly one stream. Together, the six streams form a complete and balanced profile of a computer science graduate.

### 7.2 Semesters as Temporal Orientation

In addition to the stream structure, the curriculum provides a recommended six-semester progression. This temporal structure supports planning, advising, and full-time study, particularly for learners who prefer a traditional academic rhythm.

The semester structure indicates when modules are typically offered, reflects prerequisite relationships between modules, and balances theoretical, practical, and reflective components over time.

Importantly, the semester structure represents a recommended progression, not a mandatory sequence. Learners may deviate from it, provided that module prerequisites and assessment requirements are met.

### 7.3 Learning Pathways and Flexibility

The programme explicitly supports multiple learning pathways, including full-time study, part-time study, episodic learning with interruptions, and lifelong learning with later degree aggregation.

Learners may complete modules from different streams in parallel, pause their studies without loss of recognition, and resume learning at a later point using previously earned credits.

Progression is determined by competency achievement, not by continuous enrolment or attendance.

### 7.4 Prerequisites and Progression Logic

Academic coherence is ensured through clearly defined prerequisites at the module level. Prerequisites specify required prior knowledge or competencies, recommended preparation, and, where applicable, adaptive learning options.

Prerequisites are defined in the module descriptions and serve to protect learners from overload, ensure meaningful participation in advanced modules, and maintain academic standards across flexible pathways.

### 7.5 Typical Study Patterns

To support orientation, the curriculum includes examples of typical study patterns, such as a standard six-semester full-time pathway, a part-time pathway with reduced semester load, and a stream-based pathway focused on microcredentials.

These examples are illustrative. They do not limit individual learning trajectories, nor do they replace academic advising.

### 7.6 Streams, Certificates, and the Bachelor Degree

Each competency stream can be completed independently and leads to a certified learning outcome. Stream-based certificates may be awarded once the defined modules of a stream have been successfully completed.

The Bachelor of Science degree is awarded when all required streams and modules, including the Bachelor thesis, have been completed in accordance with the programme requirements.

This structure enables learners to achieve recognised intermediate qualifications, build academic momentum over time, and combine learning outcomes into a full academic degree.

### 7.7 Reading the Curriculum Tables

Subsequent sections of this Modulhandbuch provide an overview of modules by semester and stream, followed by detailed module descriptions. Each module description specifies its stream affiliation, recommended semester placement, prerequisites, learning outcomes, assessment format, and ECTS workload.

Together, these elements allow learners, instructors, and reviewers to understand both the structure and the logic of the curriculum.

*Closing note (source): the curriculum is designed to be navigable, transparent, and adaptable. Streams provide conceptual clarity, semesters offer temporal orientation, and open pathways enable lifelong learning without compromising academic standards.*

## 8. The Competency Streams: Conceptual Overview

The Bachelor of Computer Science at the Open University of Germany is structured around six competency streams. Each stream represents a distinct but interdependent dimension of contemporary computer science education. Together, the streams form an integrated profile that prepares graduates for scientific, professional, and societal challenges in a rapidly evolving computational landscape.

The stream structure reflects the understanding that computer science is not a single linear skill set, but a layered system of competencies spanning formal reasoning, software construction, systems thinking, data-driven methods, human-centred design, and professional responsibility.

### 8.1 Stream A: Mathematical and Algorithmic Foundations

Stream A establishes the formal foundations of computer science. It develops the ability to reason precisely about computation, algorithms, and complexity using mathematical methods.

Learners in this stream acquire competencies in formal reasoning and abstraction, discrete mathematics and logic, algorithmic problem-solving, and complexity analysis and correctness arguments.

This stream ensures that graduates understand *why* computational methods work, not only *how* to apply them. It underpins all other streams and provides the intellectual discipline required for reliable, scalable, and verifiable systems.

### 8.2 Stream B: Programming and Software Construction

Stream B focuses on the construction of software systems as engineered artifacts. It develops the ability to translate abstract problem descriptions into robust, maintainable, and testable software.

Key competencies include programming paradigms and language concepts, software design and architecture, modularisation, testing, and debugging, and collaboration in software development contexts.

Rather than centring on specific tools or languages, the stream emphasises transferable principles of software construction that remain valid across technological change.

### 8.3 Stream C: Systems, Platforms, and Security

Stream C addresses computation as a systems-level phenomenon. Learners develop an understanding of how software operates within execution environments, networks, and platforms.

Competencies in this stream include operating systems and runtime environments, distributed and cloud-based systems, networking fundamentals, and security principles and threat models.

This stream prepares graduates to reason about performance, reliability, scalability, and security — essential capabilities in modern computing infrastructures.

### 8.4 Stream D: Data, Artificial Intelligence, and Intelligent Systems

Stream D focuses on data-driven computation and intelligent systems. It equips learners with the ability to work with data, models, and AI-based methods critically and responsibly.

Core competencies include data acquisition, management, and analysis, machine learning fundamentals, evaluation and validation of models, and ethical and societal implications of AI systems.

The stream emphasises AI collaboration rather than AI replacement, training graduates to act as informed designers, evaluators, and stewards of intelligent systems.

### 8.5 Stream E: Human-Centred and Societal Computing

Stream E situates computer science within its human and societal context. It develops the ability to design systems that are usable, inclusive, ethical, and aligned with human values.

Competencies include human–computer interaction and user experience, accessibility and inclusive design, social and ethical implications of computing, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

This stream ensures that graduates understand computing as a socio-technical practice, not merely a technical one.

### 8.6 Stream F: Research, Professional, and Reflective Practice

Stream F integrates scientific inquiry, professional skills, and reflective learning. It supports the development of metacognitive abilities and prepares learners for both academic and professional trajectories.

Key competencies include scientific methods and research literacy, professional communication and teamwork, self-regulated learning and reflection, and responsible practice in academic and industrial contexts.

This stream provides the connective tissue between the technical streams and supports lifelong learning, adaptability, and personal development.

### 8.7 Integration Across Streams

While each stream develops a distinct competency dimension, the curriculum is explicitly designed to foster integration across streams. Modules and projects frequently draw on competencies from multiple streams, reflecting the interdisciplinary nature of real-world computing problems.

The stream structure thus serves both as an organisational framework for curriculum design and as a cognitive scaffold that helps learners understand how different dimensions of computer science relate to one another.

### 8.8 Streams and Qualification Outcomes

Completion of a stream signifies the achievement of a coherent set of competencies within a defined domain of computer science. Streams may therefore serve as the basis for certified intermediate qualifications and stackable microcredentials.

The Bachelor degree represents the integration of all streams into a comprehensive academic qualification.

*Closing note (source): the competency streams reflect a deliberate move away from fragmented curricula toward a coherent, future-oriented model of computer science education. By structuring learning around enduring competency dimensions rather than transient technologies, the programme ensures both academic depth and long-term relevance.*

## 9. Temporal Structure: Semesters and Progression

While the competency streams define the conceptual architecture of the programme, the Bachelor of Computer Science also provides a recommended temporal structure to support planning, orientation, and full-time study.

This temporal structure is expressed through a six-semester model that reflects a typical progression from foundational competencies to advanced integration and independent work.

### 9.1 Purpose of the Semester Structure

The semester structure serves three primary purposes: **orientation** (offers learners a clear reference for planning their studies); **coherence** (reflects prerequisite relationships and increasing levels of complexity); and **advising** (supports academic guidance and workload balancing).

The semester structure is recommendatory, not mandatory. Progression through the programme is determined by the successful completion of modules and the fulfilment of prerequisites, not by adherence to a fixed timeline.

### 9.2 Recommended Six-Semester Progression

The programme is organised around six semesters, each corresponding to a thematic and competency-oriented focus: **Semesters 1–2** emphasise foundational competencies, including mathematical reasoning, introductory programming, and basic systems understanding. **Semesters 3–4** deepen technical competencies, integrate systems and data-oriented perspectives, and introduce more complex projects. **Semesters 5–6** focus on advanced integration, specialisation across streams, research-oriented work, and the Bachelor thesis.

Modules are distributed across semesters to ensure a balanced development across streams rather than isolating competencies into sequential blocks.

### 9.3 Parallelisation and Prerequisite Logic

Modules from different streams are intentionally offered in parallel within semesters. This enables learners to develop multiple competency dimensions concurrently, apply knowledge across streams, and experience computer science as an integrated discipline.

Academic coherence is ensured through explicit prerequisites, defined at the module level. These prerequisites regulate progression without imposing a rigid temporal order.

### 9.4 Flexible Study Modes

The recommended semester structure supports multiple study modes, including full-time study following the six-semester progression, part-time study with reduced semester workload, episodic learning with interruptions and later resumption, and lifelong learning with gradual accumulation of credits.

Learners are not required to justify deviations from the recommended timeline. The decisive criterion for progression is the achievement of learning outcomes.

### 9.5 Relation Between Semesters and Streams

Semesters and streams fulfil complementary roles: streams define *what* competencies are developed; semesters suggest *when* competencies are typically developed.

A single semester typically includes modules from multiple streams. Conversely, a single stream spans several semesters. This design avoids narrow specialisation and supports the development of well-rounded computer scientists.

### 9.6 Visual Curriculum Representation

To support orientation, the curriculum is represented using tables that map modules to streams, modules to recommended semesters, and prerequisites across modules. These tables provide a compact overview of the curriculum structure and support both academic planning and administrative processes.

*Closing note (source): the temporal structure of the programme balances clarity and flexibility. It offers a coherent progression model without constraining individual learning trajectories. This approach reflects the programme's commitment to academic rigour, openness, and lifelong learning.*

## 10. Preamble: Educating Computer Scientists for the Future

Computer science has become a foundational discipline shaping nearly every domain of modern society. Its impact extends far beyond software and hardware, influencing how decisions are made, how knowledge is produced, and how human and social systems operate. As a result, the education of computer scientists carries both scientific and societal responsibility.

This Bachelor of Computer Science is grounded in the conviction that future computer scientists must be more than proficient users of tools or fluent writers of code. They must be capable of reasoning about complex systems, working critically with data and artificial intelligence, designing technology in relation to human needs, and acting responsibly within societal and ethical frameworks.

The programme therefore rejects a narrow understanding of computer science education focused on transient technologies or syntactic mastery alone. Instead, it is built around enduring competency dimensions: formal reasoning, software construction, systems thinking, data-driven and intelligent methods, human-centred design, and reflective professional practice.

A second guiding principle of the programme is openness. Learning in computer science no longer follows a single, linear trajectory confined to early adulthood or uninterrupted full-time study. The programme is designed to support lifelong learning, flexible entry and exit, and diverse educational biographies, while maintaining clear academic standards and accredited qualifications.

This openness is not a relaxation of academic expectations. On the contrary, it requires explicit structures, transparent prerequisites, and rigorous assessment aligned with learning outcomes. Modularisation, stream-based organisation, and stackable microcredentials serve to make expectations visible and achievements recognisable, not to fragment or dilute the curriculum.

The programme further acknowledges the reality of intelligent tools and digital systems as integral elements of contemporary computing practice. Students are educated to engage with such tools critically and responsibly, understanding their capabilities, limitations, and implications rather than treating them as substitutes for human judgement or understanding.

Finally, the programme affirms that computer science is a human and societal endeavour. Questions of usability, accessibility, ethics, sustainability, and responsibility are not treated as peripheral topics but as integral components of technical education. Graduates are expected to understand the broader contexts in which computational systems are developed and deployed.

This Bachelor of Computer Science aims to educate computer scientists who are technically grounded, intellectually independent, socially responsible, and capable of continual learning. It provides a structured yet adaptable framework that prepares graduates not only for today's professional roles, but for long-term participation in an evolving computational world.

## Annex A: Worked Example — Flagship Module: Foundations of Programming

*The only module in the current document fully described using the Section 12 template. Included here in full, both as a worked example of the template and as the one genuine piece of module-level curriculum content available.*

### A. Formal Module Metadata (Authoritative — from JSON)

*The following elements are taken directly from the canonical module JSON and are not modified.*

- **Module Title:** Programming
- **Module Code / ID:** T4INF1004
- **Level:** Bachelor (EQF 6)
- **Duration:** 2 semesters
- **ECTS Credits:** 5 ECTS
- **Total Workload:** 150 hours
- **Contact Hours:** 84 hours
- **Module Type:** Mandatory
- **Assessment Type:** Design-based examination (*Entwurf*)
- **Grading:** Numeric grade, included in final degree grade
- **Language of Instruction:** German (English delivery possible in OUG context)

### B. Learning Outcomes (Authoritative — from JSON)

*These learning outcomes define the accreditable competence contract of the microcredential and remain unchanged.*

**Subject-Specific Competencies.** Learners understand the fundamental elements of procedural and object-oriented programming, know the syntax and semantics of programming languages, and are able to select and apply appropriate data structures for given problems.

**Methodological Competencies.** Learners design, implement, and test program solutions independently, apply structuring techniques to develop correct and maintainable programs, and use development environments and debugging tools effectively.

**Personal and Social Competencies.** Learners discuss, justify, and further develop program designs in teams, analyse and evaluate existing code, and independently acquire new tools and programming environments.

### C. Role and Positioning of the Module (OUG-Added, Interpretive)

**C1. Role within Stream B (Programming & Software Construction).** *Foundations of Programming* is the entry and anchor module of Stream B. It establishes the conceptual and cognitive foundations upon which all subsequent software-related modules build.

Within Stream B, this module develops the learner's ability to think algorithmically rather than procedurally by imitation, reason about program structure and behaviour, and take ownership of program correctness and design quality.

It introduces programming as a discipline of abstraction, modelling, and controlled formal expression, not merely as the use of a programming language.

All later modules in Stream B (e.g. advanced programming, software engineering, systems development) assume the competencies established here.

**C2. Contribution to the Bachelor Qualification Profile.** At Bachelor level, *Foundations of Programming* fulfils three critical functions:

1. **Epistemic grounding.** It introduces programming as a formal, rule-governed activity that connects mathematical reasoning, system thinking, and real-world problem solving.
2. **Capability equalisation.** The module is designed to accommodate highly heterogeneous prior knowledge. It guarantees a shared competence baseline for all learners entering the programme, regardless of background.
3. **Learning culture formation.** It establishes expectations regarding independent work, reflection, collaboration, and responsible use of digital and AI-based tools — norms that apply across the entire degree.

In this sense, the module does not only teach programming; it initiates students into the academic culture of computer science.

**C3. Position in the Semester-Based Progression.** In the semester-based view, this module is placed in Semester 1 and spans two semesters. This deliberate design choice reflects its foundational nature.

In the early phase of study, the module runs in parallel with mathematical and conceptual foundation modules, allows learners to build programming competence gradually and sustainably, and supports iterative deepening rather than compressed skill acquisition.

By extending over two semesters, the module enables repeated practice, progressive abstraction, and consolidation through increasingly complex tasks. This pacing is essential to prevent superficial learning and to support long-term competence development.

### D. Module Content (Authoritative Core + OUG Framing)

**D1. Core Content (from JSON).** Procedural programming concepts; object-oriented programming concepts; syntax and semantics of a programming language; basic data structures; program design, implementation, and testing.

**D2. OUG Conceptual Framing.** The content is taught with an explicit focus on transferable concepts rather than language-specific features. Programming languages are treated as vehicles for expressing ideas, not as ends in themselves.

Learners are encouraged to reason about multiple possible solutions, compare design alternatives, and articulate why a given solution works.

### E. Teaching and Learning Approach (OUG-Added)

The module follows a competence-progressive and feedforward-oriented didactic model: structured conceptual input for shared grounding; guided exercises to stabilise understanding; open-ended problems to stretch advanced learners; reflective elements to foster metacognitive awareness.

AI-based tools may be used to support exploration and feedback, but learners remain responsible for understanding solutions, explaining decisions, and validating program behaviour.

### F. Assessment (Authoritative Form + OUG Interpretation)

**F1. Formal Assessment (from JSON).** Assessment Type: Design-based examination. Grading: Numeric grade (standard scale). Microcredential Status: Fully graded, credit-bearing, stackable.

**F2. OUG Interpretation.** The assessment verifies that learners can independently design a programming solution, implement and test it correctly, and justify structural and algorithmic decisions. The assessment emphasises understanding and reasoning, not memorisation or mechanical reproduction.

### G. Microcredential, Stream, and Degree Integration (OUG-Added)

*Foundations of Programming* is a core stackable microcredential of the Bachelor of Computer Science.

Successful completion certifies foundational programming competence, contributes directly to Stream B certification, and is fully creditable toward the Bachelor degree.

As a standalone microcredential, it also serves lifelong learners who seek to (re)enter computer science, update foundational skills, or prepare for further academic or professional learning.
