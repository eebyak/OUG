### 4.2a The Eight Functional Roles (Human)

The following roles describe what the human author contributes within the collaborative workflow. These roles are not mere negations of AI capabilities ("what the AI didn't do"); they are positive descriptions of intellectual labour that the human must exercise and protect. Making these roles explicit gives students a vocabulary for articulating their own contribution that is specific enough to be genuinely informative in a disclosure statement.

| **Role** | **Description** | **Typical engineering writing context** |
|----------|----------------|----------------------------------------|
| **1. Problem Framer** | Defines the research question, identifies the gap, establishes why the problem matters and to whom. | Thesis: articulating the engineering problem that motivates the entire project; justifying its significance and scope. |
| **2. Domain Authority** | Supplies disciplinary knowledge, technical constraints, standards, and real-world context that the AI cannot access. | All stages: knowing which technical standards apply, what manufacturing constraints exist, which prior approaches failed in practice, what the company actually needs. |
| **3. Decision Maker** | Makes and owns all consequential intellectual choices --- selecting between alternatives, committing to a design path, accepting or rejecting AI proposals. | Year 2/3: choosing the architecture, the evaluation methodology, the requirements prioritisation, the test parameters. |
| **4. Evidence Interpreter** | Evaluates whether outputs (AI-generated or otherwise) meet domain-specific quality criteria; assigns meaning to results. | Thesis: determining whether test results validate the design; identifying whether a code output is correct vs. merely plausible; judging whether a literature synthesis captures the field accurately. |
| **5. Value Arbiter** | Introduces ethical, professional, and stakeholder commitments that shape what "good" means in this context. | All stages: deciding safety margins, accessibility requirements, sustainability trade-offs, professional accountability, what is acceptable to delegate. |
| **6. Synthesis Integrator** | Connects disparate elements into a coherent intellectual whole --- linking problem to literature to design to evaluation. | Year 3: ensuring the thesis reads as one sustained argument rather than a collection of sections; identifying where the design does not actually address the problem stated. |
| **7. Experience Bearer** | Contributes lived, practical, or situated knowledge from internships, lab work, or professional phases that gives the work authenticity. | Year 1 practical report: the observations, failures, and insights from the company placement that no AI can invent; the specific machine, process, or codebase the student actually worked with. |
| **8. Critical Reflector** | Steps back from the work to evaluate its limitations, identify blind spots, and articulate what remains unknown or unresolved. | Year 3: the honest critical reflection section that distinguishes competent from excellent work; acknowledging where AI use may have introduced limitations. |

The human roles span a spectrum from foundational (Roles 1, 2, 7) to evaluative and integrative (Roles 3, 4, 6, 8). From a pedagogical perspective, the spectrum matters because different roles carry different implications for what the student is actually learning. A student who exercises the Problem Framer (Role 1) and Synthesis Integrator (Role 6) is doing intellectually demanding work even if the AI handles expression and formatting. A student who delegates the Decision Maker (Role 3) and Evidence Interpreter (Role 4) roles to the AI --- for example, by accepting an AI-proposed design without independent evaluation --- has bypassed the engineering judgement that the grading framework is designed to assess.

The human roles are grounded in the same inductive origin as the AI roles: they were identified by asking, in sustained research practice, not only "what is the AI doing here?" but "what am I doing that the AI cannot do, and what am I doing that I must not delegate?" This second question is what the transparency framework requires students to answer.

## 5. Pedagogical Implementation

### 5.1 The DDL Vocabulary as Disclosure Framework

The primary pedagogical application of the DDL framework is as a shared vocabulary for AI disclosure. Students are introduced to the eight AI roles, the eight human roles, and the four-step loop at the beginning of the programme and are expected to use this vocabulary in the AI transparency sections of all three major written outputs.

Rather than writing "AI was used for editing assistance," a student using the DDL vocabulary would complete a two-column disclosure for each major section of their work:

**AI Roles Used:**
- Literature Synthesiser (Role 4): identifying connections across sources on neural network architectures
- Writing Collaborator (Role 7): improving clarity and academic register of the requirements section

**Human Roles Exercised:**
- Problem Framer (Role 1): identified the research gap and defined the scope based on company placement experience
- Decision Maker (Role 3): selected the design approach after evaluating three alternatives proposed by the AI
- Evidence Interpreter (Role 4): evaluated whether the literature synthesis accurately represented the state of the field
- Value Arbiter (Role 5): determined that safety requirements took priority over performance optimisation

This level of specificity is both more honest and more useful: it allows the examiner to understand what the student actually did, and it prompts the student to think about what they actually did before writing the disclosure. **Critically, the two-column structure makes visible what would otherwise remain hidden: a disclosure statement that lists AI roles but cannot identify corresponding human roles is itself a diagnostic signal that the student has not maintained authorial control over the intellectual content of the work.**

The shift from boilerplate to specific disclosure is not merely administrative. It is a metacognitive prompt. A student who must identify which DDL roles the AI played *and* which human roles they exercised in their work must first reflect on the division of labour in their process. This reflection is itself valuable: it makes explicit what the student contributed, and what they did not, in a way that supports honest self-assessment and identifies where their own capabilities need further development.

### 5.2 Mapping DDL Roles to AI Maturity Levels

The maturity levels are now assessed against *both* AI role use and human role exercise. A student at Level 1 (Dependent Use) is characterised not only by uncritical delegation to AI but by an inability to name which human roles they exercised. A student at Level 4 (Expert/Ethical Use) is characterised by intentional, balanced engagement of both AI and human roles, with explicit justification for why each role was assigned to human or machine in each context.

| **AI Maturity Level** | **Characteristic AI Role Use** | **Characteristic Human Role Exercise** | **Corresponding workbook criteria** |
|----------------------|-------------------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Level 1 --- Dependent Use | Roles 3, 7 dominate; student delegates intellectual content to AI; no critical evaluation of outputs. | Human roles are not named or are falsely claimed (e.g., "I wrote it" when the AI generated the text). Student cannot articulate what they contributed beyond "I asked the AI." | "I use AI mainly to get answers quickly. I cannot judge whether AI output is correct." |
| Level 2 --- Assisted Use | Roles 3, 7 used with active evaluation; student provides structure and content; AI assists with expression and clarity. | Problem Framer (Role 1), Experience Bearer (Role 7), and Decision Maker (Role 3) are exercised but not explicitly named. Student contributes content but may not recognise the intellectual value of their own input. | "I verify AI output with my own reasoning. I use AI to improve clarity, not skip thinking." |
| Level 3 --- Strategic Use | Roles 1, 2, 4, 6 active alongside 3 and 7; student uses AI to challenge and extend their own thinking; explicit iterative loop. | All human roles are exercised consciously. Student can name which human roles they protected in each section and why. Evidence Interpreter (Role 4) and Value Arbiter (Role 5) are regularly active. | "I use AI as a partner (debugging, brainstorming, review). I can explain why I accept or reject AI output." |
| Level 4 --- Expert/Ethical Use | All relevant roles used intentionally; student makes explicit decisions about when and how to engage each role; transparency and citation are integrated practice. | Human roles are exercised with strategic awareness. Student can articulate when they chose *not* to use AI (e.g., "I did not use AI for the critical reflection because that requires authentic self-assessment"). Synthesis Integrator (Role 6) and Critical Reflector (Role 8) are consistently demonstrated. | "I adapt AI use to context. I help peers develop responsible AI strategies. I ensure transparency and cite AI contributions appropriately." |

This mapping serves three pedagogical purposes. First, it gives students a developmental framework for understanding their own AI use --- not as a binary choice between using AI and not using it, but as a progression of increasing strategic sophistication in managing the human-AI division of labour. Second, it connects AI literacy to the broader developmental dimensions of the workbook. Third, and most critically, it makes visible the diagnostic signal that the original framework lacked: a student who can name AI roles but not human roles is not at Level 2 or above, regardless of how sophisticated their AI use appears.

### 5.3 Alignment with the Evaluation Framework

| **Grading framework element** | **DDL loop stage** | **Appropriate AI roles** | **Required human roles** |
|------------------------------|-------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| Problem identification | Authorial Input --- the student defines the problem | Role 1 (Interlocutor) to challenge and sharpen the problem statement | **Problem Framer (Role 1)**: student must own the identification of the gap and its significance; **Experience Bearer (Role 7)**: practical context that motivates the problem |
| Literature grounding | AI-Mediated Processing | Role 4 (Literature Synthesiser) with Role 1 (Interlocutor) to identify gaps | **Domain Authority (Role 2)**: knowing which sources matter in this field; **Evidence Interpreter (Role 4)**: judging whether the synthesis is accurate and complete |
| Requirements derivation | Authorial Evaluation --- student decides which requirements follow | Role 6 (Stress Tester) to identify gaps | **Decision Maker (Role 3)**: selecting and prioritising requirements; **Value Arbiter (Role 5)**: determining which requirements are non-negotiable |
| Design decisions | Authorial Input --- student makes and justifies design choices | Role 2 (Structural Editor) to organise; Role 1 (Interlocutor) to challenge alternatives | **Decision Maker (Role 3)**: committing to a design path; **Domain Authority (Role 2)**: knowing which constraints apply; **Synthesis Integrator (Role 6)**: ensuring the design addresses the problem |
| Implementation | AI-Mediated Processing + Iterative Refinement | Role 8 (Production Engineer) for code; Role 3 (Document Builder) for documentation | **Evidence Interpreter (Role 4)**: verifying that code is correct, not merely plausible; **Decision Maker (Role 3)**: accepting or rejecting implementation choices |
| Evaluation | Authorial Evaluation --- student designs and interprets | Role 6 (Stress Tester) to identify weaknesses | **Evidence Interpreter (Role 4)**: assigning meaning to results; **Decision Maker (Role 3)**: determining whether the evaluation supports the claims |
| Critical reflection | Authorial Input --- student reflects on limitations and future work | Role 1 (Interlocutor) to surface blind spots | **Critical Reflector (Role 8)**: this role cannot be delegated; authentic self-assessment requires the student's own evaluative judgement; **Value Arbiter (Role 5)**: acknowledging ethical or professional limitations |

This mapping makes visible a crucial pedagogical point: the grading framework rewards the elements of the chain that are fundamentally matters of human judgement --- identifying the problem, deriving requirements, making and justifying design decisions, interpreting evaluation results, and reflecting critically on limitations. These are precisely the steps in the DDL loop that correspond to Authorial Input and Authorial Evaluation --- the steps that belong to the student, and that map to the human functional roles. **The AI's contribution, however valuable, is concentrated in the processing and refinement steps. A student whose disclosure statement reflects this understanding --- naming both the AI roles used and the human roles exercised at each step --- has demonstrated not only honest transparency but also the metacognitive grasp of their own work that the grading framework's highest levels require.**

### 5.4 Implementation Across the Three-Stage Progression

In Year 1, the emphasis is on honest description of *both* AI and human roles. Students learn that "I wrote it" is not a sufficient human disclosure --- they must name which human roles they exercised (e.g., "I was the Experience Bearer: these observations come from my company placement"). This establishes from the outset that disclosure is a two-column exercise, not a single statement about AI use.

In Year 2, students engage more deeply with the iterative loop, learning to use the DDL not only for disclosure but as a process model for their project work. They are introduced to the concept of open-trajectory research --- work in which the structure and content of the output are not fully specified at the outset --- and they practise the plan-and-test cycle that the DDL describes. The disclosure statement for the Year 2 write-up is expected to be more specific than the Year 1 report, reflecting the student's developing awareness of the division of labour in their writing process. The AI maturity self-reflection is updated to track development from Year 1.

In Year 3, students engage with the full DDL framework as both a process model and a transparency tool. The disclosure section of the capstone and bachelor thesis is expected to provide a detailed two-column account: which AI roles played at which stages, which human roles were exercised at each step, and how the iterative loop was managed. Students are explicitly asked to identify sections where they chose *not* to use AI and to justify that choice in terms of human role protection (e.g., "I did not use AI for the critical reflection because Critical Reflector is a role that requires authentic self-assessment and cannot be delegated without defeating its purpose"). This level of transparency is not only more honest; it is also more useful to the examiner, because it makes the student's intellectual contribution legible in a way that boilerplate disclosure does not. The AI maturity self-reflection is completed for the final time, providing a longitudinal record of development across the three years.

## 6. Theoretical Grounding

### 6.1 Threshold Concepts and AI Literacy

The pedagogical rationale for the DDL framework is strengthened by independent empirical research on how students develop productive AI literacy. Anders and Dux Speltz (2026) investigated threshold concepts for AI-assisted writing --- conceptual transformations that prove troublesome yet irreversible once understood --- through an experimental "AI and Writing" course with undergraduate students from diverse academic backgrounds. Their analysis identified three threshold concepts essential for productive AI engagement.

The first is that writing with AI is an experimental process, requiring iterative refinement rather than transactional exchange. Students who begin with the expectation that AI functions as a more capable search tool --- providing usable outputs from simple queries --- must cross a threshold of recognition that productive AI use requires a plan-and-test cycle in which prompts, outputs, and revisions are directed and evaluated against the writer's own goals. The DDL's four-step loop instantiates this experimental process as a structured practice.

The second is that writing with AI requires the writer to supply situated expertise and evaluative judgement, rather than deferring to fluent AI output. Students who conflate linguistic fluency with functional competence --- assuming that authoritative-sounding AI output reflects genuine understanding --- must cross a threshold of recognition that their own disciplinary knowledge is what makes productive collaboration possible. The DDL's Authorial Input and Authorial Evaluation steps make this requirement structurally explicit: the student must contribute domain knowledge at the start of each loop iteration and must evaluate AI output against their own criteria at every step.

The third is that writing with AI should augment rather than replace the writer's rhetorical agency. Students who approach AI as a means of automating the cognitive work of writing must cross a threshold of recognition that the choices that most shape both the quality of their output and the development of their own competencies are the ones they must protect from delegation. The DDL's insistence that the author retains final authority over all intellectual decisions encodes this principle as a methodological requirement.

The addition of explicit human functional roles addresses a fourth threshold concept that emerged from classroom practice: **that transparency requires symmetrical disclosure**. Students who begin with the assumption that declaring AI use is sufficient must cross a threshold of recognition that meaningful transparency requires articulating what the human contributed, not only what the AI performed. Without this symmetry, disclosure becomes a compliance exercise that obscures rather than reveals the division of labour. The two-column disclosure structure makes this threshold visible: a student who cannot complete the human-roles column has not yet crossed it.

These four threshold concepts map directly onto the DDL's structure, providing independent empirical evidence that the framework's operating principles correspond to the conceptual transformations that students actually need to undergo in order to engage AI productively. The DDL vocabulary --- the eight AI roles, the eight human roles, the four-step loop, the distinction between what the student contributes and what the AI contributes --- provides the scaffolding that helps students cross these thresholds rather than remaining in the liminal phase of unproductive or unreflective AI use.

## 7. Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

### 7.1 What the Framework Offers

The DDL framework addresses a specific gap in current approaches to AI use in engineering education: the absence of a shared vocabulary that enables students to disclose AI use meaningfully, to reflect on their own contribution honestly, and to understand the relationship between AI use and the competencies that their programme is designed to develop.

Generic AI policies --- which typically specify whether AI use is permitted, and require some form of disclosure, without providing a framework for what meaningful disclosure looks like --- do not solve this problem. They produce compliance without understanding. The DDL framework, by contrast, connects the transparency requirement to a process model (the four-step loop), a dual role taxonomy (the eight AI functional roles and the eight human functional roles), a developmental progression (the AI maturity levels in the workbook), and an evaluation framework (the grading chain). Students who understand this connection do not experience AI disclosure as an administrative obligation; they experience it as a reflection of their own practice.

The framework now offers something that the original version did not: structural protection against the compliance-only disclosure that the author observed in practice. By requiring students to name both AI roles and human roles, the framework makes it impossible to produce a complete disclosure statement for entirely AI-generated work. A disclosure that lists AI roles but leaves the human-roles column empty or vague is itself a signal that requires pedagogical intervention, not merely a grading penalty. This structural protection is particularly important in the context of the EU AI Act (European Union, 2024), which requires that users have sufficient AI literacy to interact with AI systems responsibly. The two-column disclosure framework operationalises this requirement at the level of educational practice by making human contribution visible and assessable.

The framework also offers something that generic AI policies cannot provide: a positive account of what good AI use looks like. Rather than defining AI use primarily in terms of what is prohibited or what must be disclosed, the DDL defines it in terms of what productive collaboration requires --- authorial input, iterative evaluation, maintained authority over intellectual decisions, honest transparency about the division of labour, and explicit articulation of the human contribution. This positive account gives students something to aspire to rather than merely something to comply with.

---

### 7.2 Limitations

This paper presents the DDL framework as a theoretically grounded proposal for pedagogical implementation. It does not report empirical data on student outcomes, and its claims about the framework's effectiveness are therefore theoretical rather than evidential. The argument rests on the coherence of the framework itself, its alignment with established research on metacognition, threshold concepts, and transparency in AI-assisted writing, and its fit with the specific pedagogical context in which it is proposed.

The framework was developed in a specific institutional context --- a German dual-study engineering programme with a distinctive practical-phase structure --- and its applicability to other engineering programmes or other disciplinary contexts will require further investigation. The longitudinal structure of the programme, which allows the framework to be introduced in Year 1 and developed across three years, may not be replicable in programmes with shorter or less structured writing progressions.

The eight-role taxonomy for AI, while grounded in documented research practice, was not developed through empirical study of student AI use in educational contexts. Students may use AI in ways that do not map cleanly onto the eight roles, or may find the taxonomy difficult to apply without further scaffolding. The framework assumes a level of metacognitive development that students at Level 1 of the AI maturity progression may not yet have achieved, and may require more structured support at this stage than the framework in its current form provides.

The human-role taxonomy, like the AI-role taxonomy, was developed through inductive analysis of research practice rather than empirical study of student writing processes. Students may find certain human roles difficult to distinguish from one another (e.g., Decision Maker vs. Evidence Interpreter) or may struggle to recognise when they are exercising a role implicitly rather than explicitly. The framework may require additional scaffolding --- worked examples, peer review protocols, or structured reflection prompts --- to help students at Level 1 of the AI maturity progression develop the metacognitive awareness needed to name their own contributions accurately.

Furthermore, the two-column disclosure structure, while designed to prevent compliance-only transparency, may create a new administrative burden if students treat it as a box-ticking exercise at a more granular level. The pedagogical value of the structure depends on whether it genuinely prompts reflection or merely produces longer, more detailed compliance statements. This risk requires empirical monitoring.

---

### 7.3 Future Directions

The most immediate next step is empirical evaluation of the framework in practice. This requires collecting student disclosure statements across the three stages of the programme and analysing them for specificity, accuracy, and development over time. It also requires comparison between students who have been introduced to the DDL vocabulary (including the human roles) and those working under a generic AI policy, to assess whether the dual vocabulary produces meaningfully different disclosure practices and, critically, whether it prevents the entirely-AI-generated submissions with superficial compliance that the author observed.

Longitudinal tracking of student AI maturity self-assessments, connected to performance on the grading framework, would provide evidence about the relationship between AI maturity development and writing quality --- a relationship that the framework predicts but that requires empirical confirmation. Anders and Dux Speltz (2026) suggest that threshold concept crossings can be observed in student reflections; applying their analytical framework to student self-assessments in the DHBW context would allow direct comparison between their findings and the outcomes of the DDL pedagogical implementation, including the fourth threshold concept of symmetrical disclosure.

Extension of the framework to other engineering education contexts --- first-year composition, professional and technical communication, writing across the disciplines --- would test its generalisability and identify where adaptation is needed. The call by Anders and Dux Speltz (2026) for ground-up investigations that allow threshold concepts to emerge from situated practice in different communities of practice applies equally to the DDL pedagogical framework: implementation in other contexts will reveal aspects of the framework that need revision or extension.

A specific priority for future research is investigating how students at different maturity levels respond to the human-role requirement. Do Level 1 students find it intrusive and difficult, or does it prompt the metacognitive development that moves them to Level 2? Do Level 3 and 4 students find it a natural extension of their existing reflective practice, or does it feel redundant? Answering these questions will determine whether the human-role taxonomy requires refinement, simplification, or additional scaffolding tools.

## 8. Conclusion

Engineering students who graduate without a framework for using AI productively, disclosing it honestly, and reflecting on their own contribution are not prepared for a professional context in which AI is standard practice. Generic AI policies, which define what is permitted and require some form of disclosure without providing a vocabulary for meaningful description, do not bridge this gap. Worse, they create a loophole: students can comply with disclosure requirements while delegating all intellectual labour to AI, producing work that satisfies administrative requirements but fails to develop the engineering judgement that education is designed to cultivate.

The Decision-Dialogue Loop offers a pedagogical framework that addresses the transparency problem, the vocabulary problem, and the metacognitive development problem simultaneously --- and, with the addition of explicit human functional roles, it now addresses the symmetrical disclosure problem as well. By giving students a shared language for both sides of the collaboration --- the eight AI roles and the eight human roles, the four-step loop --- the framework makes meaningful disclosure possible and makes compliance-only disclosure structurally visible. By connecting that language to a developmental progression across three years and three escalating writing tasks, it situates AI literacy within the broader development of engineering judgement that the programme is designed to support. By embedding the framework in a metacognitive workbook that students maintain across the full programme, it transforms AI literacy from a compliance requirement into a trackable developmental competency.

Independent empirical research on threshold concepts for AI-assisted writing (Anders and Dux Speltz, 2026) provides theoretical grounding for the framework's core operating principles, confirming that the conceptual transformations the DDL embeds structurally --- towards iterative experimentation, situated expertise, augmented rhetorical agency, and symmetrical transparency --- are precisely the threshold crossings that students need to make in order to move from dependent to strategic and ultimately to expert AI use.

The framework is proposed here as a theoretically grounded contribution to the design of AI literacy education in engineering programmes. Its empirical evaluation is the necessary next step, and one that the pedagogical infrastructure described in this paper --- the longitudinal writing programme, the shared evaluation framework, and the student self-reflection workbook --- is well positioned to support.
