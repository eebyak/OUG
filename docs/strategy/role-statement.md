## Our Designed Position in the European Higher Education Ecosystem

> **What a Role Statement is — and how it differs from a mission statement.** A mission statement describes what an institution intends to do. A Role Statement describes what an institution does that other institutions do not — and commits to being assessed against that description. The mission statement is internal. The Role Statement is positioned: it can only be written by naming the ecosystem it sits within and specifying what the institution's role is within that ecosystem that no other currently fills.
>
> This document is reviewed every three years alongside the OUG's strategic plan. If the review finds that the role the OUG was designed to fill is now being filled by other institutions, the Role Statement must be revised — not to claim a different role but to describe the actual role the institution is filling. A Role Statement that is no longer accurate is not an embarrassment. It is evidence that the ecosystem has changed — and that the OUG's strategic plan must respond.

## I. The Role in One Sentence

*For external communications at all levels.*

> The OUG is the public university for the adult learner that the European higher education system was not designed for — and has not redesigned itself to serve.

---

## II. What This Role Means — In Detail

### Who we serve that others do not

The OUG's target learner population is specific, not generic. It consists of adults who need higher education but cannot access it on the terms the existing system offers: those who are employed and cannot commit to the lecture timetables of a full-time programme; those with caregiving responsibilities that create unpredictable time constraints; those in regions without a higher education institution within daily commuting distance; those whose financial situation excludes the opportunity cost of three years without income even where tuition is nominally free; and those with professional experience and prior learning that the existing system's entry requirements do not recognise.

This population is not a marginal group. According to Eurostat data (2023), approximately 37% of adults aged 25–54 in the EU hold no higher education qualification but express interest in further study. The primary barriers they name — time format, cost, geography, entry requirements — are precisely the barriers the OUG's design is built to remove. The OUG does not serve this population as an accommodation. It was designed from the beginning with this population as its primary subject.

### What we offer that others do not

The OUG offers a degree-awarding programme that is fully online, asynchronous-first, modular, and priced at €25 per ECTS — one sixth of the private market rate for comparable online provision, and accessible to learners who cannot afford to stop earning while they study. Every completed module is certified by a Europass Digital Credential, so that partial completion has portable, verifiable value from the first module. Prior learning recognition is a primary entry pathway, not an exceptional case requiring individual negotiation.

These are not adaptations of a traditional university model. They are structural features designed for a learner whose situation the traditional model cannot accommodate. The distinction matters for the ecosystem argument: the OUG is not competing with traditional universities for the same learners. It is reaching learners who would not otherwise enter higher education — extending the ecosystem's reach rather than contesting existing market share.

### What we deliberately do not do

The OUG does not offer campus-based education. It does not offer a research-intensive doctoral programme as its primary offering. It does not seek to be ranked in systems designed for research-intensive institutions. It does not pursue the research metrics that define prestige in the traditional higher education system — not because it lacks ambition but because the pressure to become what the prestigious institutions already are, at the cost of being what the ecosystem actually needs, is a real structural risk that the OUG's design explicitly guards against.

This does not mean the OUG has no research agenda. Academic contributors bring their research expertise to the design and teaching of the curriculum. Applied research that serves the OUG's learner population and the societal challenges its Mission Hubs address is valued and supported. What the OUG does not do is allow research metrics to determine the institution's governance priorities or its definition of quality.

## III. Our Position in the European HE Ecosystem

The European higher education ecosystem contains several institution types that are well served by existing design. The OUG is designed to fill a role that none of them currently fills: the degree-awarding public institution for the adult learner who cannot access any of the above on the terms they currently offer.

The institution that requires the most careful positioning is the **FernUniversität in Hagen** — Germany's only existing state-funded distance university, founded in 1974 with an access mission that in several respects resembles the OUG's. With 78,000 students, an average learner age of 37.9, and 80% of its students in employment, Hagen has demonstrated over five decades that large-scale public distance education is possible in Germany. That demonstration matters and is not in question here.

What is in question is whether Hagen's model serves the learner the OUG is designed for — and whether the two institutions are, at the governance architecture level, the same kind of thing. The answer to both questions is no, for reasons that go deeper than credential format or price point.

The surface differences are visible in the table below. But the deeper difference is architectural. Hagen is a single institution governed by five traditional faculties, each holding curriculum authority over its own programmes. Curriculum decisions at Hagen flow through faculty committees — the same bodies that also govern research, appoint staff, and set quality goals. There is no constitutional separation between financial and academic governance. Teaching roles bundle curriculum design, content creation, and delivery in the same professor who also holds a Lehrstuhl. Quality assurance is accreditation-driven, assessed against input norms by external agencies.

The OUG's governance architecture differs from Hagen's on every one of these dimensions. Curriculum authority rests with a central Design Authority. The governance layers that hold financial oversight and academic quality authority are constitutionally separated. The teaching, curriculum design, and content architecture roles are explicitly unbundled. Quality accountability includes a peer-based Developmental Audit against self-defined purpose. The federated model means that teaching capacity scales through partners while quality standards are held centrally.

These are not incremental differences. They represent a different institutional logic. A learner who cannot be served by Hagen is not served by an institution that shares Hagen's governance architecture. The OUG does not share it.

## IV. The State's Obligation — and the Evidence That It Has Not Been Met

The founding of the OUG is not primarily a market intervention. It is a constitutional one.

Germany's Basic Law establishes education as a public good and places the provision of higher education within the state's sphere of obligation. The Federal Constitutional Court's *Numerus Clausus* ruling (BVerfGE 33, 303 — 1972) established that where the state creates a system of higher education it cannot then structure access to it in ways that systematically exclude citizens. The broader doctrine of *Daseinsvorsorge* — the state's duty to ensure that essential services are available to all citizens on terms they can actually access — applies to higher education as much as to water, energy, or public transport. A higher education system that is in principle free but in practice accessible only to those who can commit to fixed formats, fixed schedules, and multi-year financial planning does not discharge that duty for the population it excludes.

The existence and scale of the IU Internationale Hochschule is the most precise empirical evidence available that this duty is currently unmet.

**140,000+ students at IU in 2026** — Germany's largest university by enrolment (IU Group annual report 2025–26). IU is not a niche provider. It is larger than any traditional German university. Its growth from a small institution in 2000 to the country's largest in 2026 is not explained by marketing. It is explained by the absence of a public alternative for the learners it serves.

**~€80–120 per ECTS at IU** — the private market rate for online higher education (IU published tuition schedules 2025). A full bachelor's degree at IU costs €8,000–12,000+. This is the price the market charges for the education the state has not provided. Learners who can afford it pay it. Learners who cannot are excluded from higher education entirely — not by law, but by the absence of a public institution designed for their situation.

**78,000 students at FernUniversität Hagen** — Germany's only public distance university (FernUniversität Hagen 2025 figures). Hagen's enrolment is significant. IU's is nearly double it. The gap between these numbers is not explained by quality differences. It is explained by architecture: IU offers per-module flexibility, payment structures that match working adult incomes, and credential portability that Hagen's degree-centric model does not provide.

### The constitutional argument in plain terms

When a private company becomes the dominant provider of a service the state is obligated to provide — and does so at prices that systematically exclude lower-income citizens — the state has not merely missed a market opportunity. It has failed a constitutional duty. That is the situation in German online higher education in 2026. IU has built a €1 billion business serving learners the public system was not designed to reach. Those learners are not choosing IU over Hagen. Many of them are choosing IU over nothing, because Hagen's architecture — committee-designed curricula, degree-only credentials, fixed semester structures, tuition that accumulates over a full programme — cannot accommodate their lives.

The OUG is the state's institutional response to that failure. Not a response to IU as a competitor — IU's existence is the evidence, not the target. The response is to the learner who is currently paying a private company for what the state is constitutionally obligated to provide, or who has given up on higher education entirely because no public institution was designed for their situation.

### What the OUG does that changes the constitutional picture

Three features of the OUG's design are constitutionally significant — not merely pedagogically desirable.

The **€25/ECTS Kostenbeitrag** is constituted as a public contribution to a non-profit educational service, not as a market price. It is the structural expression of the Allgemeinwohl mandate: the state provides the education, the learner contributes to the cost at a rate that does not exclude them. The legal basis is the Allgemeinwohlverfassung and the *Betrauungsakt* by the *Landesministerium* — the formal act by which the state recognises the OUG as discharging a public duty rather than conducting a commercial activity. This matters legally: it is the mechanism by which €25/ECTS is protected against Wettbewerbsrecht challenge.

The **per-module Europass credential** is constitutionally significant because it removes the structural barrier that a degree-or-nothing architecture creates. A learner who completes thirty ECTS and must stop — because of a health crisis, a caregiving obligation, a change in employment — holds a verified, portable, ESCO-mapped record of what they achieved. The state has not abandoned them at the moment their circumstances changed. That is *Daseinsvorsorge* in practice.

The **prior learning recognition pathway** as a primary entry route — not an exception requiring individual petition — is constitutionally significant because it removes the structural exclusion that formal entry requirements create for learners whose knowledge was acquired outside traditional channels. Millions of working professionals hold competencies that the existing system refuses to recognise because it was not designed to look for them. The OUG's competency codebook and its Bloom-level assessment architecture are designed to see what the traditional system cannot.

> The OUG does not exist to compete with IU. It exists because IU's 140,000 learners are evidence that the state has not yet built what it is constitutionally obligated to provide. The OUG is that provision.

## V. How This Role Statement Is Used

This document has four governance uses.

**First**, it is the primary benchmark for institutional reviews — including the OUG's own Developmental Audit. The first question in every review is: is the institution still filling the role it said it would fill, for the learners it said it would serve?

**Second**, it is the external positioning statement for partnership conversations, ministry engagements, and accreditation submissions — the document that explains what the OUG is by naming what it is not.

**Third**, it is the reference point for programme design decisions — every new programme must be assessed against the Role Statement before it is approved.

**Fourth**, it is the document that anchors the Communication Architecture: the 30-second, 2-minute, and 5-minute expressions of the OUG's vision are derived from this statement, not from a marketing brief.

This Role Statement is reviewed every three years alongside the strategic plan. The review is conducted by the Hochschulrat with input from the Senate and the Partnership Office. The question the review asks is not "has the institution changed?" but "has the ecosystem changed, and if so, does this statement still describe the role the institution is uniquely positioned to fill?"

> We are not trying to become what the existing system already is. We are trying to be what the existing system is not — and to hold ourselves to that distinction every time the pressure to converge on the familiar makes itself felt.
