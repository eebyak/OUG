## How the OUG Builds Strategy With Its Community — Not For It

> This Protocol governs the process by which the Open University of Germany develops, validates, and adopts institutional strategy. It specifies who participates, in what sequence, through what mechanisms, and to what standard of feasibility. It is a formal governance document: compliance with this Protocol is a condition of strategic legitimacy. A strategy produced outside this process does not carry the institutional authority of a properly formed strategy. This Protocol is addressed to the G1 Constitutional Stewardship layer, the G2 Strategic Steering layer, and the Academic Senate, whose coordinated engagement is required throughout.

---

## I. Purpose and Governance Status

The OUG's governance architecture defines how strategy is implemented, approved, and changed. It does not, by itself, define how strategy is created. This Protocol fills that gap. It establishes the participatory process through which institutional strategy emerges from the knowledge and experience of the OUG's academic community — students, academic contributors, partner institution representatives, and staff — and is consolidated into a form that can be formally adopted by the G1 and G2 governance layers.

The rationale for a formal participatory process is not procedural courtesy. It is institutional effectiveness. Strategy that is delivered to those who must implement it encounters resistance, disengagement, and misalignment that no subsequent communication effort can fully correct. Strategy that is built with those who must implement it generates ownership — people understand how their work contributes to institutional goals because they were part of forming those goals. This is the governance investment that makes implementation quality possible.

> A strategy that the community did not help build is a strategy the community will not own. Ownership is not a cultural aspiration — it is a governance outcome, produced by a specific process. This Protocol specifies that process.

This Protocol is a formal governance document at the G1 level. It may only be amended through the constitutional amendment procedure defined in the Grundordnung. The process it describes must be followed for all major strategy cycles. Minor strategic adjustments within an existing strategy period follow the Portfolio Governance Framework's change classification procedure and do not require activation of this Protocol.

## II. Scope, Activation, and Cycle

### When This Protocol Applies

This Protocol governs the formation of major institutional strategies — the multi-year frameworks that define the OUG's guiding principles, strategic priorities, and measurable objectives. It applies in three situations: **founding strategy** (the first strategy adopted by the OUG prior to or immediately following the first student cohort); **strategic renewal** (the formation of a new strategy at the end of a strategy period, typically four to six years); and **major strategic revision** (a revision of the current strategy that changes one or more guiding principles or adds or removes a strategic priority area).

### Activation

A strategy formation cycle is activated by a decision of the G1 Constitutional Stewardship layer, on recommendation from the Academic Senate. The activation decision must specify: the scope of the strategy to be formed (full renewal or targeted revision), the anticipated timeline, the resources allocated to the process, and the name of the Strategy Coordinator responsible for facilitating the cycle.

The Strategy Coordinator role is a dedicated facilitation appointment. It may be held by a senior academic staff member, the Rector, or an external facilitator with relevant experience. The Coordinator is accountable to the Academic Senate for the integrity of the process and to the G2 layer for the timeline.

### Standard Timeline

| Phase | Indicative Duration |
|---|---|
| Phase 0 — Preparation | 2–3 months |
| Phase 1 — Permission and Mandate | 1 month |
| Phase 2 — Environmental Scan | 2–3 months |
| Phase 3 — Harvesting | 3–4 months |
| Phase 4 — Consolidation | 2 months |
| Phase 5 — Iterative Validation | 2–3 months |
| Phase 6 — Feasibility Testing | 1–2 months |
| Phase 7 — Adoption | 1 month |
| **Total** | **14–19 months (typically 18)** |

A full strategy formation cycle takes up to eighteen months. This is not excessive — it reflects the investment required to build genuine ownership across a federated, distributed institution. Institutions that compress this timeline invariably produce strategies that are formally adopted and operationally ignored.

## III. The Seven-Phase Process

### Phase 0 — Preparation
*Who: Strategy Coordinator, G2 layer · Duration: 2–3 months*

Map all groups to be engaged: academic contributors by discipline and partner institution, student representatives by programme, staff by function, Senate members, Mission Hub leads, partner institution representatives, and industrial governance members.

Design the harvesting process: formats, venues (in-person strongly preferred for primary harvesting), facilitation approach, accessibility provisions for distributed participants.

Establish the communication plan: how participants are informed, what they are asked to prepare, and how findings will be shared back with them.

Appoint a small Strategy Working Group (5–7 people, drawn from across governance layers) to support the Coordinator throughout the process. This group does not make strategic decisions; it ensures process quality.

Prepare an Environmental Scan brief summarising the external context: sector trends, learner demand data, policy environment, competitor landscape, EU and national education policy developments.

### Phase 1 — Permission and Mandate
*Who: G1 layer, Rector, Academic Senate · Duration: 1 month*

The G1 layer formally authorises the participatory strategy formation process. This authorisation is the governance act that makes participation legitimate rather than merely consultative.

The Rector communicates the activation of the strategy cycle to all governance bodies, academic contributors, students, and partner institution representatives. The communication explains: why strategy is being renewed, what the process will involve, that contributions will be taken seriously, and what the timeline is.

The Academic Senate adopts a resolution confirming its role in the process: receiving the consolidated findings, validating the draft guiding principles, and approving the final strategy for submission to G2.

This phase is not administrative formality. The quality of leadership's engagement in communicating the mandate directly affects the quality of participation in subsequent phases.

### Phase 2 — Environmental Scan
*Who: Strategy Coordinator, Strategy Working Group · Duration: 2–3 months*

Compile and present the Environmental Scan: external trends, learner needs research, sector benchmarking (including international models, particularly from Nordic countries and open university systems), and policy landscape analysis.

Present the scan in accessible form to all governance bodies and to the broader academic community. The scan provides shared factual grounding for the harvesting conversations — it prevents discussion from becoming purely internal and preference-based.

Identify two to four strategic questions that the scan suggests are most pressing for the OUG. These are open questions, not predetermined answers. They frame the harvesting conversations without determining their outcomes.

Submit the Environmental Scan and strategic questions to the Academic Senate for endorsement before harvesting begins.

### Phase 3 — Harvesting
*Who: Strategy Coordinator, facilitated groups · Duration: 3–4 months*

Conduct structured in-person harvesting sessions with all identified groups. In-person engagement is strongly preferred for primary harvesting; online participation is available for those who cannot attend in person but is not the primary mode. In-person dialogue produces qualitatively different — richer, more honest, more contextually grounded — contributions than online surveys.

Each harvesting session addresses the same core questions: What is the OUG doing well that must be protected? What is not working and needs to change? What opportunities are we not yet pursuing? What challenges keep you from contributing fully to the OUG's mission? Groups are encouraged to be direct; facilitators ensure that critical contributions receive the same weight as affirmative ones.

Harvesting is conducted separately by group type (academic contributors, students, staff, partner representatives, industrial governance members) to allow different voices to speak freely. Findings are then compared across groups to identify convergences and divergences.

All contributions are recorded, anonymised where appropriate, and fed into the consolidation phase. No contribution is dismissed without documented justification.

A brief written summary of findings from each session is shared back with participants within two weeks of each session. This closes the first feedback loop and confirms that contributions have been heard.

### Phase 4 — Consolidation
*Who: Strategy Coordinator, Strategy Working Group · Duration: 2 months*

The Strategy Working Group analyses all harvesting findings, identifies themes, and drafts a small number (three to six) of candidate guiding principles that capture the dominant patterns across groups.

Candidate principles are tested against three criteria: Does this reflect what the majority of contributors said? Does this align with what the Environmental Scan identified as urgent? Is this achievable within the OUG's capacity and resources?

The consolidation produces a Candidate Strategy Document containing: the candidate guiding principles with their derivation documented, a preliminary set of priority areas (more than the final number — these will be reduced in Phase 6), and a narrative connecting the principles to the Environmental Scan and the harvesting findings.

The Candidate Strategy Document is submitted to the Academic Senate for review before iterative validation begins.

### Phase 5 — Iterative Validation
*Who: Strategy Coordinator, same groups as Phase 3 · Duration: 2–3 months*

The Candidate Strategy Document is returned to the same groups who participated in harvesting. The question is explicit: Does this accurately reflect what you told us? What is missing? What is overstated?

Iterative validation is conducted in the same in-person format as harvesting, with the same groups. This is not a second round of open-ended input — it is a structured review of whether the consolidation faithfully captured the contributions.

Groups may propose amendments to candidate principles, but amendments must be justified by reference to harvesting contributions. The Strategy Working Group documents all proposed amendments and its response to each.

After two rounds of iterative validation (or one round if consensus is strong), the Coordinator produces a Validated Strategy Document and submits it to the Academic Senate.

### Phase 6 — Feasibility Testing
*Who: Academic Senate, G2 layer, Strategy Working Group · Duration: 1–2 months*

The Validated Strategy Document typically contains more priority areas and proposed goals than the institution can realistically pursue. This phase applies disciplined reduction: goals are tested against implementation capacity, available resources, and measurability.

The reduction target: no more than five guiding principles and no more than fifteen to twenty measurable goals. This is a governance discipline, not an arbitrary number. A strategy with thirty goals is not a strategy — it is a wish list. Every goal retained must be defensible on three grounds: it is strategically significant, it is measurable, and someone in the institution is specifically responsible for it.

The Academic Senate reviews the reduced goal set for academic coherence and quality. The G2 layer reviews it for financial and operational feasibility. Both must endorse the Validated and Feasibility-Tested Strategy Document before it proceeds to adoption.

Goals that do not survive feasibility testing are not discarded — they are documented as "deferred" with the conditions under which they could be revisited. This respects the contributions from which they originated.

### Phase 7 — Adoption
*Who: G1 layer, G2 layer, Academic Senate · Duration: 1 month*

The Academic Senate formally approves the strategy document for submission to the G2 Strategic Steering layer.

The G2 layer reviews the strategy for compatibility with the OUG's constitutional principles and financial sustainability, and formally adopts it as the institutional strategy for the period.

The G1 Constitutional Stewardship layer validates that the adopted strategy is consistent with the OUG's founding mission and constitutional commitments.

The adopted strategy is published in full and communicated to all members of the OUG academic community, with a clear explanation of how the final document connects to the contributions made during harvesting and validation.

The communication at adoption closes the participation loop: contributors should be able to see their contributions reflected in the final strategy, or understand why they were not included.

## IV. Requirements and Safeguards

### In-Person Engagement Requirement

Primary harvesting and iterative validation sessions must be conducted in person wherever possible. The OUG acknowledges that its distributed, digital nature makes in-person meetings logistically demanding. This requirement exists precisely because of that demand: the quality of contribution that in-person dialogue produces cannot be replicated by asynchronous digital formats. Online participation is an accessibility provision, not the default mode.

Minimum in-person participation threshold: at least 60% of participants in any given group type must have the opportunity to attend an in-person session. Where this threshold cannot be met, the Coordinator must document why and obtain Academic Senate approval to proceed with a modified format.

### Documentation and Traceability

Every contribution made during harvesting and validation must be traceable to a group session. The Strategy Formation Record — a document maintained by the Strategy Coordinator throughout the cycle — must contain: a log of all sessions held, the groups involved, and attendance; a summary of contributions from each session; documentation of how each contribution was addressed in consolidation; a record of all proposed amendments during validation and the Working Group's response to each; and justification for each goal that was deferred at feasibility testing.

The Strategy Formation Record is submitted to the Academic Senate at adoption and retained as an institutional record for the duration of the strategy period.

### Protection Against Predetermination

The Strategy Formation Protocol exists to ensure that strategy emerges from genuine engagement rather than ratifying decisions already made. The following practices are prohibited: presenting participants with a draft strategy and asking for endorsement; using harvesting sessions to communicate a preferred direction; weighting contributions from senior staff more heavily than contributions from students or junior academic contributors; and conducting harvesting exclusively online when in-person sessions are feasible.

If the Academic Senate or the Strategy Coordinator determines that the process has been compromised at any stage, the affected phase must be repeated. A strategy produced by a compromised process does not receive Academic Senate endorsement.

### Governance of the Protocol Itself

This Protocol is reviewed at the conclusion of each strategy cycle as part of the institutional quality review. Proposed amendments are submitted to the Academic Senate and require G1 approval. Experience from each cycle — what worked, what was difficult, what should change — is documented in the Strategy Formation Record and informs the next cycle's preparation.

> Strategy formation is not an event. It is a governance process — one that takes time, requires genuine engagement, and produces an outcome that belongs to the institution rather than to its leadership. The quality of implementation depends on the quality of this process. There is no shortcut that preserves the outcome.
