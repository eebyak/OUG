## 0. Purpose, Status, and Time Horizon

### 0.1 Purpose

This Strategy Framework translates the Strategy Constitution of the Open University of Germany (OUG) into a time-bound steering instrument for institutional development up to 2030. It defines a limited set of strategic objectives and associated strategic indicators and learning signals that enable the institution to prioritise, observe progress, surface unintended effects, and adapt course over time.

The framework is designed to steer what matters — academic quality, access, learner development, coherence, and institutional learning — while preserving academic autonomy, participatory governance, and evidence-based judgement as defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md) and the [Quality Assurance & Academic Governance Concept](../quality/quality-assurance-academic-governance.md).

### 0.2 Status as SEP-Equivalent

In line with common practice in Baden-Württemberg, this Strategy Framework fulfils the function of a *Struktur- und Entwicklungsplan* (SEP). It specifies strategic direction and observability for a medium-term horizon without prescribing detailed measures, projects, or budgets. Funding-dependent initiatives and operational measures are developed iteratively and documented separately, ensuring that strategy precedes — and is not retrofitted to — financing instruments.

This separation preserves institutional agency, supports accreditation traceability, and allows strategic learning independent of short-term funding cycles.

### 0.3 Constitutional Anchoring

All objectives and indicators in this framework are derived from and constrained by the Strategy Constitution. In particular, the framework respects the constitutional precedence of: academic quality and integrity over short-term optimisation; accessibility without dilution of standards; participatory governance with clear accountability; and evidence-informed learning and adaptability.

Where trade-offs arise, interpretation and adjustment follow the constitutional decision logic and escalation pathways defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md).

### 0.4 Time Horizon and Review Logic

The framework adopts a 2030 planning horizon, consistent with sector-wide planning and financing horizons, while remaining institution-specific and adaptive.

- **Horizon:** through 2030
- **Form:** rolling framework
- **Review:** annual strategic review; mid-cycle adjustments if required
- **Renewal:** comprehensive renewal at cycle end

Strategic objectives are stable across the cycle; indicators and learning signals may be refined to improve observability and avoid perverse incentives.

### 0.5 Measurability and Learning Orientation

Measurability in this framework serves learning and steering, not control. Indicators are selected to make progress visible across diverse learning pathways and institutional functions, including quality at scale in modular, stackable provision; learner progression and agency development; coherence of skill profiles and qualification equivalence; and institutional learning and governance effectiveness.

No single indicator determines success. Interpretation remains a governed process involving Strategic Steering (G2) and Academic Governance (G3), with participatory sense-making inputs as defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md).

## 1. Strategic Objectives (2030 Horizon)

The strategic objectives defined below translate the Strategy Constitution into a limited set of institution-wide priorities for the period up to 2030. They are designed to be stable across disciplines and delivery formats, applicable to modular and non-linear learning pathways, and steerable through clearly defined strategic indicators and learning signals (Section 2).

Each objective is constitutionally anchored, mutually reinforcing, and subject to periodic review under the governance logic defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md).

### SO1 — Academic Quality and Integrity at Scale

The OUG will ensure that expansion in access, modularity, and stackability strengthens rather than dilutes academic quality. Academic standards, assessment integrity, and curriculum coherence remain non-negotiable, irrespective of learning pathways, delivery formats, or learner status.

By 2030, the institution will demonstrably: maintain equivalent academic outcomes across diverse learning trajectories; safeguard assessment independence and validity; and ensure coherence across modules, skill profiles, and qualifications.

This objective operationalises the constitutional precedence of academic quality.

### SO2 — Learner Agency and Progressive Competence Development

The OUG will enable learners from diverse backgrounds to develop increasing agency, metacognitive capability, and academic autonomy over time. Learner development is understood as a progressive trajectory supported by transparent prerequisites, feedforward-oriented evaluation, and reflective practice.

By 2030, the institution will demonstrably: support progression from guided to self-directed learning; embed metacognitive competence as an academically governed outcome; and enable informed pathway choices without compromising standards.

This objective aligns learner development with long-term academic success and societal participation.

### SO3 — Accessibility and Inclusion Across Life Situations

The OUG will expand meaningful access to academic learning across regions, ages, and life circumstances, while maintaining transparent entry conditions and progression rules. Accessibility is achieved through flexible architectures — not through lowered expectations.

By 2030, the institution will demonstrably: reduce structural barriers to participation; support diverse entry points into skill profiles and qualifications; and ensure fairness and transparency in prerequisite and recognition processes.

This objective realises the constitutional commitment to accessibility without dilution.

### SO4 — Coherent Skill Profiles and Qualification Equivalence

The OUG will ensure that modular learning units combine into academically coherent skill profiles that map reliably into recognised qualifications. Degrees are awarded based on validated competence aggregation, not on fixed curricula.

By 2030, the institution will demonstrably: maintain clear architectural rules for skill aggregation; ensure equivalence of qualification outcomes across pathways; and enable interdisciplinary combinations without loss of depth.

This objective secures trust in qualifications within a flexible learning model.

### SO5 — Institutional Learning and Adaptability

The OUG will operate as a learning institution, continuously improving governance, curriculum architecture, and academic practice through evidence-informed reflection.

By 2030, the institution will demonstrably: use strategic indicators to surface unintended effects; adapt structures and sequencing based on evidence; and document learning from success and failure.

This objective embeds adaptability as a core institutional capability.

### SO6 — Societal and Professional Relevance Without Capture

The OUG will remain responsive to societal and professional change while preserving academic judgement and independence. External frameworks and skills taxonomies (e.g. ESCO) are used to support transparency and interoperability, not to dictate content.

By 2030, the institution will demonstrably: align learning outcomes with evolving societal contexts; integrate external perspectives through governed participation; and prevent short-term demand from overriding academic standards.

This objective balances relevance with autonomy.

### Status of Strategic Objectives

Together, these objectives define the strategic development corridor of the OUG to 2030. They are intentionally non-operational and are realised through governed decision-making, quality assurance, and participatory processes as defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md) and the [Quality Assurance & Academic Governance Concept](../quality/quality-assurance-academic-governance.md). Progress toward these objectives is observed and interpreted through strategic indicators and learning signals (Section 2) at the institutional level, and through programme-level Objectives and Key Results at the level of an individual accredited programme (see § 5 and the [OKR Framework](okr-framework-dashboard.md) § II.4).

## 2. Strategic Indicators and Learning Signals

Strategic indicators and learning signals translate the strategic objectives into observable patterns of development up to 2030. Their purpose is to support reflection, prioritisation, and adaptive steering, not to function as targets, rankings, or automatic decision triggers.

Indicators are selected to be meaningful across disciplines and learning pathways, compatible with modular and lifelong learning, and interpretable through academic and strategic governance as defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md).

### 2.1 Principles for Indicator Design and Use

All strategic indicators and learning signals adhere to the following principles:

**Plurality.** No single indicator is sufficient to represent progress toward an objective.

**Interpretability.** Indicators require contextual interpretation by governance bodies.

**Non-instrumentalisation.** Indicators do not replace academic judgement and are not used for individual performance evaluation.

**Developmental orientation.** Indicators surface trends, tensions, and unintended effects over time.

**Stability with adaptability.** Indicator families are stable; specific indicators may be refined during the cycle to improve observability.

### 2.2 Indicator Families by Strategic Objective

The following indicator families define what is observed for each strategic objective. Specific indicators are selected and refined during implementation and review cycles.

**SO1 — Academic Quality and Integrity at Scale.** Indicator families may include: equivalence of assessment outcomes across learning pathways; consistency of academic level across modules and skill profiles; evidence of assessment validity and independence; frequency and nature of quality-related escalations or interventions; peer review signals regarding curriculum coherence. These indicators make visible whether expansion and modularity preserve academic standards.

**SO2 — Learner Agency and Progressive Competence Development.** Indicator families may include: progression in learners' metacognitive engagement and self-regulation; alignment between prerequisite definitions and learner readiness; evidence from feedforward-oriented evaluations; patterns of learner pathway choices over time; completion and re-engagement rates across learning phases. Indicators in this family focus on developmental trajectories, not static attainment.

**SO3 — Accessibility and Inclusion Across Life Situations.** Indicator families may include: participation patterns across regions, ages, and life situations; diversity of entry points into skill profiles and qualifications; transparency and fairness of prerequisite and recognition processes; persistence and progression across non-linear pathways. These indicators observe access as *meaningful participation*, not raw enrolment.

**SO4 — Coherent Skill Profiles and Qualification Equivalence.** Indicator families may include: coherence of skill aggregation across pathways; stability of qualification-level competence coverage; interdisciplinarity patterns and boundary management; recognition and portability of credentials. This family ensures that flexibility results in equivalent, trusted qualifications.

**SO5 — Institutional Learning and Adaptability.** Indicator families may include: documented adjustments to curriculum architecture or sequencing; governance decisions informed by evidence; frequency and outcomes of strategic reviews; learning statements derived from unmet objectives. These indicators make institutional learning explicit and traceable.

**SO6 — Societal and Professional Relevance Without Capture.** Indicator families may include: alignment of learning outcomes with evolving external frameworks; structured external input into academic governance; balance between stability of academic standards and responsiveness; evidence of resisted short-term optimisation pressures. This family observes relevance while protecting autonomy.

### 2.3 Interpretation and Governance of Indicators

Strategic indicators and learning signals are interpreted through governed processes: **Strategic Steering (G2)** reviews overall trends and trade-offs. **Academic Governance (G3)** validates interpretations affecting quality and standards. **Participatory bodies** contribute sense-making and contextual insight.

Indicators may prompt strategic reflection, prioritisation discussions, or requests for deeper analysis — but do not trigger automatic actions.

### 2.4 Review, Refinement, and Transparency

Indicators are reviewed annually for relevance, unintended effects, and interpretability. Refinements are documented to preserve continuity and learning. Summary interpretations and strategic learning outcomes are made transparent within the institution.

### Status of Strategic Indicators

The indicator framework defined in this section enables the OUG to observe progress toward its 2030 objectives without compromising academic freedom, quality, or participation. It operationalises the Strategy Constitution as a learning-oriented steering instrument rather than a control mechanism.

## 3. Review Cycles and Governance of the Strategy Framework (SEP)

The Strategy Framework is governed through clearly defined review cycles and decision responsibilities that ensure coherent steering, transparency, and institutional learning. Strategic review is treated as a governed process, not as a managerial routine, and is explicitly separated from operational control.

### 3.1 Review Rhythm and Cycle

The Strategy Framework operates on a rolling review cycle aligned with the 2030 planning horizon.

**Annual Strategic Review.** A comprehensive review of strategic indicators and learning signals is conducted once per year. The review focuses on trends, trade-offs, and unintended effects rather than target fulfilment.

**Mid-Cycle Adjustment (if required).** Where significant internal or external changes occur, targeted adjustments to indicators or priorities may be proposed, subject to governance validation.

**End-of-Cycle Renewal.** At the end of the planning cycle, the Strategy Framework is renewed. Renewal explicitly includes reflection on unmet objectives and documented learning outcomes.

This rhythm balances strategic stability with adaptability.

### 3.2 Governance Responsibilities

Strategic review and adjustment are governed through the decision architecture defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md).

**Strategic Steering (G2)** holds responsibility for: reviewing strategic indicators and learning signals; identifying strategic tensions and priority shifts; proposing adjustments to objectives or indicators within constitutional boundaries.

**Academic Governance (G3)** validates interpretations and proposals that affect academic quality, assessment integrity, curriculum architecture, and learner development principles. G3 ensures that strategic steering does not override academic judgement or quality safeguards.

**Participatory Bodies** provide structured agenda input and sense-making contributions, particularly regarding learner experience, access, and developmental effects. Participation informs interpretation but does not replace decision authority.

### 3.3 Decision Logic and Escalation

Strategic decisions derived from the review process follow the constitutional decision logic: trade-offs are made explicit; justifications are documented; and deviations from participatory recommendations require explanation.

Where disagreements arise regarding competence, constitutional alignment, or quality impact, escalation follows the pathways defined in the [Governance & Decision Framework](../governance/governance-architecture.md).

### 3.4 Outputs of the Strategic Review

Each annual strategic review produces: a **Strategic Reflection Statement**, summarising observed developments, tensions, and learning; documented decisions or non-decisions with rationale; and, where appropriate, proposals for adjustment.

These outputs are archived to ensure traceability for accreditation and institutional learning.

### 3.5 Transparency and Accountability

Strategic review outcomes are communicated internally in a transparent manner appropriate to their scope. Transparency serves institutional learning, trust in governance processes, and accountability toward academic and learner communities.

Accountability for strategic outcomes is collective and institutional; indicators are not translated into individual performance targets.

### Status of Strategic Review and Governance

The review cycles and governance arrangements defined in this section ensure that the Strategy Framework functions as a living SEP — capable of steering toward 2030 objectives while remaining adaptive, participatory, and constitutionally bound.

## 4. Relationship to State Context and External Frameworks

This Strategy Framework is developed in awareness of the state-level planning, regulatory, and financing context of Baden-Württemberg, as well as relevant national and European frameworks. The framework adopts a 2030 planning horizon, consistent with sector-wide funding and development cycles, while maintaining institutional autonomy over strategic direction and academic priorities.

External frameworks — such as qualification frameworks, skills taxonomies, and policy priorities — are used to support transparency, interoperability, and relevance. They inform strategic sense-making but do not determine academic content, curriculum architecture, or assessment standards. Alignment with external expectations is achieved through governed interpretation rather than direct adoption.

This approach enables responsiveness to the state and societal context without compromising the constitutional precedence of academic quality, participatory governance, and evidence-informed decision-making.

## 5. Relationship to Portfolio, Resources, and Measures

The Strategy Framework informs portfolio discussions and structural prioritisation at an institutional level. It provides orientation for decisions regarding capacity development, focus areas, and long-term investment directions.

The framework does **not**: define specific programmes or offerings; prescribe projects or measures; allocate budgets; or establish automatic funding formulas.

Operational measures, initiatives, and resource allocations are developed and decided through the appropriate governance processes and may be adapted in response to funding availability. This separation ensures that strategy guides action without being reduced to a project list, and that learning from implementation can feed back into strategic review.

**Where programme-level connection actually happens.** This Framework deliberately stops short of naming individual programmes, for the reason stated above — doing so here would make every new programme require an edit to this document. That connection is made instead by the [OKR Framework](okr-framework-dashboard.md) § II.4, which assigns every accredited programme an Objective derived from one or more Strategic Objectives above, once, at the point of Portfolio Governance approval. The rule generalises automatically; this Framework's own boundary against naming programmes is what makes that generalisation possible rather than something each new programme has to work around.

## 6. Safeguards Against Instrumentalisation

To prevent the misuse of strategic objectives and indicators, the following safeguards apply: strategic indicators are not translated into individual performance targets; no automatic linkage exists between indicators and budgetary sanctions or rewards; academic judgement retains precedence in interpreting evidence; trade-offs and prioritisation decisions are documented and justified; and indicators are reviewed for unintended effects and revised where necessary.

These safeguards ensure that the Strategy Framework supports learning, coherence, and accountability, rather than optimisation pressure or managerial overreach.

## 7. Evolution, Renewal, and Institutional Learning

The Strategy Framework is treated as a living document. Its primary function is to enable institutional learning over time.

At the end of each planning cycle: strategic objectives are reviewed for continued relevance; indicators and learning signals are evaluated for effectiveness; and documented learning — including unmet objectives — is used to inform renewal.

Strategic renewal is explicitly framed as a learning process, not as a corrective response to failure. This ensures that the institution remains adaptable while preserving continuity of purpose and standards.
