## From Monitoring to Transformation — How the OUG Measures What Matters and Learns From What It Finds

> This Framework establishes the OUG's approach to Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) as its primary instrument for transformation-oriented performance management. It complements — but does not replace — the quality monitoring mechanisms in the Quality Assurance and Academic Governance Framework. OKRs ask where the institution is going; quality monitoring asks whether it is functioning well. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient alone. This Framework also specifies the Governance Dashboard — the institutional infrastructure through which OKR progress is made visible, discussed, and acted upon.

## I. The Governance Rationale: Why OKRs

Traditional higher education performance management relies heavily on Key Performance Indicators: enrolment numbers, completion rates, student satisfaction scores, publication counts, financial ratios. These are monitoring instruments. They are retrospective — they tell the institution how it performed — and they are stabilising — they reward consistency and penalise deviation. A well-designed KPI system tells you whether the institution is healthy. It does not tell you whether it is moving in the right direction.

The OUG is a pre-launch institution operating in a rapidly changing sector. Its governance challenge is not primarily to maintain a stable steady state — it is to build something new, adapt continuously as it learns, and remain oriented toward a mission that sits at the frontier of how higher education is changing. For an institution in this position, a governance framework that optimises for stability is a liability, not an asset.

> KPIs answer the question: how are we doing? OKRs answer the question: where do we want to go, and are we getting there? A university that only monitors its past cannot govern its future.

Objectives and Key Results were developed in the technology sector as a framework for setting ambitious directional goals and measuring progress toward them. Their adaptation for higher education governance requires care — universities are not technology companies, and the crude application of commercial performance management to academic institutions has a poor track record. The OUG's OKR framework is designed for an academic institution: it respects academic autonomy, protects qualitative dimensions of educational quality, and treats performance data as a basis for learning rather than judgment.

The critical distinction: OKRs measure progress toward transformation. They do not measure the value of academic work directly. A faculty member who does not appear prominently on any OKR dashboard may be doing some of the most important work in the institution. OKRs govern the institution's strategic trajectory; they do not govern individual academic contribution.

## II. OKR Architecture

### Structure of an OKR

An OKR consists of two components. The **Objective** is a qualitative statement of a desired direction or state: ambitious, meaningful, and communicable. It answers the question: what do we want to achieve? The **Key Results** are measurable indicators that define what achieving the objective would look like: specific, time-bound, and verifiable. They answer the question: how will we know we are getting there?

| Component | Design Requirements |
|---|---|
| **Objective** | Qualitative. Directional. Ambitious but achievable. Written in plain language. Should be meaningful to someone outside the institution. Maximum one sentence. |
| **Key Results** | Measurable. Time-bound. Binary or scored (0.0–1.0). Defines a specific, observable outcome — not an activity ("run workshops") but a result ("80% of academic contributors report confidence in digital pedagogy"). 2–4 Key Results per Objective. Not all need be quantitative — qualitative evidence-based indicators are acceptable where quantitative measurement isn't meaningful. |
| **Scoring** | At the end of the period, each Key Result is scored. 0.7 is a good score. Consistently achieving 1.0 means the Objectives were not ambitious enough. |

### Cascade: From Institutional to Operational

OKRs operate at four levels in the OUG, corresponding to the four governance layers. Higher-level OKRs provide context and direction for lower-level OKRs; lower-level OKRs feed results upward. The cascade is not top-down command — it is contextual alignment. Each level sets its own OKRs in response to the level above, but the specific content of lower-level OKRs is determined by those closest to the work.

| Layer | Owner | Objective (example) | Key Results (example) | Review Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **G1 Constitutional** | G1 Stewardship Body | Long-cycle institutional transformation: "The OUG becomes a recognised anchor institution in the European federated learning infrastructure." | Secured HFV in Year 3; enrolled 500+ students across 3 programmes; recognised by 2 EU policy frameworks. | Annual review; linked to Strategy cycle |
| **G2 Strategic** | Governing Council / Rector | Strategic period goals: "The OUG's federation reaches 10 partner institutions and delivers seamless credential interoperability." | 10 signed partnership agreements; ECTS transfer accepted by 5 EU institutions; 0 student complaints about credential recognition. | Semester review; reported to Senate |
| **G3 Academic** | Academic Senate / Senate Committees | Academic quality and learning goals: "OUG programmes produce graduates with demonstrable competency in their field." | Employer survey: 85% rate graduate readiness as "strong"; programme completion rate above 75%; external examiner reports: no systematic quality concerns. | Semester review; Senate agenda item |
| **G4 Operational** | Programme teams, Research Clusters, Partnership Office | Execution goals: "The partnership onboarding process is seamless for new contributors." | Time from signed agreement to first module delivery: under 6 weeks; partner satisfaction score above 4.0/5; 0 onboarding failures. | Monthly or quarterly; reported to G3 |

### II.4 — Programme-Level Instantiation

The cascade above sets institutional and layer-level direction. It does not, by itself, say how a specific accredited programme connects to that direction — the gap the Strategy Framework's own review notes leave open. This section closes it with a single standing rule, so that connecting a programme to institutional strategy is a generalised, repeatable step rather than a bespoke exercise performed once and re-invented for each new programme.

**The rule.** Every accredited programme — current or future — is assigned an Objective with 2–4 Key Results at the point of Portfolio Governance approval. Each Objective must be explicitly derived from one or more of the Strategy Framework's Strategic Objectives (SO1–SO6), named in the assignment record. The Objective sits at the G3/G4 boundary, consistent with how a Mission Hub's own Objective is assigned at the point of its establishment (Grundordnung § 8 Absatz 9), and is reviewed on the same cadence as other G3/G4-level OKRs above. This assignment happens once, as a standing consequence of Portfolio Governance approval — a new programme triggers the rule automatically and does not require a new document, amendment, or bridge instrument of its own.

**Worked example — New Study (B.Sc. Computer Science, Digital Health Hub).** New Study is the OUG's first programme instantiated under this rule. It was not adapted from an existing offering to fit the OUG: it was built years ahead of and substantially beyond DHBW's own current model — skills-based, fully digitised, deliberately designed past DHBW's own envelope — and is now upscaled into the OUG's federated architecture as the first proof of concept at working scale for what the institution's design can do.

Its Objective draws on **SO1** (Academic Quality and Integrity at Scale) and **SO4** (Coherent Skill Profiles and Qualification Equivalence). Candidate Key Results are drawn from data the programme already produces rather than invented independently of it — module- and competency-level coverage from the Skill Profile Atlas, and the AI/human capability balance already tracked across its 31 modules — so the first instance of this rule is evidence-backed from the outset, not a fresh estimate.

### OKR Periods and Cycles

Institutional OKRs (G1/G2) are set for the strategy period — typically four to six years — with annual check-ins. Strategic OKRs (G2/G3) are set annually, reviewed each semester. Operational OKRs (G3/G4) are set each semester and reviewed monthly or quarterly depending on the domain. Programme-level Objectives under § II.4 follow the G3/G4 cadence. This creates a system of nested cycles: long-range direction at the top, fast-cycle learning at the bottom.

## III. Two Phases: Founding and At Scale

The OUG is a pre-launch institution. Its OKR framework must work in two fundamentally different conditions: the founding phase, when the institution is being built and every metric is a new data point, and the at-scale phase, when stable data flows allow genuine trend analysis and benchmarking. These phases require different OKR designs.

### Founding Phase OKRs (Years 0–3)

> **Founding Phase Characteristics.** In the founding phase, baseline data does not exist. Targets are informed estimates. The primary governance value of OKRs in this phase is not accurate measurement — it is shared direction. Everyone in the institution knows what the OUG is trying to achieve. OKRs in this phase should be ambitious but not punitive: if a founding cohort of 300 students becomes 250, that is not a failure of governance — it is learning about market demand.

Founding-phase OKR design principles:

- Set OKRs for institutional building, not performance optimisation. Goals like "accreditation secured", "first cohort enrolled", "HFV pilot agreement signed" are founding-phase OKRs.
- Use leading indicators, not lagging indicators. In the founding phase, the most useful measures are those that predict future performance — partner engagement levels, student application rates, accreditor feedback — not those that report past performance.
- Review monthly, not quarterly. The founding phase moves quickly. A governance dashboard that is updated monthly allows course corrections that a quarterly review misses.
- Document assumptions explicitly. Every founding-phase OKR rests on assumptions about market demand, partner engagement, and regulatory timing. When a Key Result is missed, the first question is: which assumption was wrong? This is learning, not failure.

### At-Scale OKRs (Years 4+)

> **At-Scale Phase Characteristics.** Once the institution has two or more years of operational data, OKRs can be grounded in empirical baselines. Trend analysis becomes meaningful. Peer benchmarking against comparable institutions becomes possible. The governance challenge shifts from "are we building the right things?" to "are we operating at the quality level we claim?"

At-scale OKR design principles:

- Anchor OKRs in empirical baselines. Set targets as improvements on demonstrated performance, not arbitrary aspirations.
- Benchmark externally. Where comparable data exists from similar institutions — particularly open universities and federated learning networks — use it to calibrate ambition.
- Distinguish transformation OKRs from maintenance OKRs. Not every OKR should be about growth. Some OKRs should be about deepening quality, strengthening community, or improving the partner experience. These are equally strategic.
- Allow OKRs to be retired. An OKR that has been achieved and is now a stable operational standard should be converted to a KPI (quality monitoring) and retired from the OKR system. OKRs are for transformation, not for tracking things that are already working well.

## IV. The Governance Dashboard

The Governance Dashboard is the institutional infrastructure through which OKR progress is made visible to all governance participants. It is not a reporting tool — it is a governance instrument. Its purpose is not to hold people accountable for numbers but to create shared situational awareness and enable collective decision-making about where the institution needs to redirect effort.

> A dashboard that no one looks at is decoration. A dashboard that generates fear is counterproductive. The OUG's Governance Dashboard is designed to generate learning: it makes progress visible, flags concerns early, and creates the shared understanding that enables collective governance.

### Dashboard Architecture

The dashboard is structured in four panels, one per governance layer. Each panel displays the current OKRs for that layer, the progress score for each Key Result (updated each review period), a traffic-light status indicator, and a brief narrative from the responsible body on what the data means and what action is planned.

**G1 — Constitutional & Long-Cycle Panel:** Institutional mission fidelity (are we still serving the learners we were designed to reach?); strategic period OKR progress (annual review of long-cycle objectives); constitutional compliance indicators (no items in breach); Hochschulfinanzierungsvertrag status (agreement timeline and milestone tracking).

**G2 — Strategic Panel:** Federation growth (partner institutions signed, active, in pipeline); enrolment trajectory (actual vs. projected across programmes); revenue and sustainability (three-pillar funding — public / fees / industry — tracking); strategic OKR scores (semester update on G2-level Key Results).

**G3 — Academic Quality Panel:** Programme completion and progression rates by programme; student experience indicators (survey results, community participation rates); academic contributor engagement (teaching quality indicators, research output); quality concern log (open items, resolved items, time-to-resolution); programme-level Objectives assigned under § II.4, one entry per accredited programme.

**G4 — Operational Panel:** Partner onboarding pipeline (applications, agreements, active delivery); student support (advisory caseload, response times, escalation rate); platform performance (uptime, accessibility compliance, user satisfaction); OKR execution rate (% of G4 operational OKRs on track).

### Access and Transparency

The full Governance Dashboard — all four panels — is visible to all members of the OUG governance community: Senate members, committee members, academic contributors, student representatives, and partner institution representatives. Selective visibility (showing only some layers to some participants) is not permitted; it undermines the shared situational awareness that the dashboard is designed to create.

A summary version of the dashboard, focused on G1 and G2 panels, is published in the annual Institutional Report and is publicly accessible. This fulfils the OUG's commitment to transparency with prospective students, partner institutions, and accreditation bodies.

### The Bug Protocol — When Key Results Are Missed

When a Key Result is scored below 0.4 at a review point, this triggers the **Bug Protocol** — a structured response process that treats the missed result as institutional learning rather than individual failure.

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1. Flag** | The missed Key Result is flagged on the dashboard with a brief narrative from the responsible body explaining what happened. |
| **2. Diagnose** | The responsible body, in consultation with the Strategy Coordinator, identifies the root cause: wrong assumption, insufficient resource, external factor, or poorly designed Key Result. |
| **3. Classify** | The diagnosis classifies the miss as: (a) assumption failure — the OKR needs revision; (b) execution failure — the work plan needs revision; (c) external — no revision needed, document and monitor. |
| **4. Act** | For (a) or (b), a formal amendment is proposed through the Portfolio Governance Framework change classification process. The amendment and its rationale are documented in the dashboard. |
| **5. Learn** | At the end of each strategy period, all Bug Protocol activations are reviewed collectively as part of the institutional learning cycle. Patterns of assumption failure inform the next Environmental Scan. |

### OKRs and Academic Freedom

OKRs at G3 and G4 level must be designed to support, not constrain, academic work. No OKR may be used to direct the content of academic teaching or research. No individual academic contributor may be evaluated on institutional OKR scores. The function of academic OKRs is to create shared awareness of the institution's learning and quality trajectory — not to measure individual academic performance.

Where an OKR touches areas of academic judgment — for example, programme quality or research output — the Academic Senate has exclusive authority to interpret and respond to the data. The G2 layer may note concerns but may not direct academic responses to OKR findings. This is the operational expression of G3 independence.

> The Governance Dashboard does not run the institution. The people do. The Dashboard's function is to give those people — in every governance layer — the shared view of institutional reality that makes genuine collective governance possible. Without it, governance is based on impression and authority. With it, governance is based on evidence and shared responsibility.
