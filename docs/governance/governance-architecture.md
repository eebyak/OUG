## 0. Purpose and Scope of the Governance & Decision Framework

This Governance & Decision Framework specifies how the Open University of Germany (OUG) exercises authority, participation, and accountability in a legally compliant and academically robust manner. It operationalises the Strategy Constitution by translating constitutional principles into a clear architecture of decision-making, governance roles, and escalation paths. The framework ensures that strategic direction, academic quality, and operational execution are coherently aligned while remaining adaptable to change.

This document is intentionally positioned **between** the Strategy Constitution and the institution's formal statutes (Grundordnung and related regulations). It does not replace legally binding documents, nor does it prescribe detailed procedures. Instead, it provides a transparent and auditable reference for how decisions are made, how participation is structured, and how responsibilities are assigned. As such, it serves three functions simultaneously: (1) it guides the drafting and interpretation of statutes, (2) it supports accreditation by making governance traceable and intelligible, and (3) it stabilises institutional practice by preventing ambiguity, overlap, or informal power shifts.

**On the meaning of "binding" in this document.** Three distinct senses of *binding* appear throughout this framework and should not be conflated:

1. **Legally binding** — competences fixed by the LHG, the Grundordnung, and other applicable statutes. This framework does not itself establish legal force.
2. **Internally authoritative** — once adopted as a governance and interpretation instrument, this framework is binding on institutional practice and on the drafting and interpretation of statutes.
3. **Procedurally binding** — specific mechanisms defined in Sections 4 and 6 (e.g. mandatory agenda intake, justification duties) become formally binding once incorporated into rules of procedure, statutes, or institutional regulations.

Where this document uses "binding" without further qualification below, sense (2) is meant. It does not, on its own, create sense (1).

## 1. Governance Principles

The governance model of the OUG is grounded in a set of explicit principles derived from the Strategy Constitution. These principles are binding across all governance layers and decision domains.

**1.1 Legitimacy through Participation.** Legitimacy is established not solely through formal authority, but through structured, meaningful participation. Academic staff, learners, and relevant societal partners are systematically involved in agenda-setting, co-creation, and consultation processes, particularly in matters affecting academic quality and strategic direction.

**1.2 Clear Accountability.** Every decision domain is assigned to a single, clearly identifiable authority. Consultation and participation do not dilute responsibility. This principle ensures that accountability remains transparent and enforceable.

**1.3 Protection of Academic Quality.** Academic standards, assessment integrity, and curricular coherence are constitutionally protected. Decisions in these domains are insulated from short-term financial or political pressures and are governed by academically legitimate bodies.

**1.4 Strategic Coherence and Long-Term Orientation.** Strategic decisions are aligned with the institution's mission and long-term objectives. Governance mechanisms are designed to prioritise sustainability, adaptability, and institutional learning over reactive or fragmented decision-making.

**1.5 Transparency and Escalation.** Decision-making processes, rationales, and outcomes are documented and accessible. Clear escalation paths exist for resolving conflicts, addressing constitutional tensions, and correcting governance failures.

## 2. Governance Layers and Their Roles

To manage complexity without sacrificing clarity, governance at the OUG is structured into four interrelated layers. Each layer is defined by its purpose, decision horizon, and accountability logic. Together, they form a coherent system that separates constitutional stewardship, strategic steering, academic governance, and operational execution.

**2.1 G1 — Constitutional Stewardship.** This layer safeguards the integrity of the Strategy Constitution. It is responsible for interpreting constitutional principles, resolving constitutional conflicts, and approving constitutional revisions. Decisions at this level are rare, high-impact, and oriented toward long-term institutional stability. G1 does not engage in operational or day-to-day strategic management.

**2.2 G2 — Strategic Steering.** The strategic steering layer sets institutional priorities within the bounds of the constitution. It is responsible for strategic objectives, portfolio logic at an abstract level, resource allocation, and structural decisions. G2 operates on multi-year cycles and integrates evidence from academic, societal, and financial perspectives. While it holds strategic authority, it cannot override academic standards or reinterpret constitutional principles.

**2.3 G3 — Academic and Quality Governance.** This layer governs all matters related to academic standards, curriculum architecture, assessment, and quality assurance. It ensures the coherence and integrity of educational offerings and maintains continuous improvement processes. Decisions here are grounded in academic expertise and collegial legitimacy and are protected from undue strategic or financial interference.

**2.4 G4 — Operational Execution.** Operational execution translates decisions from the upper layers into concrete actions. This layer manages day-to-day operations, projects, staffing, infrastructure, and service delivery. It has no authority to redefine strategy, academic standards, or constitutional principles and is accountable for implementation quality and efficiency.

### 2.5 Institutional Mapping

Sections 2.1–2.4 define the four layers functionally. This table names, with statutory precision, which body occupies each layer under the [Grundordnung](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/grundordnung.md).

| Governance layer | Primary institutional body | Supporting bodies | Matters excluded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **G1 — Constitutional Stewardship** | **Hochschulrat** (Grundordnung § 6) | — | No operational management; no day-to-day strategic steering; does not engage in academic or resource decisions directly |
| **G2 — Strategic Steering** | **Rektorat** (Grundordnung §§ 3–4) | Kanzlerin/Kanzler as hauptamtliches Rektoratsmitglied for the resource-allocation dimension (§ 7) | No override of academic standards (D-D, D-E, D-F); no reinterpretation of constitutional principles |
| **G3 — Academic and Quality Governance** | **Senat** (Grundordnung § 5) | Mission Hubs / Stewards of Missions within their scope (§ 8); programme and examination committees operating under Senate-approved frameworks | No budgetary execution; no organisational restructuring |
| **G4 — Operational Execution** | **Kanzlerin/Kanzler and central administration** in their execution function (Grundordnung § 7) | Steward of Missions administrative delivery within each Mission Hub (§ 8) | No reinterpretation of strategy, academic standards, or constitutional principles |

**Note on the Kanzler/in's dual position.** The Kanzlerin or Kanzler is, by law, a *member of the Rektorat* (§ 3 Absatz 2 Nr. 2) and therefore formally part of G2. Functionally, however, the Kanzler/in's statutory responsibility is *lawful execution* of the budget and administration (§ 7), which corresponds to a G4-type function. Section 3.7 below states this split explicitly for the resource-allocation domain.

**Escalation between G1 and the Strategy Constitution's "highest strategic authority."** The Strategy Constitution (§ 6.4) reserves interpretation to "the highest strategic authority of the institution, as defined in subsequent governance arrangements." This table constitutes that governance arrangement: the highest strategic authority for constitutional interpretation is the **Hochschulrat**, acting as G1.

## 3. Decision Domains and Decision Rights

To ensure clarity, accountability, and legal robustness, decision-making at the OUG is organised by **decision domains**. A decision domain defines a class of decisions that share the same authority logic, risk profile, and escalation requirements. This approach deliberately separates *what* is decided from *how* participation is organised and *who* ultimately carries responsibility.

Each decision domain is assigned to **exactly one governance layer** as the final decision authority. Other layers may propose, co-create, or be consulted, but responsibility for the decision outcome remains unambiguous. This design prevents overlap, informal vetoes, and diffusion of accountability, while remaining fully compatible with participatory governance and legal requirements.

**On authority, responsibility, and related terms.** The domains below assign a single **decision authority** — the body that formally decides — to each domain. This is distinct from: **process ownership** (who prepares and routes the decision), **implementation responsibility** (who executes it), **oversight** (who reviews legality, alignment, or outcomes), and **participatory rights** (who must be involved before the decision is made, per Section 4). Where a domain description below says a layer "holds final authority," it means decision authority specifically; the other roles are distributed across layers as described in each domain and in Sections 4–6.

### 3.1 Constitutional Integrity and Interpretation (D-A)

This domain governs the interpretation, safeguarding, and evolution of the Strategy Constitution. It includes decisions on constitutional meaning, the resolution of conflicts between constitutional principles, and the approval of constitutional revisions. Decisions in this domain are rare, long-term in scope, and existential in nature. Final authority rests with the Constitutional Stewardship layer (G1 — Hochschulrat, see Section 2.5), which may act upon escalations from other layers when constitutional tensions arise.

### 3.2 Strategic Direction and Priorities (D-B)

This domain encompasses decisions on institutional strategy within the bounds of the constitution. It includes the formulation of strategic objectives, cross-pillar prioritisation, approval of strategic key performance indicators, and the adjustment of strategy across defined review cycles. Final authority lies with the Strategic Steering layer (G2 — Rektorat). Strategic decisions are informed by structured participation and evidence, but responsibility for coherence and prioritisation remains clearly assigned.

### 3.3 Portfolio Logic and Scope (D-C)

Portfolio decisions concern the abstract scope of academic and educational offerings. This includes determining which fields, domains, or types of programmes are sustained, expanded, transformed, or phased out at an institutional level. This domain does not include individual programme approvals or curriculum design. Portfolio logic is governed by the Strategic Steering layer (G2), with escalation to Constitutional Stewardship (G1) where portfolio decisions raise constitutional questions, particularly regarding mission alignment or academic integrity. Change classification and routing procedures for this domain are governed by Portfolio Governance (Grundordnung § 9).

### 3.4 Academic Standards and Assessment (D-D)

This domain defines what constitutes academically valid learning, assessment, and certification. It includes academic standards, assessment principles, examination frameworks, recognition of prior learning, and rules governing academic integrity. Given its centrality to institutional credibility, final authority is vested in the Academic and Quality Governance layer (G3 — Senat, Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 1). Strategic or financial considerations may inform but cannot override decisions in this domain.

### 3.5 Curriculum Architecture (D-E)

Curriculum architecture governs the structural design of curricula across programmes. It includes modularisation rules, progression logic, workload and credit models, and alignment with national and international qualification frameworks. Decisions in this domain ensure coherence and comparability across educational offerings. Final authority rests with the Academic and Quality Governance layer (G3), ensuring that curricular design remains academically grounded and systematically aligned.

### 3.6 Quality Assurance and Continuous Improvement (D-F)

This domain covers the mechanisms through which educational quality is monitored, evaluated, and improved. It includes quality indicators, evaluation frameworks, feedback loops, accreditation readiness, and thresholds for intervention or redesign. Continuous improvement is treated as an ongoing governance responsibility rather than a periodic compliance exercise. Final authority is assigned to the Academic and Quality Governance layer (G3), operationalised in part through the Quality Commission (see the [Governance Review Calendar](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/governance-review-calendar.md)), whose independence from G2 is itself a scheduled legitimacy criterion.

### 3.7 Resource Allocation and Budgeting (D-G)

Decisions on the allocation of financial and material resources are governed within this domain. It includes budget distribution, strategic investment priorities, and financial risk management. Resource allocation decisions are explicitly aligned with strategic priorities and institutional sustainability.

**G2 (Rektorat) determines strategic allocation priorities** — which pillars, portfolio areas, or structural investments receive resourcing emphasis — **within the competences of the legally responsible organs.** Budget preparation, adoption, execution, and control remain governed by the Grundordnung, applicable budget law, and the Kanzlerin/Kanzler's statutory responsibility for lawful execution of the Wirtschafts- or Haushaltsplan (§ 7 Absätze 2–3). G2 does not itself own budget execution; that is a G4 function performed within the Kanzler/in's statutory role (see the note on dual position in Section 2.5).

**Senate consent is required, specifically and only, where a resource decision would materially affect academic standards or programme viability** — this is a narrow, targeted consent right protecting what Section 1.3 already commits to protecting, not a general financial approval power. A revenue-distribution formula, a partner financial settlement, or an institutional retention percentage does not, by itself, trigger this consent right merely because it involves federation partners who also deliver teaching; it triggers only where the specific allocation choice would change what can be taught, assessed, or maintained at OUG-required academic standard.

### 3.8 Organisational and Structural Design (D-H)

This domain concerns the internal organisational structure of the institution. It includes decisions on organisational units, internal roles, responsibilities, and structural adaptations required to execute strategy effectively. Structural design decisions are strategic in nature and fall under the authority of the Strategic Steering layer (G2), with implementation delegated to the Operational Execution layer (G4).

### 3.9 Operational Management (D-I)

Operational management covers day-to-day execution of decisions across all domains. This includes project implementation, staffing, infrastructure management, and service delivery. Authority in this domain lies exclusively with the Operational Execution layer (G4). Operational decisions do not include the authority to reinterpret strategy, academic standards, or constitutional principles.

### 3.10 Escalation and Boundary Integrity

Where decisions in any domain raise conflicts across domains or governance layers, clearly defined escalation paths apply. Escalation is limited to questions of constitutional alignment, domain boundary violations, or unresolved conflicts of competence. This ensures that governance remains both decisive and self-correcting, while preserving the integrity of the Strategy Constitution.

> The vertical escalation path described in Section 6.5 is the **constitutional escalation** route specifically. Horizontal coordination (e.g. between two Mission Hubs), competence-boundary review, academic escalation, administrative/legal escalation, and individual legal remedies each follow a different route — see the **[Conflict Escalation Framework](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/conflict-escalation-framework.md)** for the full set of six routes and worked examples.

## 4. Participation and Agenda-Setting Architecture

The OUG distinguishes deliberately between **decision authority** and **participatory intelligence**. While formal decision rights are clearly assigned to specific governance layers and decision domains (Section 3), the quality, legitimacy, and sustainability of those decisions depend on how issues are identified, options are developed, and perspectives are integrated **before** formal decisions are taken.

This section defines the Participation and Agenda-Setting Architecture through which academic expertise, learner experience, and societal knowledge systematically shape institutional decisions. Participation is therefore not an informal add-on or a consultative gesture, but a **structurally embedded component** of governance.

### 4.1 Purpose of the Participation Architecture

The participation architecture serves four core purposes:

1. **Strategic Intelligence.** To ensure that strategic decisions are informed by disciplinary knowledge, pedagogical expertise, learner experience, and societal developments.
2. **Legitimacy.** To anchor decisions in shared understanding and collective sense-making, particularly in areas that affect academic work and learning conditions.
3. **Quality Protection.** To surface risks, unintended consequences, and quality implications early in the decision process.
4. **Agenda Balance.** To prevent strategic agendas from being driven exclusively by executive, financial, or short-term considerations.

Participation does not replace decision authority. It **precedes and informs** it.

### 4.2 Modes of Participation

Participation within the OUG governance model is structured into four distinct modes, each with a clearly defined function:

**Agenda-Setting.** Agenda-setting bodies have the right to formally place issues, questions, or challenges on the strategic or academic agenda of decision-making bodies. Issues raised through agenda-setting must be acknowledged and processed through the appropriate decision domain.

**Co-Creation.** Co-creation bodies are responsible for developing substantive options, scenarios, or alternatives within a defined scope. Co-creation ensures that decisions are based on considered alternatives rather than binary approval or rejection.

**Mandatory Consultation.** In defined domains, consultation with specific groups is mandatory. Consultation does not imply veto power, but requires documented consideration of input and reasoned response.

**Feedback and Evidence Loops.** Continuous feedback from learners, academic staff, and partners feeds into quality assurance, strategic review, and institutional learning.

Each decision domain specifies which modes of participation apply and which bodies are involved.

### 4.3 Participatory Bodies and Their Functions

Within this architecture, existing legally recognised bodies operate with **expanded constitutional semantics**, and additional participatory formats are explicitly recognised as part of the governance system.

**Community Circles.** Serving as primary agenda-setting forums for strategic direction, institutional priorities, and cross-cutting issues. This is the constitutional identity the Senate carries quarterly, in addition to its formal decision-making sessions (Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 14) — a deliberately separate convening, not restructured time inside the statutory vote. Participation is two-tier: the quarterly Community Circle itself seats the Senate together with Mission Hub Stewards and academically affiliated persons (advisory voice, per Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 14); External Cooperation Councils (below) feed into the Community Circle on a defined trigger rather than attending every session. For partner-institution input specifically into curriculum and programme design, the **Programme Development Forum** (see the [Governance Review Calendar](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/governance-review-calendar.md)) is the operative venue producing documented Decision Accounts, distinct from the Community Circle's broader strategic remit.

**Permanent representation for non-degree lifelong learners.** Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 15 grants Kontaktstudium participants — learners engaged in non-degree stackable learning under § 31 LHG, who are not immatrikulierte students and therefore fall outside the Verfasste Studierendenschaft under § 65 LHG — a **standing, elected, permanent advisory seat** in the Community Circle, and, where relevant, in their Mission Hub's own sessions (Grundordnung § 8 Absatz 14). The seat count is not fixed at a token one regardless of scale: it starts at one, but rises to **two — matching the regular student seat count under § 5 Absatz 6 Nummer 3** — once Kontaktstudium participants are at least as numerous as immatrikulierte students, certified by the Kanzler/in against registry data before each electoral term and logged in the Governance Review Calendar. Once raised, the count does not fall again mid-term even if the ratio later shifts back. This is deliberately not an occasional guest invitation: the seat exists for the full electoral term, exactly as the Mission Hub Steward and affiliated-academic seats do. The distinction between this seat and a Senate vote is a real legal boundary, not a design choice OUG made — but the boundary stops at the vote. It does not extend to standing, voice, continuity of presence, or proportionality of representation as the population grows. A learner moving between immatrikuliertem Studium and Kontaktstudium participation — in either direction, at any point — does not lose institutional standing in the transition (Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 15).

**Mission Hubs.** Operating at the level of faculties or academic domains, Mission Hubs combine disciplinary expertise with mission-oriented responsibility. They initiate agenda items related to portfolio logic, curriculum development, and academic innovation, and lead co-creation processes within their scope.

**Strategic Rapporteurs and Task Groups.** Time-limited, mandate-based roles responsible for synthesising inputs, developing options, and preparing decision-ready materials for strategic and academic bodies.

**Learner and Alumni-for-Life Communities.** Providing continuous feedback on learning experience, progression, and outcomes, particularly relevant for quality assurance and access-related decisions. This participatory format is distinct from, and does not substitute for, the formal constitutional standing of the *Verfasste Studierendenschaft* and elected student representatives in the Senate and in Mission Hub bodies (Grundordnung § 11 and § 5 Absatz 6 Nummer 3).

**External Cooperation Councils.** Offering structured consultation and horizon-scanning input from societal, regional, and professional contexts. This category spans several distinct stakeholder types with different legitimacy and relationships to academic authority, and should not be treated as a single undifferentiated group: (1) partner higher-education institutions, (2) Dual Partners and employers, (3) public and regional stakeholders, and (4) professional and societal bodies. The specific participation rights of each are governed by the relevant partnership and federation instruments, not by this framework.

**Cadence.** External Cooperation Councils convene **annually**, reviewed under the [Governance Review Calendar](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/governance-review-calendar.md), and feed their findings into the next scheduled Community Circle rather than attending it directly — this is deliberately lighter-touch than the Community Circle's own quarterly cadence, appropriate to a group whose relationship to academic authority is advisory rather than the sustained, mandated engagement that characterises Community Circle participation. Where an External Cooperation Council's input concerns a specific Mission Hub's societal or regional relevance rather than institution-wide strategy, it routes to that Hub's own **[Mission Hub Advisory Panel](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/mission-hub-advisory-panels.md)** rather than through the Community Circle — each Mission Hub convenes its own dedicated panel annually, distinct from this institution-wide mechanism.

These participatory formats are explicitly linked to decision domains and governance layers through defined coupling mechanisms — see Section 6.

### 4.4 Coupling Participation to Decision Authority

To ensure that participation has real effect, the governance framework defines binding coupling mechanisms between participatory processes and formal decision-making. These mechanisms — mandatory agenda intake, option-based decision making, the justification duty, and transparency of outcomes — are defined in full in Section 6, which is the authoritative source for their procedural detail. In short: participation is coupled to decision authority through the mechanisms defined in Section 6, not through a separate parallel set of rules.

### 4.5 Relationship to Legal Authority

The participation and agenda-setting architecture operates fully within the boundaries of legal governance requirements. It does not alter formal competences, legal accountability, or statutory decision rights. Instead, it **shapes how those competences are exercised** by embedding structured intelligence, deliberation, and legitimacy upstream of formal decisions.

This design ensures compliance with legal frameworks while significantly expanding the quality and inclusiveness of governance practice.

### 4.6 Safeguards Against Governance Drift

To prevent informal power shifts or procedural overload, participation is governed by the following safeguards: clear mandates and scopes for participatory bodies, time-limited and purpose-bound participatory formats, explicit linkage to decision domains, and regular review of participation effectiveness.

Participation is thus treated as a **governed capability**, not an open-ended process.

### Status of the Participation Architecture

The Participation and Agenda-Setting Architecture is an integral part of the Governance & Decision Framework. It is internally authoritative in the sense defined in Section 0 — in its design logic and coupling mechanisms — and must be reflected in subsequent governance documents, quality assurance processes, and institutional practice.

## 5. Dual-Identity Governance Bodies

The governance model of the OUG is based on a **dual-identity approach** to institutional bodies. Each governance body retains its **legal identity and competences** as defined by applicable higher education law, while simultaneously operating under an **explicit constitutional identity** derived from the Strategy Constitution.

This dual-identity model allows the institution to preserve legal clarity and accountability while redefining the internal logic, purpose, and mode of operation of governance bodies. It ensures continuity with statutory requirements while enabling a governance culture oriented toward stewardship, participation, and mission-driven decision-making.

### 5.1 Principle of Dual Identity

Under the dual-identity model: the **legal identity** of a body defines its formal existence, statutory competences, and accountability under law; the **constitutional identity** defines how the body understands and exercises its role within the institutional governance system.

The constitutional identity does not replace or contradict the legal identity. Rather, it **interprets and operationalises** legal competences in alignment with the Strategy Constitution and the Governance & Decision Framework.

### 5.2 Senate — Community Circle for Academic and Strategic Deliberation

**Legal Identity:** Senat (Grundordnung § 5) · **Constitutional Identity:** Community Circle

The Senate operates as the central forum for academic governance and collective deliberation. In its constitutional identity as a Community Circle — convened quarterly, separately from its formal decision-making sessions, per Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 14 — it extends its role beyond formal approvals to structured agenda-setting, strategic co-creation, and the articulation of academically grounded institutional priorities. This separation is deliberate: it exists so that strategic deliberation has protected time of its own, rather than being compressed into the agenda of a session whose primary business is formal votes.

The Senate holds overarching and statutory academic authority over academic standards, curriculum architecture, and quality assurance (Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 1). Defined programme-level, examination-related, and quality functions may be delegated to Mission Hubs, examination boards, and academic committees within Senate-approved frameworks (Grundordnung § 8 Absätze 7–8). Within this role, the Senate also initiates and shapes strategic discussions affecting academic work, contributes to strategic option development through deliberation and mandates, and ensures that academic perspectives are integrated into institutional strategy.

The Senate does not assume executive authority for strategy or resource allocation. Its constitutional role strengthens academic agency and strategic participation while preserving clear decision boundaries.

### 5.3 Rectorate — Coordination Council

**Legal Identity:** Rectorate / Rektorat (Grundordnung §§ 3–4) · **Constitutional Identity:** Coordination Council

The Rectorate holds executive responsibility for strategic steering, resource allocation, and organisational design. In its constitutional identity as a Coordination Council, it understands its role as integrating, arbitrating, and implementing strategy rather than monopolising strategic imagination.

In this role, the Rectorate consolidates strategic inputs from participatory and academic bodies; decides among strategically viable options developed through co-creation; ensures alignment between strategy, resources, and operations; and is accountable for implementation and institutional performance.

The Rectorate does not unilaterally define strategy without prior participatory development and remains bound by academic standards and constitutional principles.

### 5.4 Faculties — Mission Hubs

**Legal Identity:** Faculties (Fakultäten), formally Mission Hubs under Grundordnung § 8 · **Constitutional Identity:** Mission Hubs

Faculties operate as Mission Hubs that integrate disciplinary expertise with institutional mission and societal responsibility. As Mission Hubs, faculties are not merely administrative containers but active sites of strategic intelligence, academic innovation, and curriculum development.

Mission Hubs initiate agenda items related to academic portfolio, curriculum, and innovation; lead co-creation processes within their academic scope; steward academic quality and coherence across programmes; and connect disciplinary expertise with external partners and learners. Every Mission Hub additionally maintains the four designed cross-Hub interfaces specified in the **[Mission Hub Permeability Architecture](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/mission-hub-permeability-architecture.md)** — cross-Hub research seminars, a cross-Hub project strand, rotating joint governance membership, and an annual Permeability Review — as a standing governance requirement, not an optional enrichment. This addresses a distinct risk from the one this section otherwise discusses: not whether Mission Hubs connect *outward* to society and partners, but whether they connect *sideways* to each other, so that replacing disciplinary faculties with mission-organised Hubs does not simply recreate the Comfortable Silo antipattern under a new name. The outward-facing counterpart — whether the Hub remains relevant to the societal challenge it claims to organise around — is the dedicated **[Mission Hub Advisory Panel](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/mission-hub-advisory-panels.md)** each Hub convenes annually, publishing a public Communiqué rather than a private report.

Mission Hubs retain their formal competences but operate with an explicit responsibility for mission alignment and institutional contribution.

> **Resolved dependency:** Mission Hub membership under the Grundordnung (§ 8 Absatz 3) is defined through locally employed personnel and enrolled students; it does not extend to academically affiliated staff engaged through partner institutions. This is not a gap to be reconciled — it is by design. Affiliated academics participate through a different, real mechanism: advisory voice in the Community Circle (Grundordnung § 5 Absatz 14) and curriculum contribution through the Programme Development Forum, both detailed in the [Federated Legal Model](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/federated-legal-model.md) § 8. Statutory Mission Hub membership and Senate voting rights remain reserved to those the Grundordnung actually grants them to; extending either would require a Grundordnung amendment, not a contractual workaround.

### 5.5 Deans — Academic Stewards

**Legal Identity:** Deans / Dekanate · **Constitutional Identity:** Academic Stewardship

Deans exercise leadership within faculties while safeguarding academic standards and coherence. In their constitutional identity as Academic Stewards, deans focus on stewardship rather than managerial control.

Academic Stewards facilitate co-creation and strategic reflection within Mission Hubs; ensure alignment between academic innovation and institutional principles; represent academic concerns in strategic processes; and support quality assurance and continuous improvement.

This role emphasises coordination, mediation, and long-term academic responsibility over hierarchical command.

### 5.6 Programme and Subject Committees — Program Leadership and Expertise Councils

**Legal Identity:** Programme committees, subject commissions, or equivalent · **Constitutional Identity:** Program Leadership and Expertise Councils

These bodies ensure the academic integrity, coherence, and continuous improvement of individual programmes and subject areas. They operate under delegated authority within the Academic and Quality Governance layer and apply institutional standards to specific disciplinary contexts.

### 5.7 Hochschulrat — Strategic Alignment Board

**Legal Identity:** Hochschulrat (Grundordnung § 6) · **Constitutional Identity:** Strategic Alignment Board and Constitutional Steward (G1)

The Hochschulrat functions as the external oversight body and, as established in Section 2.5, is the institutional embodiment of Constitutional Stewardship (G1) — the final authority for constitutional interpretation, resolution of constitutional conflicts, and approval of constitutional revisions (Domain D-A, Section 3.1).

Its role includes safeguarding alignment between institutional strategy, mission, and societal expectations; overseeing the integrity of governance processes; and acting as an escalation point for constitutional conflicts.

The Hochschulrat does not engage in operational or academic micromanagement.

### 5.8 Consistency and Boundary Protection

The dual-identity model is governed by explicit boundary rules: no body may act outside its legally defined competences; constitutional identities do not create additional formal authority; conflicts between constitutional interpretation and legal competences are resolved through defined escalation mechanisms.

This ensures that governance innovation strengthens, rather than undermines, legal clarity and institutional trust.

### Status of Dual-Identity Governance

The dual-identity governance model is internally authoritative, in the sense defined in Section 0, as an interpretive framework for institutional practice and the drafting of statutes. It provides continuity with legal requirements while enabling a governance culture oriented toward stewardship, participation, and mission-driven strategy.

## 6. Coupling Mechanisms, Escalation, and Safeguards

The governance model of the OUG is designed to combine **broad participation** with **clear decision authority**. To ensure that participatory processes are consequential and that formal authority is exercised responsibly, explicit coupling mechanisms and escalation rules are defined. These mechanisms bind participation to decision-making without creating ambiguity regarding competence, accountability, or legality.

### 6.1 Purpose of Coupling Mechanisms

Coupling mechanisms serve to: ensure that participatory intelligence meaningfully informs decisions; prevent unilateral or opaque decision-making; protect academic quality and constitutional principles; and maintain decisiveness and institutional velocity.

Participation without coupling would risk becoming symbolic; authority without coupling would risk becoming disconnected. The mechanisms defined below are therefore **binding elements** of the governance framework, in the procedurally-binding sense defined in Section 0 once incorporated into rules of procedure.

### 6.2 Mandatory Agenda Intake

Recognised participatory bodies, including Community Circles and Mission Hubs, have the right to place issues, questions, or proposals on the agenda of the relevant decision-making body.

Formal decision bodies are required to acknowledge agenda submissions, place them on the agenda within defined timeframes, and indicate the decision domain under which they will be processed.

Agenda items may not be silently filtered or indefinitely deferred. Where an agenda item is rejected as inadmissible, a written justification referencing decision domains and constitutional principles must be provided.

> **Known gap:** the specific timeframes for agenda intake, and the rules determining what makes a "recognised participatory body" or an "admissible" submission, are not yet defined in this document. These belong in the Senate's, Rectorate's, and Gremienordnung's rules of procedure, or in a dedicated participation statute; until drafted, this mechanism is aspirational rather than enforceable.

### 6.3 Option-Based Decision Making

For strategic, portfolio, and structural decisions, formal authorities act on **options**, not on single, pre-determined proposals.

Accordingly: **material strategic, portfolio, and structural decisions must normally be based on at least two distinguishable alternatives, one of which may be continuation of the status quo; a single-option process requires documented justification.** Options must articulate implications for academic quality, access, sustainability, and mission alignment. Decision authorities may select, combine, or reject options but may not substitute them with unilaterally developed alternatives without restarting the participatory process.

This mechanism ensures that strategic authority is exercised on the basis of informed alternatives rather than executive preference, and aligns with the two-option (A/B) comparator logic already used in the Strategic Constitution's decision protocol (Anlage 2).

### 6.4 Justification and Transparency Duties

Where formal decision authorities deviate from recommendations or preferences articulated through participatory processes, they are subject to a **justification duty**.

Such justifications must be documented, reference relevant constitutional principles, explain trade-offs and prioritisation choices, and be communicated back to the originating participatory bodies.

This duty does not create veto power, but it establishes transparency, accountability, and institutional learning.

### 6.5 Escalation Rules

Escalation mechanisms apply when decision domains overlap or are contested, constitutional principles appear to be violated, or persistent conflicts arise between governance layers.

Escalation follows a defined path: (1) clarification within the relevant governance layer, (2) escalation to the next higher governance layer, (3) constitutional review where boundary or principle conflicts persist.

Escalation is limited to questions of **competence, constitutional alignment, or unresolved conflict**. It is not a substitute for disagreement resolution within normal governance processes, and it is not the correct route for every kind of conflict — see the note in Section 3.10. In particular, this path does not cover: horizontal conflicts between two Mission Hubs (which should resolve through direct coordination before any escalation); operational-legality questions properly routed to the Kanzler/in rather than to G3; or examination disputes, which follow formal legal appeal paths under the Prüfungsordnung rather than constitutional escalation. The complete typology of escalation routes, with worked examples, is defined in the **[Conflict Escalation Framework](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/conflict-escalation-framework.md)**.

### 6.6 Safeguards Against Power Concentration

To prevent governance capture or informal power shifts, the following safeguards apply: single-decision authority per domain, as defined in Section 3; separation of agenda-setting and decision authority, as defined in Section 4; time-limited mandates for participatory roles and task groups; transparent documentation of decisions and rationales; and regular review of governance effectiveness and participation quality.

These safeguards ensure that governance remains adaptive, balanced, and resilient.

### 6.7 Relationship to Legal Authority

All coupling and escalation mechanisms operate within the boundaries of legally defined competences. They shape **how** authority is exercised, not **where** authority resides. Legal accountability remains fully intact.

The governance framework thus strengthens legality by making decision processes explicit, transparent, and auditable.

### Status of Coupling and Escalation Mechanisms

The coupling mechanisms and escalation rules defined in this section are internally authoritative components of the Governance & Decision Framework, in the sense defined in Section 0. They must be reflected in institutional practice and considered in the drafting and interpretation of statutes, regulations, and quality assurance procedures.

## 8. Status, Review, and Amendment

**8.1 Adoption.** This framework becomes internally authoritative, in the sense defined in Section 0, upon adoption by the governance bodies named in Section 2.5, following the same procedure by which the Grundordnung itself is adopted (Senat and Hochschulrat resolution).

**8.2 Relationship to conflicting statutes.** Where this framework and a statute conflict, the statute prevails (Section 0); the conflict should be treated as a signal that this framework requires revision.

**8.3 Review cycle and responsibility.** The Institutional Mapping (Section 2.5) and the Decision Domains (Section 3) should be reviewed whenever the Grundordnung, the Federated Legal Model, or the Strategic Constitution are revised, and otherwise on a regular governance review cycle. Responsibility for maintaining this framework, including the mapping table, rests with G1 (Hochschulrat).

**8.4 Amendment.** Amendments to this framework follow the **[Amendment Procedure](https://github.com/eebyak/OUG/blob/main/docs/governance/amendment-procedure.md)**, which also clarifies its relationship to the Strategic Constitution's own § 8 procedure and the Grundordnung's § 16 procedure.
