*Open University of Germany · Institutional Foundations · 2026*

> **Status:** *(to be set — see [Document Status](../index.md#document-status))* · **Version:** v3.0
> **What this document is.** This is a standalone register of every principle articulated across the dialogues underlying *Designing a University from First Principles*, re-sorted by meaning rather than by the dialogue it came from. It is derived from, and should be read alongside, the book — the full argument, the participant context, and the derivation reasoning for each principle live there. This register exists because the book's own forty-two-item pattern catalogue (the antipattern/design-pattern pairs) is a different, smaller instrument: derived from these principles, not identical to them. As the software-engineering distinction this register borrows puts it — a principle is always present; a design pattern is a reusable solution that may or may not be used to implement it. Multiple principles can collapse into one derived pattern, and one principle can inform several patterns. Sorting the principles on their own terms, independent of the pattern catalogue's count, is the necessary first step before that catalogue — or the institution's own maturity signature — can be checked against its actual foundations.
> **How this register is organised.** Every principle below carries its original identifying number and its source dialogue as a short citation at the end of its entry — for traceability back to the book — but the *sections* of this register are organised by theme, not by source. A reader should be able to use this document without ever opening the book, though the book remains the authoritative account of why each principle was derived and what it means in full.
> **What this register does not do.** It does not restate the book's argument, its literature grounding, or its worked examples. It does not describe which OUG governance documents implement each principle — that mapping is a separate, later exercise, once this register is settled as the fixed reference point. Fifty-five principles are listed, derived from eight dialogue sources, plus three reconstructed founding principles that sit above and inform all of them — fifty-eight in total. *(An earlier pass through this register undercounted by four — HP-1, HP-6, CP-1, and CP-4 were identified but never placed in a section. Corrected below.)*

## 0. The Three Founding Principles

*These three sit above the fifty-eight that follow. They are not derived from a contemporary dialogue but reconstructed from the documented governing arrangement of the University of Bologna at its founding — offered not as nostalgia but as a normative, historically grounded standard. Every principle in Sections 1–9 either supports, operationalises, or was checked against one of these three.*

**Learning Is the Goal.** An institution exists to produce learning — not credentials, not research output, not ranking position, not accreditation compliance as ends in themselves. When institutional structures serve purposes other than learning, they must be held accountable to that divergence. *(Founding Principle 1, Chapter 1 — reconstructed from the original Bologna arrangement.)*

**The Learner Directs the Learning Relationship.** Accountability runs from the institution toward the learner's development and access — not from the learner upward through an institutional hierarchy. This is a structural claim, not a rhetorical one: the original Bologna professor was accountable to the student who hired him, not the reverse. *(Founding Principle 2, Chapter 1 — reconstructed from the original Bologna arrangement.)*

**What Is Available to Learn Is Determined by Learner Need.** The range of what an institution teaches should follow demonstrated need, not inherited disciplinary structure or supply-side convenience. Diversity of offering is a consequence of diversity of demand, not a faculty commitment pursued independently of it. *(Founding Principle 3, Chapter 1 — reconstructed from the original Bologna arrangement.)*

## 1. Purpose: Learning as the Institutional Goal, Not Its Byproduct

*Principles addressing what an institution is actually for, and what happens when that purpose is displaced by proxies — grades, throughput, compliance — that can be optimised independently of whether learning occurs.*

**The University as a Socially Embedded Institution.** An institution's purpose is a direct, structural response to an unmet social condition — not a mission statement layered onto an otherwise self-directed institution. *(P-12, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Purpose Before Access.** An institution must define its purpose and target population in substantive terms — who it serves, what they gain, why existing provision fails them — before any access mechanism is designed. Access without a defined purpose is expansion without direction. *(CP-1, Person Curr4.)*

**Presence as Pedagogical Design.** Deep learning happens through the presence of a more capable other providing the right scaffolding at the right moment — not through the transmission of content at scale. Institutions must design for this presence deliberately rather than assume it emerges from enrolment. *(GP-2, Person 6gov.)*

**Learning Is Measured by What Endures.** Institutional success should be measured by what a learner can still do after completion, not by inputs, throughput, or satisfaction at the point of delivery. *(GP-3, Person 6gov.)*

**The Living Mandate.** An institution's design should respond to the learner the existing legal and institutional framework does not yet recognise as its primary subject — not the learner the framework was originally built around. *(HP-2, HEI Observations.)*

**Concept Over Content.** Curriculum should be organised around the development of transferable concepts and demonstrated competency, not around coverage of content — content delivery is a proxy that can be satisfied without the underlying capability ever developing. *(CP-5, Person Curr4.)*

**Transformation-Oriented Performance.** Institutional performance management should track whether the institution is progressing toward what it said it would become, not only whether it is currently stable and healthy — the two are different questions and require different instruments. *(P-7, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Visible Development.** A learner's growing capability should be visible throughout their engagement with the institution, not only certified at a single endpoint. Partial progress carries real value. *(LP-3, Person Learn8.)*

## 2. Accountability Direction: Toward the Learner, Toward the Learner's Development

*Principles addressing which direction institutional accountability actually runs — a distinct question from purpose, since an institution can state a learner-centred purpose while its structures quietly hold it accountable upward, to funders, accreditors, or its own survival, instead.*

**Distributed and Staged Decision-Making.** No single actor should be able to take a binding institutional decision unilaterally; decisions move through defined preparation, coordination, and ratification stages so that accountability is structurally distributed, not concentrated. *(P-2, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Continuous Feedback and Adaptive Execution.** Institutions require structured, frequent feedback loops between implementation and strategy — not only periodic, retrospective review — so that adaptation happens while it can still matter. *(P-6, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Checks and Balances.** No single actor or governance layer should control outcomes across every domain; a multi-layer system of distinct, overlapping authorities is what makes institutional accountability real rather than declared. *(P-10, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Committees as Distributed Intelligence.** Governance committees should be constituted by domain expertise and function, not by status or representation for its own sake — their purpose is to bring relevant knowledge into decisions, not to perform inclusion. *(P-8, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Participation, Authority, and Accountability Must Be Connected.** Who participates, who decides, and who is accountable for the outcome must each be named explicitly and linked to one another — participation without a connection to authority and accountability produces the appearance of legitimacy without its substance. *(GP-7, Person 6gov.)*

**Designed Participation.** Participation formats must be deliberately designed for the full range of learner circumstances — including asynchronous, non-traditional engagement — or participation rights become formally available but practically inaccessible to the learners who most need them. *(LP-4, Person Learn8.)*

## 3. Access and the Learner's Starting Point

*Principles addressing who can enter, on what terms, and whether the institution is designed around the learner who actually exists rather than the learner traditional structures assume.*

**Designed Diversity.** The range of learner profiles an institution is built to serve should be an explicit design decision, stated and planned for, not an incidental consequence of whoever happens to apply. *(LP-1, Person Learn8.)*

**Designed Access.** Entry pathways — recognition of prior learning, professional experience, flexible admission criteria — must be operationally specified, not merely permitted in principle; access without a genuine, resourced pathway is not access. *(LP-5, Person Learn8.)*

**Quality-Gated Federation.** In a model where multiple institutions contribute to a shared credential, the entry gate is quality verification, not negotiation between institutions — an explicit, non-negotiable standard replaces case-by-case institutional bargaining. *(CP-2, Person Curr4.)*

## 4. Guided Flexibility: Coherence Without Rigidity, Freedom Without Fragmentation

*The most consistently recurring tension across every dialogue source: flexibility that lacks a coherent developmental logic produces fragmentation, not freedom, while orientation without flexibility produces rigidity. Every source names a version of this same problem from a different vantage point.*

**Strategic Clarity and Feasibility.** Institutional goals must be few enough, and concrete enough, to be genuinely assessed for feasibility — a strategy that cannot be checked against reality is not a strategy, however inspiring it reads. *(P-5, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Structure as Home and Challenge.** An academic unit should be simultaneously a stable intellectual community and a source of productive friction — a unit that is only comfortable becomes a silo; a unit with no stable identity becomes a matrix structure with no home at all. *(SP-3, Person 7org.)*

**Adaptive Governance.** Governance architecture should be designed with revision built in from the start — structures that cannot be reviewed, retired, or reformed without crisis will eventually calcify regardless of how well they were originally designed. *(SP-2, Person 7org.)*

**Quality-Gated Federation as Curricular Boundary Condition** *(see also Section 3)* — the same quality gate that governs entry also governs how much flexibility a federated contributor is given once inside: flexibility in delivery, not in the standard being delivered against. *(CP-2, Person Curr4, cited again for this dimension.)*

**Year 1 as Designed Trust.** The earliest phase of a learner's engagement should be deliberately designed to establish trust and relevance quickly — flexibility introduced too early, before trust exists, reads as instability rather than responsiveness. *(CP-6, Person Curr4.)*

**Strategy Before Structure.** Institutional structure should follow from a clearly articulated strategy and target population — not the reverse. An institution that designs its organisational chart before it has decided who it serves will retrofit its purpose to its structure instead of the other way around. *(CP-7, Person Curr4.)*

**The Guiding Thread.** Modular or stackable learning is valuable only when connected by a visible, coherent developmental logic that shows a learner how each piece contributes to a meaningful whole — modularity without a thread is fragmentation wearing flexibility's name. *(LP-2, Person Learn8.)*

**Flexibility Already Exists — The Challenge Is Enabling Institutions to Use It.** Before designing a new framework to gain flexibility, an institution should check whether existing legal and structural frameworks already permit it — inflexibility is frequently cultural and procedural, not structural, and a new governance layer built inside an unchanged administrative culture will reproduce the old rigidity under a new name. *(GP-8, Person 6gov.)*

## 5. Role Clarity, Mandate, and Bounded Authority

*Principles addressing whether every governance role has an explicit, written boundary — what it may decide, what it must consult on, what it must refer upward — since without this clarity, institutional dysfunction gets attributed to individual failure rather than structural ambiguity.*

**Layered System Alignment.** Governance authority should be organised into explicit layers with clearly bounded domains, each insulated from inappropriate interference by the others — particularly academic authority from financial and executive pressure. *(P-1, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Role Clarity and Mandate Transparency.** Every governance role requires an explicit, written mandate specifying its authority, its consultation obligations, and its boundaries — role ambiguity is not a minor inefficiency, it is the condition under which power quietly accumulates in whoever is willing to exceed their remit. *(P-9, Person 1 & 2.)*

**The Aligned Authority.** Every governance body should carry a single, consistent authority classification — decision, consent, or advisory — applied uniformly, so that no body's actual weight in a decision depends on who happens to be in the room. *(HP-3, HEI Observations.)*

**The Bounded Role.** Governance roles must have explicit boundaries on scope and duration — an unbounded role, however well-intentioned its holder, tends to expand to fill whatever space institutional drift leaves open. *(HP-4, HEI Observations.)*

**Expert Design Authority.** Curriculum design decisions and governance ratification decisions are distinct kinds of authority and should be exercised by different bodies on different grounds — design authority rests on expertise; governance authority rests on representation and accountability. Collapsing the two lets either capture the other. *(CP-3, Person Curr4.)*

**The Unbundled Teacher.** Teaching delivery and curriculum design/outcome specification are separable authorities and should be held separately — a partner or contributor may deliver teaching without thereby holding authority over what the curriculum requires or how it is assessed. *(CP-4, Person Curr4.)*

**Responsibility for Transformation Must Be Explicitly Assigned.** An institution that intends to change must name the specific person or body responsible for driving that change and hold them accountable for the outcome — when transformation is everyone's concern, it is structurally no one's mandate. *(GP-5, Person 6gov.)*

**Encode the Innovation, Not the Innovator.** Governance architecture should encode the conditions that sustain innovation structurally, so they survive leadership transitions — an innovation that depends on one person's energy is not yet institutionalised. *(EH-1, Helix.)*

## 6. Governance Architecture: Protected Independence and Legitimate Participation

*Principles addressing the constitutional design of governance itself — where authority sits, what must be insulated from what, and how genuine participation is distinguished from its appearance.*

**Collegial Governance.** Academic decision-making should be genuinely collegial, with real checks on the concentration of individual power — a governance culture that is collegial in name but hierarchical in practice under pressure has not actually built this principle in. *(P-3, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Participatory Strategy Formation.** Strategy that is handed down to those who must implement it produces compliance; strategy built together with them produces ownership — participatory formation is not a courtesy extended to stakeholders, it is what makes the resulting strategy actually survive contact with implementation. *(P-4, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Governance as Designed Counterbalancing.** Governance layers should be designed to counterbalance one another deliberately — strategic direction checked by academic independence, executive authority checked by constitutional stewardship — rather than assuming goodwill will hold the balance informally. *(SP-4, Person 7org.)*

**The Protected Strategy.** Genuine strategic deliberation requires protected time and space, structurally separated from routine operational coordination — strategy discussed only inside operational meetings will always lose to whatever is most urgent that week. *(HP-5, HEI Observations.)*

**The Structured Crossing.** The points where distributed actors — students, partner institutions, external stakeholders — meet institutional governance should be deliberately designed as structured crossings, not left as informal, ad hoc contact points. *(HP-8, HEI Observations.)*

**The Designed Task.** In any relationship involving distributed contributors, every recurring task must be explicitly assigned an owner — tasks left unassigned are not neutral; they default to whoever happens to be nearest, which is not the same as being correctly assigned. *(HP-7, HEI Observations.)*

## 7. Leadership as a Professionalised, Bounded Vocation

*Principles addressing leadership specifically: not as a reward for academic excellence or a permanent status, but as a learnable, time-bounded, professionally developed role.*

**Professionalisation of Leadership.** Governance leadership should be treated as a developable competency with structured development pathways — not assumed to follow automatically from academic seniority or disciplinary excellence. *(P-11, Person 1 & 2.)*

**Leadership as Legitimate Vocation with Structural Accountability.** Leadership roles should carry term limits, defined return pathways, and structural protection from being consumed entirely by operational demand — a vocation, not a permanent status or an unbounded sacrifice. *(SP-7, Person 7org.)*

**The Heart and Mind of Leadership.** Effective institutional leadership requires both genuine commitment to academic norms (the scientist's heart) and professional capacity to design organisations and make decisions (the manager's mind) — neither alone is sufficient, and institutions should develop and assess both dimensions explicitly rather than assuming one implies the other. *(SP-6, Person 7org.)*

**Exogenous Incentives Can Shift Governance Logic — But Only If Leadership Converts Them.** External policy incentives can shift institutional behaviour, but only if leadership has the capacity to convert the incentive into genuine internal direction — an incentive arriving without that conversion capacity produces reorganisation dressed up as transformation. *(GP-6, Person 6gov.)*

**The Designed Incentive.** Institutional financial structures should be designed so they do not create their own perverse incentives — the classic case being a year-end spending flush produced by block-grant absorption pressure. The incentive structure itself is a governance design question, distinct from whether leadership can convert an external incentive well. *(HP-6, HEI Observations.)*

## 8. Law and Structure as Enabling Conditions, Not Just Constraints

*Principles addressing the relationship between an institution's design ambitions and the legal and structural framework it operates within — the recurring finding, across multiple independent sources, that existing frameworks typically permit more than institutional habit assumes.*

**Design Before Constraint.** The design conversation should precede the constraint conversation — purpose is defined independently of current feasibility, with constraints legitimately shaping implementation afterward, not the reverse. Starting with feasibility produces answers that reflect the constraint more than the actual need. *(GP-1, Person 6gov.)*

**The Legible Law.** An institution should be able to state, for any of its own design choices, whether that choice is a statutory requirement or an institutional choice made within legally available room — legibility about this distinction is itself a governance discipline, not a one-time compliance exercise. *(HP-1, HEI Observations.)*

**Law as Enabling Structure.** Legal and regulatory frameworks should be read for the flexibility they actually grant, not assumed to be as restrictive as institutional convention treats them — the gap between "what the law permits" and "what institutions habitually do" is frequently wide and frequently unexamined. *(LP-6, Person Learn8.)*

**Audit Over Accreditation — Maturity Against Self-Defined Goals.** An institution should hold itself accountable to a genuine, peer-based review of whether it is achieving its own stated purpose — a review distinct from, and prior to, formal accreditation compliance, which asks a narrower and different question. *(SP-8, Person 7org.)*

## 9. Governance Capacity for Innovation, Renewal, and Institutional Learning

*The theme every completed dialogue source touches independently — a striking convergence from otherwise very different vantage points. These principles address not what a well-designed institution looks like at a single moment, but what allows it to keep being well-designed as conditions change and its founders eventually leave.*

**Governance Is the Condition for Sustained Pedagogical Innovation.** Excellent individual teaching produces excellent individual learning experiences, but only deliberate governance structures — protected time, propagation mechanisms, recognition systems — turn a personal achievement into an institutional asset that survives the individual who created it. *(GP-4, Person 6gov.)*

**The Learning Organisation.** Institutions need explicit structural permission to experiment, observe outcomes without punishing failure, and propagate successful practice beyond its point of origin — a learning organisation is a governance design, not a cultural aspiration. *(SP-5, Person 7org.)*

*Designed Access (LP-5, cross-referenced from Section 3) has a renewal dimension worth noting here: entry design is not only an access question but a renewal question — an institution that only ever admits learners who already fit its existing structures cannot discover what those structures are failing to serve.*

**Expressible Vision.** An institution's distinctive vision should be tested at multiple levels of compression — a short, immediate statement through to a full narrative — against real audiences, not merely asserted internally; a vision that has never been said aloud to a stranger carries real risk of being wrong in ways only that conversation would reveal. *(CP-8, Person Curr4.)*

**The Diverse Ecosystem Principle.** An institution should be able to articulate the distinctive role it plays in its wider ecosystem — what only it does, or does uniquely well — rather than defining itself only by comparison to nearby institutions. *(SP-1, Person 7org.)*

**Diagnose at the Correct Level.** Before applying any governance intervention, determine whether the problem actually resides at the individual, organisational, or ecosystem level — intervening at the wrong level produces visible effort and invisible change, and is the single most common reason institutional reform fails to hold. *(EH-0, Helix.)*

**Design Bureaucracy as a Medium for Experimentation.** Governance architecture should distinguish explicitly between the parts that must remain stable and the parts that should stay permeable to experimentation — not every decision requires full institutional ratification, and treating all of them as though it does eliminates the institution's capacity to learn from trying things. *(EH-2, Helix.)*

**Design the Interfaces First.** The productive crossing points between institutional actors — university, industry, government, partner institutions — matter more than optimising any single actor in isolation; interface design should precede and shape unit design, not follow it as an afterthought. *(EH-3, Helix.)*

**Build the Ecosystem That Produces Innovators.** Sustained innovation is better secured by investing in the norms, support structures, and community conditions that let ordinary people innovate together than by trying to recruit exceptional individuals — the ecosystem produces the innovator, not the reverse. *(EH-4, Helix.)*

## Status of This Register

This register is the fixed reference point against which the pattern catalogue (the forty-two antipattern/design-pattern pairs) and the institution's maturity signature should both, eventually, be checked — not the reverse. A pattern that cannot be traced to a principle in this register has no derivation; a principle in this register with no corresponding pattern is not a gap in the register, since not every principle requires a structural pattern to be honoured — some are honoured by a single governance decision rather than a reusable design.

This register does not itself claim that any principle is currently built, partial, or absent in the OUG's governance architecture. That assessment belongs to a separate, later exercise.
