*Proposed for adoption by European higher education institutions, university alliances and national cooperative bodies.*

## Preamble

> *"Digital infrastructure is not a product. It is the foundation on which democratic society, open science and human development operate. Europe must build and govern it accordingly."*

We, the higher education institutions, university alliances and national cooperative bodies that adopt this Manifesto, make a collective declaration about the kind of digital future we intend to build — and the role that European higher education will play in building it.

This declaration arises from a recognition that digital infrastructure — the cloud platforms, artificial intelligence systems, data networks and software that now underpin every dimension of education, research and public life — has become as fundamental to European society as the energy grid, the transport network, the public health system and the rule of law. These things are not optional amenities. They are the operating conditions of a functioning, free and prosperous society.

Yet unlike energy, transport and health, the digital infrastructure on which Europe increasingly depends is largely owned, operated and governed outside Europe. The consequences are not merely commercial. They are constitutional. When the platforms on which European citizens communicate, learn, research and govern themselves are subject to foreign law, foreign commercial priorities and foreign strategic interests, European autonomy is structurally compromised — regardless of what our laws say in principle.

This Manifesto argues for something fundamental: that Europe must ensure it has the sovereign capacity to choose its infrastructure, govern its data, develop its artificial intelligence, and define its own digital future. That capacity requires deliberate, coordinated, publicly supported investment. Higher education institutions have a specific and irreplaceable role in making it happen.

We adopt this Manifesto because the decisions made in the next five years — about AI, cloud infrastructure, data governance, digital credentials and educational technology — will determine the competitive, cultural and democratic position of Europe for a generation. We intend to participate actively in shaping those decisions, not merely respond to them.

## Part I: The Case

### 1. Digital Infrastructure Is a Public Good

The foundational insight of this Manifesto is that digital infrastructure — cloud computing, artificial intelligence platforms, data networks, identity systems and the software that runs on them — now belongs in the same category as roads, electrical grids, water systems and public health networks. It is essential infrastructure with specific economic, political and ethical properties that distinguish it from ordinary commercial products.

#### 1.1 The Characteristics of Public Infrastructure

Public infrastructure is characterised by four properties that, taken together, explain why market mechanisms alone cannot govern it adequately:

**Network effects at scale.** The value of a network increases with each participant. Once a network reaches sufficient scale, alternatives become practically unusable regardless of their technical merit. This is why digital platforms tend toward natural monopoly. Leaving natural monopolies to unregulated commercial governance produces concentration, rent extraction and the subordination of public interest to shareholder value.

**High barriers to entry.** Building competing infrastructure requires capital investment at a scale that most actors — including most nation states — cannot sustain alone. This is why European countries built the energy grid cooperatively, established public rail networks, and the EU funds the Galileo satellite system. The same logic applies to cloud infrastructure and AI compute.

**Externalities that markets cannot price.** The social costs of inadequate digital infrastructure — citizens unable to access public services, researchers locked out of their own data, democratic processes vulnerable to platform manipulation — cannot be captured in a price signal. Markets will systematically underprovide infrastructure whose social value exceeds its commercial value.

**Security and resilience as public necessities.** Critical infrastructure must be resilient against attack, failure and abuse. This requires governance mechanisms — mandatory security standards, incident reporting, redundancy requirements — that commercial actors will resist unless compelled. The NIS2 Directive recognises this for physical infrastructure; the same logic must be applied consistently to digital infrastructure.

#### 1.2 The Economic Stakes

Global AI-related economic activity is projected to reach approximately €1.3 trillion in 2026, growing to €14 trillion by 2030. Europe currently captures a small fraction of this value — not because European talent or research capability is inadequate, but because the infrastructure layer on which AI applications run is largely controlled by a small number of non-European platforms.

Infrastructure control is power. The entity that controls the platform on which European AI applications run determines what those applications can do, what they cost, and under what conditions they continue to operate. Dependency at the infrastructure layer creates strategic vulnerability at every layer above it — in research, in education, in healthcare, in public administration, and in democratic governance itself.

Europe has allowed this dependency to develop gradually, procurement decision by procurement decision, each individually defensible on commercial grounds and individually insufficient to trigger the political response it warranted. The accumulated effect is a structural vulnerability that no single institution can address alone. Addressing it requires coordinated, publicly governed, multi-stakeholder investment — the kind Europe has historically made successfully in energy, transport and research infrastructure.

#### 1.3 The Governance Model: Public Foundation, Industry Partnership

Asserting that digital infrastructure is a public good does not mean industry has no role. It means the governance relationship must be correctly ordered. Industry participates as a partner under public governance — contributing capital, engineering capability and commercial expertise — not as the primary governance authority.

Cross-financing through industry participation is necessary and desirable. The scale of investment required for competitive European AI and cloud infrastructure exceeds what public budgets alone can sustain. The governance principle is simple: participation in decision-making is proportional to public accountability, not to financial contribution. Industry contributes; public institutions govern.

> **The Governance Principle**
>
> Industry may co-finance European digital infrastructure. Industry may operate components under public contract. Industry may participate in standards development and governance advisory processes. Industry may not hold majority governance authority over infrastructure that serves public functions. The distinction between contribution and control is constitutional, not commercial.

### 2. Artificial Intelligence: Reclaiming the Narrative

Artificial intelligence has become the defining technology of this decade. We believe the current dominant narrative about AI is both historically inaccurate and strategically damaging to Europe's interests. We offer a different account.

#### 2.1 Where AI Actually Came From

The modern artificial intelligence revolution is not a commercial invention. It is the product of decades of publicly funded academic research, conducted overwhelmingly in universities, and made available to the world through open publication, open-source software and the collaborative norms of science.

Alan Turing's foundational work on computation and machine intelligence was conducted at Cambridge and Manchester, supported by British public funding. The perceptron was developed by Frank Rosenblatt at Cornell on US government contracts. Backpropagation — the algorithm that makes deep learning possible — was published by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986 as academic research. The transformer architecture that underlies every major language model was published in 2017 by Google Brain researchers as an open academic paper titled "Attention Is All You Need," freely available to the world from the day of publication.

European academic institutions contributed fundamentally to this history. The European Research Council has funded AI research across hundreds of institutions. INRIA in France, the Max Planck Institute in Germany, ETH Zürich, the Turing Institute in the UK, and dozens of other European centres have produced foundational work in statistical learning, probabilistic reasoning, computer vision, natural language processing and AI safety. The open-source ML ecosystem — PyTorch, Hugging Face's Transformers library, scikit-learn — was built primarily by academic researchers and freely shared with the world.

This history matters because it refutes the narrative that commercial AI companies are the originators of the technology they monetise. They are, in the main, the beneficiaries of decades of publicly funded academic work shared openly as a matter of scientific principle. The commercial AI industry was built on a foundation that European and global academia constructed and donated to humanity. That foundation deserves recognition, and the institutions that built it deserve a central role in governing what grows from it.

#### 2.2 The Captured Narrative and Its Consequences

The dominant AI narrative today frames it as a competitive race between a small number of well-capitalised technology companies, measured by benchmark performance, and governed by the commercial interests of the leading players. This narrative has four consequences that are harmful to Europe and to the broader public interest:

**It marginalises open-source and academic AI.** When progress is measured by the capabilities of the largest closed commercial models, the substantial and valuable work on open, auditable, governable AI systems is systematically undervalued. Open-source AI — Mistral, Llama, Falcon and the broader Hugging Face ecosystem — represents a legitimate development model that is more consistent with European values and more amenable to public governance.

**It creates artificial urgency.** The race framing creates pressure to deploy systems faster than is prudent, to prioritise capability over safety, and to treat governance as an obstacle. This serves the interests of actors who benefit from first-mover advantage; it does not serve the public interest in safe, reliable and accountable AI.

**It concentrates governance in the wrong hands.** When AI is framed as a commercial competition, leading companies acquire de facto governance authority over infrastructure that affects everyone. Safety standards, access policies and the fundamental question of what AI systems should do are determined by commercial incentives rather than democratic deliberation.

**It disempowers European institutions.** The narrative that only the largest commercial actors can develop competitive AI systematically discourages European public investment and institutional ambition. It is self-fulfilling: if European policymakers believe that academic AI cannot compete, they will not invest at the scale required to make it competitive.

#### 2.3 The Alternative Model: AI as Open Science and Public Infrastructure

We assert a different model. AI development that serves European society must be grounded in the traditions of open science: open publication of methods and results, peer review, collaborative development across institutional boundaries, and governance by the communities most affected by the technology.

This is not utopian. The Mistral AI models, developed in France and released under open licences, demonstrate that European institutions can build frontier AI capabilities. The Hugging Face platform, founded by French researchers, is now the central hub of the open-source AI ecosystem. The LAION datasets, assembled by a European non-profit, enabled the open development of image generation models. The European Language Grid provides multilingual resources that commercial AI would never have created because the market is too thin.

The European AI ecosystem is not a consolation prize. It is a parallel and legitimate development model with specific strengths: transparency, auditability, multilingual capability, privacy by design, and alignment with democratic values. European higher education institutions are the natural home and champion of this model.

> **What We Mean by AI Sovereignty**
>
> AI sovereignty does not mean that European institutions must develop all AI independently. It means: European institutions can choose which AI systems to use based on capability, safety and values alignment — not because they are locked into a platform. They have the technical capacity to understand, audit and govern the AI they deploy. They contribute to AI development that serves the public interest. Personal data generated by European citizens is governed under European law, stored on European infrastructure, and used to benefit European society. The economic value generated by AI applications built on European data and research contributes to European prosperity.

#### 2.4 The Right to Contribute Personal Data to the Commons

The current dominant model of AI data governance is extractive: platforms collect user interactions as a condition of service, use them to train commercial AI systems, and return no value to the individuals whose data made the training possible. This model is both ethically problematic and legally questionable under GDPR's purpose limitation requirements.

We propose a different model: the right and the infrastructure for European citizens to voluntarily donate their personal data — including interactions with AI systems, learning activities and digital communications — to publicly governed AI training infrastructure. This donation model inverts the extractive relationship. Instead of data being taken as a condition of service, it is given freely, with informed consent, for a purpose the donor understands and endorses.

Higher education institutions are uniquely positioned to implement this model. They have relationships of trust with large populations of educated individuals, the data governance infrastructure to manage consented data collection, and the institutional credibility to assure donors that contributions will be used for the public good. We commit to working toward a European Personal Data Commons — a voluntary, governed, transparent infrastructure through which European citizens can contribute their data to AI development that serves the public interest.

## Part II: The Responsibility

### 3. The Responsibility of Higher Education to Support Europe

Higher education institutions occupy a unique position in European society. They are simultaneously: the primary producers of knowledge and talent that European society depends on; custodians of the open scientific tradition on which European AI and digital development must build; large-scale users of digital infrastructure whose procurement decisions shape markets; trusted public institutions with democratic legitimacy to speak in the public interest; and the training grounds for the next generation of researchers, engineers, policymakers and citizens who will govern the digital future. This position creates both an opportunity and a responsibility.

#### 3.1 HEIs as Anchor Institutions for European Digital Sovereignty

When a university of 30,000 students makes a procurement decision — choosing between a US hyperscaler and European sovereign cloud infrastructure, between a commercial LMS and an open-source European alternative, between a proprietary AI assistant and a sovereign, auditable model — that decision sends a market signal. Multiplied across hundreds of institutions, coordinated through alliances and cooperative bodies, procurement decisions at European higher education scale can shift markets, sustain open-source ecosystems, and create the demand that makes European digital infrastructure commercially viable.

This is not idealism. SURF's decision to build SURFdrive on Nextcloud created a production-scale deployment that validated the technology and contributed resources to its development. The German research network DFN's long-term commitment to open-source identity federation made eduGAIN viable. The Finnish Digivisio 2030 programme's commitment to a shared open learning platform is creating infrastructure that would not exist if 37 institutions had each made individual commercial choices. Coordinated European institutional procurement is a form of industrial policy, and it is one that European higher education should pursue deliberately.

#### 3.2 HEIs as Knowledge Producers and Governance Participants

The European AI Act, the Data Governance Act, the Data Act, the European Digital Identity framework and the emerging European Education Area interoperability standards are among the most significant pieces of digital governance legislation produced anywhere in the world. They were shaped by European academic research — in AI ethics, data law, privacy-preserving computation and digital identity — and they require academic expertise for their implementation, interpretation and evolution.

European universities have a responsibility to participate actively in this governance process — not as lobbyists for institutional interests, but as providers of independent, rigorous, publicly accountable expertise. We commit to making this expertise available through advisory participation in European legislative processes, through independent research on the impacts of digital governance decisions, through open publication of findings, and through active contribution to the standards bodies that govern the technical standards on which European digital infrastructure depends.

#### 3.3 HEIs as Demonstrators of the Alternative Model

Perhaps the most important role European higher education can play is demonstrating — in practice, at scale, in production — that the alternative model works. The argument that European open-source, sovereign, privacy-respecting infrastructure is less capable or less convenient than US commercial alternatives will only be refuted by evidence. That evidence must come from institutions that have implemented the alternative and can report honestly on their experience.

We commit to sharing our implementation experience — successes and failures equally — through open publication, through participation in European cooperative bodies, and through active support for institutions that are earlier in their transition journey. The knowledge we accumulate through our own digital transformation is itself a public good, and we will govern it accordingly.

#### 3.4 HEIs as Guardians of Student Data Rights

Universities hold among the most sensitive personal data that any institution collects: the academic performance records, learning behaviour traces, pastoral support interactions, financial circumstances, health disclosures and career outcomes of millions of young people at a formative stage of their lives. This data is held in trust. Students generated it as a condition of pursuing their education. The institutions that hold it bear a correspondingly elevated duty of care.

We commit to governing student data as a public trust, not as an institutional asset: collecting only what is necessary for defined educational purposes; retaining it only for as long as those purposes require; protecting it with security measures proportionate to its sensitivity; making it accessible to students themselves in usable forms; and never using it for commercial purposes or sharing it with commercial actors without explicit, informed, freely given consent. Student data protection is not a policy statement that sits above the architecture. It must be built into the architecture from the ground up.

## Part III: The Commitments

### 4. What We Commit To: The Platform Principles

The following principles govern the digital infrastructure decisions of institutions that adopt this Manifesto. These principles are binding in the sense that governance decisions — including procurement, architectural and partnership decisions — must be documented against them. Deviations are possible but must be explicitly justified, time-limited, and accompanied by a transition plan toward compliance.

#### Principle I: Infrastructure First, Products Second

**1. Architecture precedes procurement.** Before any system is procured, the institution defines the architectural layer in which that system will operate, the API contracts it must satisfy, and the exit conditions that will apply. No system is procured as a centre of gravity around which everything else must adapt. Every system is procured as a component that must fit into a defined architecture.

**2. Standards define the boundaries.** The boundaries between systems are defined by open, publicly documented standards — not by vendor-specific integration agreements. Where European standards exist (EWP, ELM, ESCO, eduGAIN, SAML 2.0, OIDC, LTI, xAPI, IMS Caliper), they are mandatory. Where they do not yet exist, the institution commits to working with European standards bodies to create them rather than accepting proprietary alternatives as default.

**3. Exit is a design requirement.** Every system procurement must include a documented exit strategy: how the institution's data will be migrated to a successor system, in what open format, within what timeframe, at what cost. For proprietary software components, contracts must additionally require: source code escrow with a European escrow agent, so the institution can continue operating the software if the vendor ceases to trade or withdraws the product; a documented build-from-source procedure that allows the institution to compile and deploy patched versions independently; and the explicit right to engage any third-party European support provider for updates, security patches and maintenance without the original vendor's permission or involvement. A contract that does not include a workable exit clause and these software continuity provisions does not meet the institution's procurement standards.

#### Principle II: European Sovereignty

**4. European infrastructure by default.** Where European sovereign infrastructure meets the institution's functional requirements, it is the default choice. "European" is defined functionally — infrastructure that operates under European legal jurisdiction, with data stored on European soil, governed under European law, with no extraterritorial access by foreign authorities.

**5. Open source as the sovereignty instrument.** Open-source software satisfies sovereignty requirements when three conditions are met simultaneously. First, hosting independence: the software runs on infrastructure the institution controls or that is under European jurisdiction. Second, update independence: security patches, bug fixes and functional updates can be received and deployed without dependency on a non-European vendor's commercial decisions, continued existence or pricing — the software is governed by a foundation or community with open governance and no single controlling entity, or the institution holds source code rights and can compile and deploy its own patched versions, or a European support provider can supply updates independently of the original vendor. Third, fork rights: the software licence (OSI-approved open-source) grants the right to modify and redistribute the source code, ensuring the institution or a European consortium can maintain and evolve the software independently if the upstream project is abandoned or its governance is captured. A proprietary product hosted on European servers satisfies hosting independence but fails on update independence and fork rights — it does not meet this principle. An open-source product whose update pipeline is controlled by a single non-European commercial entity satisfies hosting independence but not update independence, and requires case-by-case assessment. National origin of the software's original developers does not determine sovereignty status — governance structure and licence terms do. Moodle hosted by the institution on European infrastructure satisfies all three conditions; Moodle hosted by a US commercial cloud provider does not satisfy hosting independence regardless of the other conditions being met.

**6. Data residency is non-negotiable.** Personal data of European students, staff and research participants is stored, processed and governed under European law. Institutions that cannot currently meet this requirement for specific systems have a documented transition plan and a binding timeline for compliance.

#### Principle III: AI Governance

**7. Transparency and auditability.** AI systems deployed for functions that affect students or staff must be transparent in their operation and auditable by the institution's own staff without dependence on the vendor. This means: documented model architecture, accessible training data documentation, explainable outputs for consequential decisions, and the ability to audit system behaviour without the vendor's cooperation.

**8. Human decision authority.** AI systems support human decision-making; they do not replace it for consequential decisions affecting individuals. The EU AI Act's classification of high-risk AI systems in education is a floor, not a ceiling. Institutions commit to human review of all AI-generated recommendations that affect individual students or staff.

**9. European AI preference.** Where AI systems meeting functional requirements exist in the European open-source ecosystem — including Mistral-family models, the Hugging Face ecosystem, BLOOM and their successors — they are preferred over commercially closed non-European alternatives. This preference is subject to genuine functional equivalence assessment; it does not require accepting inferior capability.

**10. No surveillance by design.** AI systems in educational contexts do not perform surveillance of student behaviour beyond what is necessary for the stated educational purpose and consented to by the student. Behavioural data is pseudonymised or anonymised before leaving the learning system. No student data is shared with AI providers as training data without explicit, informed consent.

**11. AI literacy as institutional responsibility.** Institutions are responsible for ensuring that students and staff have sufficient AI literacy to use AI tools critically, understand their limitations, and recognise when AI outputs require human verification. AI literacy is a foundational competency for every graduate of a European institution.

#### Principle IV: Student Data as a Protected Trust

**12. Learner ownership of credentials.** Academic grades, qualifications and credentials are certified facts about a learner's achievement. Once certified by the institution, they belong to the learner. The institution maintains its archive as the authoritative witness; the learner holds the portable, cryptographically sealed representation that cannot be altered by any party. Students may export their credentials to any compatible system — digital wallets, Europass, partner institution registries — at any time without cost or institutional permission.

**13. Analytics in the service of learning.** The primary purpose of analysing student behaviour data is to improve the learning experience, identify students who need support, and enable personalisation of learning pathways. Institutional quality assurance is a legitimate secondary purpose. Commercial data exploitation, staff performance management based on student behaviour data, or any use that treats students as data sources rather than data beneficiaries are prohibited.

**14. The right to understand and challenge.** Students have the right to access their own learning data, to understand what inferences have been drawn from it, and to challenge those inferences through a human review process. Every learning analytics system must provide a student-accessible data dashboard, a plain-language explanation of how data is used, and a documented process for students to request corrections or contest automated recommendations.

#### Principle V: Cooperation Over Competition

**15. Alliance membership as the default.** Before any significant IT system is procured individually, the institution actively seeks an alliance or cooperative solution — at national level through bodies equivalent to SURF, SWITCH or DH.NRW; at European level through emerging cooperative structures. Individual procurement is the exception, justified when no suitable cooperative solution exists.

**16. Open contribution as a civic duty.** Implementation experience, connector code, API contract specifications, configuration templates and evaluation results produced by signatory institutions are contributed to European open-source and cooperative communities under open licences. The knowledge produced through publicly funded institutional transformation is itself a public good.

**17. Standards participation as a leadership responsibility.** Signatory institutions actively participate in the European and international standards bodies governing educational technology: 1EdTech, GÉANT, Erasmus Without Paper, the European Digital Credentials Infrastructure working groups, and relevant ISO and W3C working groups. Participation means contributing, not merely attending.

## Part IV: The Call to Action

### 5. What We Call For

This Manifesto is not addressed only to higher education institutions. Digital sovereignty and the responsible governance of AI are challenges that exceed the capacity of any single sector. We address specific calls to each actor who must contribute to a European digital future that is sovereign, open and democratically governed.

#### 5.1 To European Higher Education Institutions

- Commit to the architecture-first procurement principle: no significant system is procured without a documented architectural framework defining its role, its APIs and its exit conditions.
- Join or establish national cooperative IT bodies — equivalents of SURF, SWITCH and DH.NRW — and channel a meaningful share of IT procurement through these cooperatives.
- Implement student data rights principles operationally — not as policy statements but as system requirements in every learning technology procurement.
- Contribute to the European open-source educational technology ecosystem through code, configuration, documentation and governance participation.
- Participate actively in European University Alliances and ensure digital interoperability — not just academic collaboration — is a core alliance commitment.
- Report publicly and honestly on digital sovereignty status: what is currently sovereign, what is not, and what the transition plan is.

#### 5.2 To the European Commission and Member State Governments

- Establish a European AI and Cloud Infrastructure Fund — a dedicated public investment vehicle, jointly financed by EU institutions, member states and industry partners, to build the compute, storage and AI platform infrastructure that European institutions need. The EuroHPC programme demonstrates this model works at the compute layer; it must be extended to the full infrastructure stack.
- Mandate European interoperability standards for all publicly funded higher education systems — EWP compliance for all Erasmus+ participants, ELM/EDCI compliance for all publicly funded credential systems, eduGAIN membership for all publicly funded research institutions.
- Establish a European Personal Data Commons — publicly governed infrastructure for voluntary, consented data contribution to AI training, operated under the Data Governance Act framework, with full transparency about use and benefit-sharing with contributors.
- Sustain funding for open-source AI development in European academic institutions through Horizon Europe, the Digital Europe Programme, and dedicated national programmes — at a scale genuinely competitive with commercial R&D investment.
- Establish a European Digital Education Agency — a permanent body with mandate, staff and budget to coordinate European educational technology standards, support institutional transitions to sovereign infrastructure, and provide independent expert advice on digital governance in education.

#### 5.3 To European Industry

We call on European technology companies and platform operators to engage with this framework as partners. The European market for sovereign digital infrastructure represents a commercial opportunity of extraordinary scale. Companies that align their products and business models with European sovereignty requirements, open standards and data protection principles will find in European public institutions a large, stable and loyal customer base. We call specifically on European industry to: contribute to European AI and cloud infrastructure funds on terms that accept public governance authority; support open-source educational technology development through code contributions and commercial services built on open-source cores; implement European interoperability standards natively; and accept mandatory data portability, open API requirements and meaningful exit clauses as conditions of public sector contracting.

#### 5.4 To the Open-Source AI and Technology Community

We call on the global open-source AI and technology community to continue the tradition of open publication, collaborative development and public benefit orientation that has been the source of the most important advances in the field. We recognise the tension between openness and safety in AI development, and call for a nuanced approach that distinguishes between different levels of openness and different risk profiles, rather than treating "closed" as synonymous with "safe." The safety of AI systems depends primarily on governance, not on secrecy.

## Part V: The Declaration

### 6. The Declaration

> *"We do not inherit the digital future. We build it. And what we build reflects what we value."*

We, the higher education institutions and university alliances that adopt this Manifesto, declare the following:

> **Declaration of Principles**
>
> **We declare** that digital infrastructure — cloud, AI, data platforms, identity systems — is a public good that must be governed in the public interest, financed in part through public investment, and operated under democratic accountability.
>
> **We declare** that artificial intelligence is not the invention of commercial companies. It is the product of decades of publicly funded academic research, and the institutions that produced that research have both the legitimacy and the responsibility to govern its development and deployment.
>
> **We declare** that open science and open-source development are not inferior alternatives to commercial AI. They are a legitimate, valuable and specifically European tradition that deserves equal standing in policy, equal access to funding, and equal respect in public discourse.
>
> **We declare** that student data is a protected trust, not an institutional asset. Students have the right to own their credentials, to understand and challenge AI-generated inferences about their learning, and to benefit from the analytics conducted on their data.
>
> **We declare** that European higher education institutions have a responsibility — not merely an interest — in building European digital sovereignty. Through our procurement, our research, our governance participation and our open contributions, we are active shapers of the European digital infrastructure.
>
> **We declare** that cooperation is not weakness. The greatest achievements of European digital infrastructure — SURF, SWITCH, Digivisio, eduGAIN, EWP — were built cooperatively. We will extend this tradition to cloud, AI and every domain of educational technology.
>
> **We declare** that the time for incremental, institution-by-institution progress is past. The scale of the challenge demands coordinated, ambitious, European-level action. We commit to providing it.

This Manifesto is a living document. It will be reviewed annually by its signatories, updated to reflect developments in technology, policy and institutional practice, and extended as new challenges emerge. Its core commitments — sovereignty, openness, student data rights, cooperative infrastructure, and the active responsibility of higher education to serve the public good — are permanent.

### Adoption and Signature

This Manifesto is open for adoption by any European higher education institution, university alliance, national cooperative body or associated European institution that endorses its principles. Adopting institutions commit to implementing the principles through the Architecture Reference Model and Evaluation Framework that accompany this document, and to reporting on their progress annually.
